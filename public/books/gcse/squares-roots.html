    Squares and square roots

    Squaring and square-rooting as two sides of the same idea: side and area, and a first look at cubes.

    GCSE Maths Book / Squares and square roots

    Before you start: what this stands on

    Maths is built like a pyramid, each topic standing on the ones below. This topic needs:

    • Factors and multiples Number

    The "Last time" questions below are a quick check of that base. If they feel shaky, build the base first.

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Square number
    The answer when a whole number is multiplied by itself.
    Square root
    The positive number that, multiplied by itself, gives the number you started with. Written with the sign √.
    Cube number
    The answer when a whole number is multiplied by itself twice, so three copies of the number are multiplied together. It is the volume of a cube whose side length is that whole number.
    Cube root
    The number that, multiplied by itself twice, gives the number you started with. It is written with the sign ∛.
    Power (index)
    A small raised number that says how many times to multiply a number by itself. The plural of index is indices.
    Irrational number
    A number that cannot be written as a fraction. Its decimal never ends and never repeats.

    Warm-up: what do you already know?

    Have a go before you read anything. The recap below is from Factors and multiples, the layer this stands on.

    A square tile has sides of 7 cm. What is its area, in cm²?

    1. Area of a square = side × side.
    • That is the perimeter (4 × 7). Area is side × side.
    • That is 7 + 7. Area multiplies the side by itself.

    7 × 7 = 49 cm².

    Quick recap of earlier topics (skip if you are happy)

    Last time: how many factors does 36 have?

    1. Factor pairs: 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, and 6 × 6.
    • 6 × 6 gives only one factor, 6, not two.
    • You missed one. 6 is a factor too.

    1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36: nine. (6 is its own partner, so it is counted once.)

    What is the HCF of 12 and 18?

    1. Factors of 12 and 18.
    • 36 is a common multiple, not a factor.

    Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 6. HCF = 6.

    Work out 49 ÷ 7.

    1. 7 × 7 = 49.

    49 ÷ 7 = 7, exactly. So 49 is a multiple of 7.

    1. Square numbers

    To square a number, multiply it by itself. The answer is a Square number, and it has that name because it is the area of a square with that side length. For example, 4² = 4 × 4 = 16.

    1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144 …

    Factors come in pairs, except for square numbers, which have one factor paired with itself (6 × 6 = 36): that is why a square number has an odd number of factors.

    Key idea: learn the squares up to 15² = 225, because they come up everywhere (Pythagoras, area, algebra). The small 2 means “multiply the number by itself”. It does not mean “times 2”: 6² = 36, not 12.

    Worked example

    Work out 1.1².

    1. Ignore the decimal points: 11 × 11 = 121.
    2. Each 1.1 has 1 decimal place, so the answer has 2 decimal places in total (1 + 1): 1.21.

    Sense check: 1.1 is a bit more than 1, so its square is a bit more than 1.

    Check your knowledge: squares

    Work out 9².

    1. 9 × 9.
    • That is 9 × 2. Squaring multiplies 9 by itself.

    9 × 9 = 81.

    Work out 12².

    1. 12 × 12: ten twelves plus two twelves.
    • That is 12 × 2. Squaring multiplies 12 by itself.

    120 + 24 = 144.

    Work out 15².

    1. 15 × 15: 15 × 10 = 150, then 15 × 5 = 75.
    • That is 15 × 2.
    • That is only 15 × 10. Add 15 × 5 as well.

    150 + 75 = 225.

    Work out 0.5².

    1. 5 × 5 = 25, then put the decimal point back so that there are two decimal places.
    • That is 0.5 × 5. Squaring multiplies 0.5 by itself.
    • That is 0.5 × 2. Squaring multiplies 0.5 by itself, not by 2.

    0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. (Half of a half is a quarter.)

    Which of these is a square number?

    • 50
    • 64
    • 80
    1. Look for a whole number that multiplies by itself.

    8 × 8 = 64.

    2. Square roots

    A Square root undoes squaring. The square root of 49 is the positive number that gives 49 when it is multiplied by itself. It is written √49 = 7. Think of it as the side length of a square with area 49.

    Key idea: the root sign works like brackets: √(36 + 64) means work out 36 + 64 = 100 first, then √100 = 10. But √36 + √64 = 6 + 8 = 14. These are different!

    Worked example

    Work out √(36 + 64).

    1. The root sign groups what is under it: 36 + 64 = 100.
    2. √100 = 10.

    Trap: √36 + √64 = 14, which is not the same. In general, the square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots. (The one exception is when a part is 0: √(0 + 9) = √0 + √9 = 3.)

    Check your knowledge: roots

    Work out √81.

    1. Which number times itself is 81?
    • That is 81 ÷ 2. A square root asks “what times itself?”

    9 × 9 = 81, so √81 = 9.

    Work out √144.

    1. 12 × 12 = 144.
    • That is 144 ÷ 2.

    √144 = 12.

    Work out √169.

    1. It is a little more than 12, because 12² = 144.
    • That is 169 ÷ 2.

    13 × 13 = 169.

    Work out √0.25.

    1. Which decimal times itself is 0.25?
    • 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.0025.

    0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.

    Work out √(225 + 64).

    1. Add under the root first.
    • That is √225 + √64 = 15 + 8. The root sign groups what is under it: add first.

    225 + 64 = 289 and √289 = 17.

    3. Roots that are not whole numbers

    Most numbers are not perfect squares. The root of a whole number that is not a square is irrational. There are two ways to say what that means: its decimal never ends and never repeats, or it cannot be written as a fraction of two whole numbers. Both describe the same numbers. (1 over 3 = 0.333… goes on for ever but it repeats, and it is a fraction, so it is not irrational.) You can still estimate a root by finding the two square numbers either side. √50 is between √49 = 7 and √64 = 8, and very close to 7 because 50 is very close to 49.

    Key idea: a calculator gives a decimal such as √50 = 7.0710…, which is only an approximation of the exact value. Estimate first so you can tell whether the calculator answer makes sense. If the answer must be exact, leave it as a root: a Surd such as √50. The next section shows how to simplify one.

    Worked example

    Estimate √20 by finding two decimals, to 1 decimal place, that it lies between.

    1. 16 = 4² and 25 = 5², so √20 is between 4 and 5.
    2. Try decimals between 4 and 5: 4.4² = 19.36, which is a little too small. 4.5² = 20.25, which is a little too big.
    3. So √20 is between 4.4 and 4.5. (The calculator says 4.472…)

    Check your knowledge: estimating roots

    Between which two whole numbers does √50 lie?

    • 6 and 7
    • 7 and 8
    • 8 and 9
    1. Find the squares either side of 50: 49 and 64.

    7² = 49 and 8² = 64. 50 is between them, so √50 is between 7 and 8.

    Use a calculator to find √30 to 1 decimal place.

    1. It should be between 5 and 6, because 25 < 30 < 36.
    • The next digit is 7, which rounds up. √30 = 5.477…
    • That is 30 ÷ 2.

    √30 = 5.477… ≈ 5.5 (1 d.p.).

    Which is closest to √40?

    • 6.3
    • 6.7
    • 7.0
    1. Square each option and see which is nearest 40.

    6.3² = 39.69, very close to 40.

    Use a calculator to find √70 to 1 decimal place.

    1. It is between 8 and 9 because 64 < 70 < 81.
    • 8.366… rounds up to 8.4 (the digit after the 3 is 6).
    • That is 70 ÷ 2.

    √70 = 8.366… ≈ 8.4 (1 d.p.).

    4. Simplifying a surd Higher

    You can split a root over a multiplication (but not over an addition or subtraction, in general): √(a × b) = √a × √b, which works when a and b are both 0 or more. So pull out the biggest square number that divides into the number under the root.

    Worked examples

    1. √50 = √(25 × 2) = √25 × √2 = 5√2.
    2. √18 = √(9 × 2) = 3√2.
    3. √12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3.

    Check: 5√2 is about 5 × 1.414 = 7.07, the same as √50.

    Key idea: use the biggest square factor, so the number left under the root has no square factor. √50 = √(25 × 2) gives 5√2, which is the exact form.

    Check your knowledge: surds Higher

    Write √45 in the form a√b, with b as small as possible. (Type it like 3√5 or 3sqrt5.)

    1. Which square number divides into 45?
    2. Write 45 as that square number times another number.
    • You took the 9 outside without rooting it. √9 = 3.
    • That is where you started. Pull the square factor 9 out of the root.

    √45 = √(9 × 5) = √9 × √5 = 3√5.

    Write √75 in its simplest form. (Type it like 5√3 or 5sqrt3.)

    1. Try the square numbers 4, 9, 25. Which divides into 75?
    • You took the 25 outside without rooting it. √25 = 5.
    • You swapped the numbers. 5 comes from √25 and 3 stays under the root.

    √75 = √(25 × 3) = 5√3.

    Write √72 in its simplest form. (Type it like 6√2 or 6sqrt2.)

    1. Use the biggest square number that divides into 72.
    • That is correct but not fully simplified: √18 can be simplified again. Start with 36, the biggest square factor.
    • You took the 36 outside without rooting it. √36 = 6.

    √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2. (Or √72 = 2√18 = 2 × 3√2 = 6√2 in two steps.)

    5. Cubes and cube roots

    To cube a number, multiply it by itself twice. 43 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. A Cube number is the volume of a cube with that side length. The Cube root undoes cubing: ∛64 = 4.

    Key idea: cubes grow fast: 23 = 8, 33 = 27, 43 = 64, 53 = 125, 103 = 1,000. A negative number cubed stays negative: (−2)3 = −8.

    Check your knowledge: cubes

    Work out 33.

    1. 3 × 3 × 3.
    • That is 3². Cubing multiplies three 3s.
    • That is 3 + 3.

    9 × 3 = 27.

    Work out 43.

    1. 4 × 4 × 4.
    • That is 4 × 3. Cubing multiplies three 4s.

    16 × 4 = 64.

    Work out 103.

    1. 10 × 10 × 10.
    • That is 10 × 3.

    100 × 10 = 1,000.

    Find the cube root of 125. (That is, which number cubed gives 125?)

    1. 5 × 5 = 25, and 25 × 5 = ?
    • 5² = 25, but 125 needs three 5s.

    5 × 5 × 5 = 125.

    Work out (−2)3.

    1. (−2) × (−2) = 4, then × (−2).
    • There are three negative signs, an odd number, so the answer is negative.

    4 × (−2) = −8. An odd number of negatives gives a negative.

    Flip it: area and side length

    So far you have gone one way. Now run it backwards: this is where much of the real understanding comes from.

    Forwards

    A square has side 9 cm. Its area is 9² = 81 cm².

    Side to area: square it.

    Backwards

    A square has area 81 cm². Its side is √81 = 9 cm.

    Area to side: square-root it.

    Key idea: for numbers 0 or more, squaring and square-rooting undo each other, just as multiplying and dividing do. One goes from side to area, the other from area to side.

    There is one more thing to know when you solve an equation like x² = 49: both 7 and −7 square to 49, so there are two solutions, x = 7 and x = −7. The square root sign (√) itself always means the positive root, √49 = 7.

    Check your knowledge: flip it

    A square has an area of 81 cm². What is the length of one side, in cm?

    1. Undo the squaring.
    • That is 81 ÷ 2. Take the square root.

    √81 = 9 cm.

    A square has a perimeter of 20 cm. What is its area, in cm²?

    1. Perimeter is 4 sides. Find one side first.
    • That is the side length. The question asks for the area.
    • That squares the perimeter. Find the side first.

    Side = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 cm. Area = 5² = 25 cm².

    √x = 7. What is x?

    1. Undo the square root by squaring.
    • That is 7 ÷ 2. Undo a square root by squaring.
    • That is 7 × 2.

    7² = 49. Check: √49 = 7.

    x² = 169. Write down the positive solution for x.

    1. Both 13 and −13 square to 169.
    • That is 169 ÷ 2. Take the square root.

    x = 13 or x = −13. The positive solution is 13.

    How many different numbers satisfy x² = 49?

    1. 7 is one of them. What is the other?
    • (−7) × (−7) = 49 too. A negative squared is positive.

    x = 7 and x = −7: two solutions.

    Spot the mistake

    Ian says: “6² = 12.” What went wrong?

    • He doubled it: 6² = 36.
    • Nothing is wrong: 6 × 2 = 12, so he is right
    • It should be 8, because 6 + 2 = 8
    1. What does the small 2 stand for?

    He doubled it. 6² = 6 × 6 = 36.

    Jen says: “√(9 + 16) = √9 + √16 = 7.” What went wrong?

    • Add first: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.
    • Nothing is wrong: a root can be split over an addition, and 3 + 4 = 7
    • The answer should be 25, with no root left to take
    1. Add the numbers under the root first, then take the square root.

    In general, the square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots. Add first: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

    Kit says: “√16 = 8.” What went wrong?

    • He halved it: 4 × 4 = 16.
    • Nothing is wrong: the square root of a number is half of it
    • It should be 256, because 16 × 16 = 256
    1. Which number times itself is 16?

    He halved it. √16 = 4 because 4 × 4 = 16.

    Check your knowledge: mixed review

    Work out 11² + 5².

    1. 121 + 25.
    • That is (11 + 5)². Square each separately.

    11² = 121 and 5² = 25, so 146.

    Work out √(100 − 36).

    1. Work out the subtraction under the root first.
    • That is √100 − √36 = 10 − 6. Subtract under the root first.

    100 − 36 = 64 and √64 = 8.

    Work out √(13² − 5²).

    1. Square each number, then subtract, then root.
    • That is √169 − √25 = 13 − 5. Subtract under the root first.

    169 − 25 = 144 and √144 = 12.

    Which of these is NOT a square number: 36, 48, 100?

    • 36
    • 48
    • 100
    1. 6² = 36 and 7² = 49: where does 48 fit?

    48 is between 36 and 49, so it is not a square.

    Work out 23 + 3².

    1. 8 + 9.
    • 2² + 3² would be 13. The 2 here is cubed.
    • You may have used 2 × 3 = 6 for the cube.

    23 = 8 and 3² = 9: 17.

    Exam style

    No calculator for (a) and (b).

    (a) [2 marks] A square garden has an area of 144 m². Work out the perimeter of the garden, in m.

    1. Find the side first.
    • That is the side. The perimeter is all four sides.
    • That is 144 × 4. Find the side length (square root) first.

    √144 = 12 m, so the perimeter is 4 × 12 = 48 m.

    • M1 for √144 = 12
    • A1 for 4 × 12 = 48

    (b) [2 marks] Work out √(5² − 3²).

    1. 25 − 9 under the root.
    • Subtract the squares first: √25 − √9 is not the same thing.

    25 − 9 = 16 and √16 = 4. (This is a 3, 4, 5 right-angled triangle.)

    • M1 for 25 − 9 = 16
    • A1 for √16 = 4

    (c) [2 marks] Use a calculator to work out √90. Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

    1. Estimate first: between 9 and 10.
    • The digit after the 4 is 8, which rounds up.

    √90 = 9.486… ≈ 9.5 (1 d.p.).

    • M1 for √90 = 9.486… from the calculator
    • A1 for 9.5, rounded to 1 decimal place

    Going further

    What is the smallest whole number you can multiply 12 by to get a square number?

    1. Write 12 as a product of primes.
    2. Think about how many times each prime must appear in a square number.
    • 12 × 12 = 144 is a square, but 3 is smaller.
    • 12 × 2 = 24, not a square.

    12 × 3 = 36 = 6².

    How many square numbers are there between 100 and 200?

    1. Find the first whole number whose square is more than 100.
    2. Keep going until the square is more than 200.
    • That counts 100 itself, which is not between 100 and 200.
    • You missed one. Check every square from 11² to 14².

    121, 144, 169, 196: four square numbers (10² = 100 and 15² = 225 are outside).

    Higher tier: the next question.

    A rational number can be written as a fraction of two whole numbers. Which statement about √2 is correct?

    • It is irrational: no fraction of whole numbers equals it.
    • It is rational, because its decimal goes on for ever without stopping.
    • It is rational, because 1.4 × 1.4 = 1.96 is close enough to 2.
    • It is rational, because a calculator shows it as a decimal number.
    1. What does “rational” mean?
    2. Which kinds of decimal can always be written as a fraction?

    No fraction of two whole numbers equals √2, so it is irrational. The two descriptions go together: a number that is not a fraction of whole numbers has a decimal that never ends and never repeats, and the other way round. So its decimal, 1.41421…, never ends and never repeats. (Ending and repeating decimals can always be written as fractions.)

    Work out 31² − 30².

    1. Square each number first.
    2. Try smaller cases to spot a pattern: 2² − 1², 3² − 2², 4² − 3².
    • That is 31 − 30. Square each number before you subtract.
    • That is 2 × 31. The gap is 2 × 30 + 1: use the smaller number.
    • That is 2 × 30. The gap is 2n + 1, so add the 1.

    961 − 900 = 61. In general, n² and (n + 1)² differ by 2n + 1. Here n = 30, so 2 × 30 + 1 = 61.

    The difference between two consecutive square numbers is always odd. Use the pattern from the previous question to work out 25² − 24² without squaring either number.

    1. What did you find for the gap between n² and (n + 1)²?
    • That is 2 × 25. Use the smaller number: 2 × 24 + 1.
    • That is 2 × 24. The gap is 2n + 1, so add the 1.
    • That is 25 − 24. The gap between squares grows as the numbers grow.

    2 × 24 + 1 = 49. Check: 625 − 576 = 49. ✓

    Higher tier: proof and reasoning.

    Here are four lines from a proof that √2 is irrational. Which line is the contradiction?

    • So p and q are both even, which contradicts “no common factor”.
    • Suppose √2 = p/q, where p and q are whole numbers with no common factor.
    • Then p² = 2q², so p² is even, so p is even (an odd number squared is odd).
    • Write p = 2k. Then 4k² = 2q², so q² = 2k² and q is even.
    1. A contradiction is a result that disagrees with something we assumed.
    2. What did we assume about p and q at the start?

    The proof runs in this order: assume √2 = p/q in lowest terms; p² = 2q² so p² is even, so p is even (if p were odd, p² would be odd too); p = 2k gives q even; so p and q are both even. That disagrees with the assumption that they have no common factor, so no such fraction exists.

    The expression n² + n + 41 gives a prime number for every whole number n from 0 to 39. Find the smallest whole number n that shows it does not always give a prime.

    1. You need a value where the answer has a factor other than 1 and itself.
    2. Try n = 40 and look at 40² + 40 + 41.
    • 41 also works (it gives 41 × 43), but 40 is smaller.
    • 39 gives 1601, which is prime.

    When n = 40: 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 × 41, which is not prime. One counter-example is enough to show a claim is false. (Forty examples in a row were all primes, which is why examples alone are not a proof.)

    Which line is a correct algebraic proof that the sum of three consecutive whole numbers is always a multiple of 3?

    • n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1), a multiple of 3.
    • 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 and 6 + 7 + 8 = 21 are both multiples of 3, so it is always true.
    • The numbers are n + n + n = 3n, a multiple of 3.
    • The sum is 3n + 2, which is a multiple of 3.
    1. Call the first number n. What are the next two?
    2. Add the three and collect like terms.

    n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1). This has a factor 3 whatever whole number n is, so it is a multiple of 3.

    Work out √(10 × 11 × 12 × 13 + 1). (The number under the root is a perfect square, so the answer is a whole number. Look for a pattern rather than using a calculator.)

    1. Four consecutive numbers multiplied, plus 1. What do you get with the smallest case, 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 + 1?
    2. Try 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 + 1, then 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 + 1, then 3 × 4 × 5 × 6 + 1. Take the square root of each and look at 5, 11, 19.
    3. If the first number is n, the root is n² + 3n + 1 (check it gives 5, 11, 19). Now put n = 10.
    • That is the number under the root. You still need its square root.
    • 130 is 10² + 3 × 10, one short. The pattern adds 1: n² + 3n + 1.

    The pattern: √(1 × 2 × 3 × 4 + 1) = √25 = 5, √(2 × 3 × 4 × 5 + 1) = √121 = 11, √(3 × 4 × 5 × 6 + 1) = √361 = 19, and 5, 11, 19 are n² + 3n + 1. Why: let k = n(n + 3) = n² + 3n. Then (n + 1)(n + 2) = k + 2, so the product plus 1 is k(k + 2) + 1 = (k + 1)². For n = 10, k = 130, so the root is 131. Check: 131² = 17,161 = 17,160 + 1.

    [3 marks] Non-calculator Grade 8 a and b are any two numbers. Show that a² + b² is never less than 2ab. (Try a = 3 and b = 5 first.)

    1. Which expression, made from a and b, can never be negative whatever a and b are?
    2. A number squared is never negative. Try (a − b)² and see what you get when you expand it.
    3. Write (a − b)² ≥ 0, expand the bracket, then move one term to the other side.

    A square is never negative, so (a − b)² ≥ 0 for all a and b.

    Expanding: a² − 2ab + b² ≥ 0.

    Add 2ab to both sides: a² + b² ≥ 2ab. So a² + b² is never less than 2ab. (Check with a = 3 and b = 5: 9 + 25 = 34 and 2 × 15 = 30, and 34 ≥ 30.)

    • B1 for (a − b)² ≥ 0, because a square cannot be negative
    • M1 for expanding to a² − 2ab + b² ≥ 0
    • C1 for adding 2ab to both sides and stating a² + b² ≥ 2ab

    A student tries to show that (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1. Line 1: (n + 1)² − n² = n² + 1 − n². Line 2: = 1. Line 3: So the difference is 1, not 2n + 1. Where is the flaw?

    • Line 1: (n + 1)² is not n² + 1, because the middle term 2n is missing.
    • Line 2: the n² terms should add together, not cancel.
    • Line 3: a proof about consecutive squares cannot be done with letters, so a table of examples is needed instead.
    1. Check line 1 with n = 3: is (3 + 1)² the same as 3² + 1?
    2. Expand (n + 1)² yourself as (n + 1)(n + 1).

    Line 1: (n + 1)² is not n² + 1, because the middle term 2n is missing. (n + 1)² = n² + 2n + 1, so the difference is n² + 2n + 1 − n² = 2n + 1, as it should be. Check with n = 3: 16 − 9 = 7 = 2 × 3 + 1.

    Why it works

    • A square number is an area. Squaring takes a side to an area; the square root takes an area back to a side.
    • The root sign groups. √(36 + 64) needs the sum first. In general, the square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots.
    • Estimating a root by the squares either side tells you whether a calculator answer makes sense.

    Common traps

    • Doubling instead of squaring (6² is not 12).
    • Splitting a root over an addition or subtraction.
    • Forgetting that x² = 49 has two solutions.
    • Halving instead of square-rooting.
    • Confusing square (two lots) and cube (three lots).
