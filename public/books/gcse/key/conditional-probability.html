    Answers for Conditional probability
    No.QuestionAnswerMarks
    1Work out 1 − 2/5. Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/8.3/5
    Working: 1 = 5/5, so 5/5 − 2/5 = 3/5.
    2Work out 3/8 × 2/7, in its simplest form. Type it like 3/10.3/28
    Working: 3/8 × 2/7 = 6/56. Divide the top and bottom by 2: 3/28.
    3The probability that a train is late is 0.65. What is the probability that it is not late?0.35
    Working: 1 − 0.65 = 0.35.
    4A fair coin is tossed and a fair dice is rolled. What is the probability of heads and a 6? Type it like 1/20.1/12
    Working: 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12.
    5A student is chosen at random from the class. What is the probability that the student is a girl? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.1/2
    Working: 30 of the 60 students are girls: 30/60 = 1/2.
    6A girl is chosen at random. What is the probability that she walks to school? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.3/5
    Working: Only look at the 30 girls. 18 of them walk, so P(walk | girl) = 18/30 = 3/5.
    7A student who walks is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is a girl? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.9/16
    Working: Only look at the 32 walkers. 18 of them are girls, so P(girl | walk) = 18/32 = 9/16.
    8A boy is chosen at random. What is the probability that he takes the bus? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.8/15
    Working: Only look at the 30 boys. 16 of them take the bus, so P(bus | boy) = 16/30 = 8/15.
    9A student who takes the bus is chosen at random. Which fraction is P(boy | bus)?16/28
    Working: 28 students take the bus and 16 of them are boys. P(boy | bus) = 16/28, which is 4/7 in its simplest form.
    10A heating firm made 50 call-outs. 30 were for gas boilers and 6 of those needed a new part. 20 were for oil boilers and 8 of those needed a new part. One oil-boiler call-out is chosen at random. What is the probability it needed a new part? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5. [Figure: Tree for two counters, all branches filled in]2/5
    Working: Only the 20 oil jobs matter. 8 of them needed a new part, so the probability is 8/20 = 2/5.
    11A bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two counters are taken without replacement. What is the missing probability on the tree, the chance the second counter is red when the first was red? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/8. [Figure: Tree for two counters from a bag of eight]4/7
    Working: One red counter has gone, so 7 counters are left and 4 are red. P(second red | first red) = 4/7.
    12Use the tree. What is the probability that both counters are red? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: Tree for two counters, all branches filled in]5/14
    Working: P(RR) = 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 = 5/14.
    13A box holds 10 fuses and 3 of them are faulty. Two are taken at random without replacement. What is the probability that both are faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/15
    Working: P(first faulty) = 3/10. Then 9 fuses are left and 2 are faulty, so P(second faulty | first faulty) = 2/9. P(both) = 3/10 × 2/9 = 6/90 = 1/15.
    14The same box of 10 fuses has 3 faulty ones. Two are taken without replacement. What is the probability that exactly one is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.7/15
    1. M1 for 3/10 × 7/9 or 7/10 × 3/9
    2. A1 for 7/15, from 42/90 simplified
    Working: Faulty then good: 3/10 × 7/9 = 21/90. Good then faulty: 7/10 × 3/9 = 21/90. Add: 42/90 = 7/15.
    		2
    15P(A) = 0.6 and P(B | A) = 0.5. Use P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A) to find P(A and B).0.3
    Working: P(A and B) = 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3.
    16A bag has 4 red and 3 blue counters. One blue counter is taken out and not put back. What is the probability that the next counter is also blue? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.1/3
    Working: After one blue counter is taken, 6 counters are left and 2 are blue. P = 2/6 = 1/3.
    17On the tree, the second branches have 7 on the bottom but the first branches have 8. Why? [Figure: Venn diagram for football and tennis]One counter is out, so only 7 are left
    Working: The first counter is not replaced, so only 7 are left for the second pick. That is why the second branches are conditional probabilities: they depend on the first pick.
    18The Venn diagram shows 30 students and the sports they play. A student who plays football is chosen. What is the probability that they also play tennis? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for football and tennis]5/18
    Working: The football circle holds 13 + 5 = 18 students. 5 of them also play tennis, so P(tennis | football) = 5/18.
    19A student who plays tennis is chosen. What is the probability that they also play football? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for football and tennis]5/12
    Working: The tennis circle holds 7 + 5 = 12 students and 5 of them also play football. P(football | tennis) = 5/12.
    20A student is chosen at random from all 30. What is the probability that they play football and tennis? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for football and tennis]1/6
    Working: The overlap holds 5 of the 30 students: 5/30 = 1/6. Check: P(football) × P(tennis | football) = 18/30 × 5/18 = 5/30. ✓
    21A student who does not play tennis is chosen. What is the probability that they play football? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for football and tennis]13/18
    Working: Outside the tennis circle: 13 + 5 = 18 students. 13 of them play football (and not tennis), so the probability is 13/18.
    22The Venn diagram shows the 40 students in a school who are in the maths club, the science club, both or neither. A maths club member is chosen. What is the probability they are also in the science club? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for maths and science clubs]1/2
    Working: The maths circle holds 12 + 12 = 24 students. 12 of them are also in science, so P(science | maths) = 12/24 = 1/2.
    23A science club member is chosen from the same Venn diagram. What is the probability they are also in the maths club? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: Venn diagram for maths and science clubs]3/5
    Working: The science circle holds 12 + 8 = 20 students. 12 of them are also in maths, so P(maths | science) = 12/20 = 3/5. This is not the same as P(science | maths).
    24Which formula gives P(B | A), the chance of B given A?P(A and B) ÷ P(A)
    Working: P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A): the part of A that is also B, divided by all of A.
    25A part made by Machine X is chosen. What is the probability it is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/20
    Working: 10/200 = 1/20.
    26A part made by Machine Y is chosen. What is the probability it is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/20
    Working: 15/300 = 1/20.
    27Are “faulty” and “made by Machine X” independent for these parts?Yes: each machine has a fault rate of 1 in 20
    Working: P(faulty | X) = 10/200 = 1/20, P(faulty | Y) = 15/300 = 1/20 and P(faulty) = 25/500 = 1/20. All equal, so knowing the machine tells you nothing about faults: independent.
    28Are “faulty” and “made on the day shift” independent for these parts?No: P(faulty | day) = 0.12, but P(faulty) = 0.08
    Working: P(faulty | day) = 12/100 = 0.12. P(faulty) = 16/200 = 0.08. These are different, so knowing it is the day shift changes the chance of a fault: not independent.
    29A and B are independent events with P(A) = 0.5 and P(B) = 0.4. What is P(A and B)?0.2
    Working: P(A and B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2.
    30A fair coin is tossed and a fair dice is rolled. Given that the dice shows a 6, what is the probability that the coin shows heads? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10.1/2
    Working: The coin and the dice are independent, so P(heads | six) = P(heads) = 1/2.
    31Counters are picked one after the other from a bag. Which statement is correct? [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]Independent with replacement, dependent without
    Working: If the counter is put back, the second pick has the same chances as the first: independent. If it is not put back, the bag has changed: dependent.
    32A test flags faulty parts. It was tried on 1000 parts: 20 faulty and 980 good. Use the tree to find the total number of parts that were flagged. [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]67
    Working: 18 faulty parts and 49 good parts were flagged: 18 + 49 = 67.
    33A faulty part is tested. What is the probability that it is flagged? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10. [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]9/10
    Working: Of the 20 faulty parts, 18 are flagged: P(flagged | faulty) = 18/20 = 9/10.
    34A part is flagged. What is the probability that it really is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10. [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]18/67
    Working: There are 18 + 49 = 67 flagged parts and only 18 of them are faulty. P(faulty | flagged) = 18/67, about 0.27.
    35P(flagged | faulty) is 9/10 but P(faulty | flagged) is only about 0.27. Why is the second one so much lower? [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]Most parts are good, so false alarms add up
    Working: 980 of the 1000 parts are good, so even a 5% false-alarm rate flags 49 of them. Only 18 of the 67 flagged parts are faulty.
    36A flagged part is checked by hand. What is the probability that it turns out to be a good part? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10. [Figure: The same test on 1000 parts]49/67
    Working: There are 67 flagged parts and 49 of them are good, so the probability is 49/67. Check: 18/67 + 49/67 = 1. ✓
    37P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6 and P(A and B) = 0.12. Find P(B | A).0.4
    Working: P(B | A) = 0.12 ÷ 0.3 = 0.4.
    38P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6 and P(A and B) = 0.12. Find P(A | B).0.2
    Working: P(A | B) = 0.12 ÷ 0.6 = 0.2. This is different from P(B | A) = 0.4.
    39P(A) = 0.5 and P(B | A) = 0.4. Find P(A and B).0.2
    Working: P(A and B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2.
    40P(B | A) = 0.4 and P(A and B) = 0.2. Find P(A).0.5
    Working: 0.2 = P(A) × 0.4, so P(A) = 0.2 ÷ 0.4 = 0.5.
    41In a class of girls and boys, P(walk | girl) = 3/5 and there are 30 girls. How many girls walk to school?18
    Working: P(walk | girl) is the fraction of the girls who walk, so 3/5 × 30 = 18 girls.
    42A bag has 6 counters, some red and the rest blue. Two are taken without replacement and P(both red) = 1/5. How many red counters are in the bag?3
    Working: Try r = 3: 3/6 × 2/5 = 6/30 = 1/5. ✓ So there are 3 red counters. (r = 2 gives 1/15 and r = 4 gives 12/30 = 2/5.)
    43Sam says: in the class table, P(boy | bus) = 16/60, because there are 60 students. What went wrong?The bottom should be the 28 students on the bus, not all 60
    Working: You are told the student rides the bus, so look only at the 28 bus riders. 16 of them are boys: P(boy | bus) = 16/28 = 4/7.
    44Mia says: a bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement, so P(both red) = 5/8 × 5/8. What went wrong?After one red is out, the second pick is 4/7, not 5/8
    Working: Without replacement, the second pick is from 7 counters with 4 red. P(both red) = 5/8 × 4/7 = 5/14.
    45Kit says: the test flags 90% of faulty parts, so if a part is flagged there is a 90% chance it is faulty. What went wrong?The two probabilities have different bottoms, so they are usually not equal
    Working: 90% is P(flagged | faulty): the share of faulty parts that are flagged. P(faulty | flagged) is the share of flagged parts that are faulty. It depends on how many good parts are also flagged, so it can be much lower.
    46A bag has 6 red and 2 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. What is the probability that the first is red and the second is blue? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.3/14
    Working: 6/8 × 2/7 = 12/56 = 3/14.
    4780 people were asked. 50 own a car, and 30 of the car owners are over 40. One car owner is chosen at random. What is the probability they are over 40? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.3/5
    Working: 30/50 = 3/5.
    4812 plumbers do gas work, 9 do oil work and 4 do both. A plumber who does gas work is chosen at random. What is the probability that they also do oil work? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/3
    Working: 4 of the 12 gas plumbers also do oil. P(oil | gas) = 4/12 = 1/3.
    49P(A) = 0.5 and P(A and B) = 0.15. Find P(B | A).0.3
    Working: P(B | A) = 0.15 ÷ 0.5 = 0.3.
    50A fair dice is rolled and a fair coin is tossed. What is the probability of a 6 and tails? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10.1/12
    Working: 1/6 × 1/2 = 1/12.
    51An electrician’s tray has 12 fuses: 4 are 13 amp and 8 are 5 amp. She takes two without looking and without putting the first back. What is the probability that both are 13 amp? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/11
    Working: 4/12 × 3/11 = 12/132 = 1/11.
    52A plumber buys 100 valves: 60 from supplier A and 40 from supplier B. 3 of A’s valves leak and 4 of B’s valves leak. A valve from supplier B is picked at random. What is the probability that it leaks? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/10
    Working: 4/40 = 1/10: divide the top and bottom by 4.
    53A carpenter has 20 planks and 6 of them are warped. She takes two at random without replacement. What is the probability that neither is warped? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10.91/190
    Working: 14/20 × 13/19 = 182/380 = 91/190.
    54A tester checks 500 sockets. 25 are faulty. The tester flags 24 of the faulty sockets and 38 of the 475 good ones. A socket is flagged. What is the probability that it is really faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.12/31
    1. M1 for 24 + 38 = 62 flagged sockets
    2. A1 for 12/31, from 24/62 simplified
    Working: Flagged: 24 + 38 = 62. Of these, 24 are faulty. P(faulty | flagged) = 24/62 = 12/31, which is about 0.39.
    		2
    55A pack of 15 roof tiles has 3 cracked ones. Two tiles are taken without replacement and the first is cracked. What is the probability that the second is cracked as well? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.1/7
    Working: 14 tiles are left and 2 are cracked, so P = 2/14 = 1/7.
    5660% of deliveries to a site arrive before 9 am. Of those, 5% are damaged. Of the deliveries that arrive later, 10% are damaged. Each damaged delivery costs £85 to replace. In 200 deliveries, what is the expected cost of replacements, in pounds? Type it like 1250.1190
    1. M1 for 0.6 × 0.05 = 0.03 and 0.4 × 0.1 = 0.04
    2. M1 for 0.03 + 0.04 = 0.07
    3. A1 for 200 × 0.07 × 85 = 1190
    Working: P(early and damaged) = 0.6 × 0.05 = 0.03. P(late and damaged) = 0.4 × 0.1 = 0.04. P(damaged) = 0.07. Expected damaged = 0.07 × 200 = 14. Cost = 14 × 85 = £1190.
    		3
    57In a group of 50 people, 28 play chess, 20 play draughts and 8 play both. A person who plays chess is chosen at random. Work out the probability that they also play draughts. Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.2/7
    1. M1 for 28 as the number who play chess
    2. M1 for 8/28
    3. A1 for 2/7
    Working: Chess is given, so look only at the 28 chess players. 8 of them also play draughts. P(draughts | chess) = 8/28 = 2/7.
    		3
    58Higher [4 marks] A box has 4 red cards and 3 green cards. Two cards are taken without replacement. Work out the probability that at least one is green. Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.5/7
    1. M1 for 4/7 × 3/6
    2. M1 for 12/42 or 2/7
    3. M1 for 1 − 2/7
    4. A1 for 5/7
    Working: P(both red) = 4/7 × 3/6 = 12/42 = 2/7. P(at least one green) = 1 − 2/7 = 5/7.
    		4
    59Higher [3 marks] Machine P made 60 parts and 9 were faulty. Machine Q made 40 parts and 2 were faulty. Show that “faulty” and “made by machine P” are not independent events.Written answer: see the mark scheme
    1. M1 for P(faulty | made by P) = 9/60 = 0.15
    2. M1 for P(faulty) = 11/100 = 0.11
    3. C1 for saying they are different, so the events are not independent
    Working: P(faulty | made by P) = 9/60 = 0.15. In all, 11 of the 100 parts were faulty, so P(faulty) = 11/100 = 0.11. These are not equal, so knowing the part came from machine P changes the chance of a fault: the events are not independent.
    		3
    60Higher [4 marks] P(A) = 0.4. P(B | A) = 0.5. P(B | not A) = 0.2. Work out P(B).0.32
    1. M1 for 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2
    2. M1 for 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.12
    3. M1 for 0.2 + 0.12
    4. A1 for 0.32
    Working: P(A and B) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2. P(not A and B) = 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.12. P(B) = 0.2 + 0.12 = 0.32.
    		4
    61P(A and B) = 0.18 and P(B) = 0.45. Work out P(A | B).0.4
    1. M1 for P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)
    2. M1 for 0.18 ÷ 0.45
    3. A1 for 0.4
    Working: P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B) = 0.18 ÷ 0.45 = 0.4.
    		3
    62Higher [3 marks] A test is tried on 1000 parts. 40 are faulty and 960 are good. The test flags 36 of the faulty parts and 48 of the good parts. A part is flagged. What is the probability that it is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.3/7
    1. M1 for 36 + 48 = 84 flagged parts
    2. M1 for 36/84
    3. A1 for 3/7
    Working: Flagged: 36 + 48 = 84. 36 of them are faulty. P(faulty | flagged) = 36/84 = 3/7.
    		3
    63A bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Without drawing a tree, you might guess that the second counter is less likely to be red than the first. Work out the probability that the second counter is red. Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.5/8
    Working: Red then red: 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56. Blue then red: 3/8 × 5/7 = 15/56. Add: 35/56 = 5/8. It is the same as the chance the first is red: if you do not look at the first counter, the second is just as likely to be red.
    64Three cards are in a hat. One has red on both sides, one has white on both sides and one has red on one side and white on the other. A card is drawn and put down with one side showing. The side you see is red. What is the probability that the other side is red as well? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/10.2/3
    Working: There are 3 red faces: 2 on the red-red card and 1 on the red-white card. Seeing red means one of these 3 faces is showing, each equally likely. For 2 of them the other side is red, so the probability is 2/3.
    65Higher [3 marks] Events A and B are such that P(B | A) = P(B). Show that P(A | B) = P(A). (This shows that if B does not depend on A, then A does not depend on B.) Assume P(B) is not 0.Written answer: see the mark scheme
    1. M1 for P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)
    2. M1 for using P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A) = P(A) × P(B)
    3. C1 for cancelling P(B) to get P(A | B) = P(A)
    Working: P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B). Also P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A) = P(A) × P(B), because P(B | A) = P(B). So P(A | B) = P(A) × P(B) ÷ P(B) = P(A), as required.
    		3
