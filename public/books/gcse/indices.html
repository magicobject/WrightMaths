    Powers and indices

    A power is a short way to write repeated multiplication. Learn what the small raised number means, the three laws for combining powers, and what zero and negative indices do. Then write very big and very small numbers in standard form, and run it all backwards.

    Where you will use this: the size of an atom or a planet, how much room is left on a phone, and the k, M and m on electrical kit (kW, mA, mm).

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Power (index)
    A small raised number that says how many times to multiply a number by itself. The plural of index is indices.
    Base (of a power)
    The number that is multiplied by itself in a power. In 5 to the power 3, the base is 5 and the index is 3.
    Laws of indices
    Rules for powers of the same base: add the powers when multiplying, subtract when dividing, multiply when raising a power to a power. A negative power means "one over"; a fractional power means a root.
    Standard form
    A way to write very big or very small numbers as a number from 1 up to (but not including) 10, times a power of 10.

    Units and typing

    kilo (k) = 103 = 1000, mega (M) = 106 = 1 000 000, milli (m) = 10−3 = 0.001.

    So 1 kW = 1000 W, 1 MW = 1 000 000 W, 1 mA = 0.001 A and 1 mm = 0.001 m.

    Type a power with ^, like 2^5. Type standard form like 3.2x10^5, and a negative power like 4x10^-3. The answer box ignores spaces.

    1. What a power is

    A Power (index) tells you how many times to multiply a number by itself. The big number is the Base (of a power). The small raised number is the power, also called the index. (More than one index are called indices.)

    53 = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125

    Here 5 is the base and 3 is the index. It does not mean 5 × 3. You multiply three 5s together.

    You already know two special cases. Raising to the power 2 is squaring, and the answer is a Square number: 62 = 6 × 6 = 36. Raising to the power 3 is cubing, and the answer is a Cube number: 43 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

    Powers worth knowing

    • Powers with base 2: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024. Each one is double the one before.
    • Powers with base 3: 3, 9, 27, 81, 243. Each one is three times the one before.
    • Powers with base 10: 101 = 10, 102 = 100, 103 = 1000, 104 = 10 000. The index is the number of zeros.

    Key idea: a power is repeated multiplication. Count how many times the base is written, and that is the index. 25 is five 2s: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32.

    Negative numbers raised to powers

    Multiply carefully, one step at a time. A negative times a negative is positive, so the answer flips sign each time you multiply by another negative.

    • An even index gives a positive answer: (−3)2 = (−3) × (−3) = 9.
    • An odd index gives a negative answer: (−2)3 = (−2) × (−2) × (−2) = −8.

    Brackets matter. (−3)2 = 9, but −32 (with no brackets) means the minus of 32, which is −9. On a calculator, put a negative number in brackets before you square it.

    2. Multiplying and dividing powers

    Write the powers out in full and the law appears by itself.

    23 × 24 = (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2 × 2 × 2) = 27

    There are three 2s, then four 2s, so seven 2s altogether. You added the indices: 3 + 4 = 7.

    am × an = am+n

    Dividing cancels. In 26 ÷ 22 you cross out two of the six 2s on top against the two 2s underneath, so four 2s are left.

    am ÷ an = am−n

    Key idea: when you multiply powers with the SAME base, add the indices. When you divide, subtract them. The base stays the same.

    • It works with letters too: x3 × x5 = x8, and y7 ÷ y2 = y5.
    • A letter on its own is a power of 1: x = x1. So x × x4 = x5.
    • With numbers in front, multiply those separately: 3x2 × 4x5 = 12x7.

    The laws only work when the bases are the same. 23 × 32 cannot be made into a single power. And the base never changes: 23 × 24 is 27, not 47.

    Worked example: multiply

    Write 54 × 53 as a single power.

    1. The bases are both 5, so add the indices: 4 + 3 = 7.
    2. 54 × 53 = 57.

    Worked example: divide

    Simplify x9 ÷ x4.

    1. The bases are both x, so subtract the indices: 9 − 4 = 5.
    2. x9 ÷ x4 = x5.

    (am)n = am×n

    4. Zero and negative indices

    Look at a pattern. Each line is the one above divided by 2.

    • 23 = 8
    • 22 = 4
    • 21 = 2
    • 20 = 1
    • 2−1 = 1 over 2
    • 2−2 = 1 over 4

    The pattern keeps going, and it gives two facts.

    a0 = 1 (for any a that is not 0)    a−n = 1 over an

    Why is a0 = 1? Because a3 ÷ a3 = 1 (anything divided by itself), and the law says a3 ÷ a3 = a3−3 = a0.

    Key idea: a negative index does NOT make the answer negative. It makes it a Reciprocal: 2−3 = 1 over 23 = 1 over 8. Turn the power over (one over), and the index becomes positive.

    • 5−1 = 1 over 5 = 0.2.
    • 10−2 = 1 over 102 = 1 over 100 = 0.01. 10−3 = 0.001.
    • The laws still work: 3−1 × 34 = 33 = 27.

    Worked example: a negative index

    Work out 5−2 as a fraction and a decimal.

    1. A negative index means one over the positive power: 5−2 = 1 over 52.
    2. 52 = 25, so 5−2 = 1 over 25.
    3. As a decimal, 1 ÷ 25 = 0.04.

    5. Fractional indices Higher

    A fraction in the index means a root. This follows from the laws: a1/2 × a1/2 = a1/2+1/2 = a1, so a1/2 is the number that multiplies by itself to make a. That is the square root.

    a1/2 = √a    a1/3 = cube root of a    am/n = (n-th root of a)m

    Key idea: the bottom of the fraction is the root, the top is the power. Take the root FIRST (it keeps the numbers small), then apply the power. 82/3: root 3 first, then power 2.

    Worked example

    2. The top number 2 is the power: 22 = 4.
    3. So 82/3 = 4.

    6. Standard form

    Scientists write very big and very small numbers in a short way. The distance from the Earth to the Sun is about 150 000 000 km. That is hard to read and easy to get wrong. In Standard form it is 1.5 × 108 km.

    A × 10n, where A is at least 1 and less than 10, and n is a whole number

    • Big numbers have a positive n: 4 500 000 = 4.5 × 106.
    • Small numbers (between 0 and 1) have a negative n: 0.00072 = 7.2 × 10−4.

    To write a number in standard form: put the decimal point after the first non-zero digit, count how many places the point moved, and use that as the index. Moved left (big number): n is positive. Moved right (small number): n is negative.

    To change it back: move the point the other way. 3.2 × 105 = 320 000 (5 places right). 6.1 × 10−3 = 0.0061 (3 places left).

    Key idea: A must be at least 1 and less than 10. 45 × 103 is NOT standard form (45 is too big), and 0.45 × 104 is NOT either (0.45 is too small).

    Worked example: a big number

    Write 530 000 in standard form.

    1. The first non-zero digit is 5, so the point goes after it: 5.3.
    2. The point moves 5 places to the left, so n = 5.
    3. 530 000 = 5.3 × 105.

    Worked example: a small number

    1. The first non-zero digit is 4, so the point goes after it: 4.2.
    2. The point moves 4 places to the right, so n = −4.
    3. 0.00042 = 4.2 × 10−4.

    8. Powers of ten in the real world

    A Metric prefix is a power of ten in disguise. Use it to change units.

    • You divided 2.5 by 5 but did not use the powers. Subtract the indices: 2 − 1.
    • That is 250 ÷ 5. The tap uses 5 × 101 = 50 litres a day, not 5.

    (2.5 × 102) ÷ (5 × 101) = 250 ÷ 50 = 5 days.

    2. Use the laws: add the indices for the multiplication, subtract for the division.

    [3 marks] Light from the Sun takes about 5 × 102 seconds to reach the Earth. Light travels at about 3 × 108 metres per second. Work out the distance from the Sun to the Earth, in metres, in standard form. Type it like 3.2x10^5.

    2. Divide the first parts and subtract the indices, then write it out in full.

    Why it works

    • A power is repeated multiplication. That is why the laws are really just counting: 23 × 24 has three 2s and four 2s, which is seven 2s.
    • Zero and negative indices keep the pattern going. Each step down divides by the base, so 21 = 2, then 20 = 1, then 2−1 = 1 over 2.
    • Standard form is a power of ten with a tidy first part. Moving the decimal point one place is the same as multiplying or dividing by 10, so the index tells you how many places.
    • Prefixes are powers of ten. Kilo, mega and milli are just a short way to write 103, 106 and 10−3.

    Common traps

    • Reading 53 as 5 × 3. It is 5 × 5 × 5.
    • Multiplying the bases when you multiply powers (23 × 24 is 27, not 47).
    • Adding indices when you should multiply them in a power of a power, or the other way round.
    • Thinking a negative index makes a negative number. It makes a reciprocal.
    • Forgetting that the outer index applies to the number in front: (3a2)2 = 9a4, not 3a4.
    • Leaving the first part of a standard form number outside the range 1 to 10, or counting the jumps of the point wrongly.

    Going further

    Find x if 2x × 43 = 211. (Hint: write 4 as a power of 2 first.)

    1. The bases are different, 2 and 4. Can you write 4 using a base of 2?
    2. 4 = 22, so 43 = (22)3.
    3. Write the whole left-hand side as one power of 2, then compare the indices.
    • That is 11 − 3. But 43 is not 23: 4 is 22.
    • That is the index of the 4 as a power of 2. You need to find x in the whole equation.

    4 = 22, so 43 = (22)3 = 26. Then 2x × 26 = 2x+6 = 211. So x + 6 = 11 and x = 5.

    Which is bigger, 2100 or 350? You are not allowed to use a calculator on the whole numbers.

    • 350, because 3 is bigger than 2
    • 2100
    • They are equal, because 100 = 2 × 50
    1. Try to make both numbers into something raised to the power 50.
    2. 2100 = (22)50.

    2100 = (22)50 = 450. Now compare 450 with 350: the indices match, and 4 is bigger than 3, so 450 is bigger. So 2100 is bigger.

    [3 marks] Show that 2n+3 − 2n = 7 × 2n, where n is a whole number.

    1. Use the multiplication law backwards: 2n+3 = 2n × 23.
    2. Both terms now have 2n. Treat 2n like a letter.
    3. Take 2n out as a common factor.

    2n+3 = 2n × 23 = 8 × 2n.

    8 × 2n − 2n = (8 − 1) × 2n = 7 × 2n, as required.

    • M1 for 2n+3 = 2n × 23 = 8 × 2n
    • M1 for 8 × 2n − 2n = (8 − 1) × 2n
    • C1 for 7 × 2n, stated as required

    A sheet of paper is 0.1 mm thick. Pretend you could fold it in half 10 times. Each fold doubles the thickness. How thick is the pile, in mm?

    1. Each fold doubles the thickness. After 1 fold the thickness is 0.1 × 2.
    2. After 10 folds, the thickness has been multiplied by 2 ten times.
    3. Work out 210, then multiply by 0.1.
    • That is 10 × 0.1. You have to multiply by 2 ten times, which is 210.
    • That is 210, the number of layers. The thickness is 0.1 mm times that.

    After 10 folds the thickness is 0.1 × 210 = 0.1 × 1024 = 102.4 mm. That is about 10 cm, from a sheet about as thin as a hair!
