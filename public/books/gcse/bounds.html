    Bounds and error intervals

    Every measured or rounded number hides a small range of possible true values. Learn to write the limits of a rounded number, and to find the biggest and smallest answers a calculation could give. Then run it backwards: from the limits, back to the number that was rounded.

    Where you will use this: the tolerance on a cut length, the size of a cable or pipe, and a speed or a cost worked out from measurements that were rounded.

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Lower bound
    The smallest value a rounded or measured number could really be. For a rounded number it is the number minus half the rounding unit.
    Upper bound
    The limit at the top of the possible values of a rounded or measured number. For a rounded number it is the number plus half the rounding unit.
    Error interval
    All the values a rounded number could really be, written as an inequality: lower bound ≤ x < upper bound.
    Truncation
    Cutting off the digits after a chosen place without rounding.

    Typing and rounding

    Bounds are numbers, so type them in full, like 2.35 or 4250. Do not round a bound unless the question says so.

    Type an error interval with < and <=, using the letter in the question, like 2.35<=x<2.45. Type <= for ≤. The answer box also accepts the ≤ sign if you paste it. Spaces do not matter.

    Keep the full calculator value in a calculation, and round only at the end.

    1. The limits of a rounded number

    A length written as 7 cm to the nearest centimetre is not exactly 7 cm. It could be 6.6 cm, or 7.3 cm, or 7.49 cm. Anything from 6.5 cm up to (but not including) 7.5 cm rounds to 7 cm. These limits have names.

    • The Lower bound is the smallest value that rounds to the number. For 7 it is 6.5.
    • The Upper bound is the limit at the upper end. For 7 it is 7.5. Values just under 7.5 round to 7, but 7.5 itself would round up to 8.

    Here is the rule. Find the size of the rounding unit, halve it, then go that far down and up from the rounded number.

    Half the rounding unit
    Rounded toRounding unitHalf the unitExample
    nearest 1010540 is from 35 up to 45
    nearest whole number10.57 is from 6.5 up to 7.5
    1 decimal place0.10.055.3 is from 5.25 up to 5.35
    2 decimal places0.010.0052.46 is from 2.455 up to 2.465
    2 significant figuresthe place of the last digithalf of that4300 is from 4250 up to 4350

    For significant figures, look at the place of the last digit. In 4300 to 2 s.f. the 3 is in the hundreds place, so the unit is 100 and half the unit is 50. In 530 to 2 s.f. the 3 is in the tens place, so the unit is 10 and half the unit is 5.

    lower bound = number − half the unit    upper bound = number + half the unit

    Every value from 6.5 (filled: included) up to 7.5 (open: not included) rounds to 7.

    Key idea: half the rounding unit, below and above. The most common slip is to use the whole unit, or half of the wrong unit.

    Worked example: decimal places

    A plank is 2.4 m long, to 1 decimal place. Find its lower and upper bounds.

    1. The rounding unit is 0.1, so half the unit is 0.05.
    2. Lower bound: 2.4 − 0.05 = 2.35 m.
    3. Upper bound: 2.4 + 0.05 = 2.45 m.

    Worked example: significant figures

    A mass is 530 g to 2 significant figures. Find its bounds.

    1. The last significant figure is the 3, in the tens place. The unit is 10, so half the unit is 5.
    2. Lower bound: 530 − 5 = 525 g.
    3. Upper bound: 530 + 5 = 535 g.

    A mass is 62 kg, to the nearest kg. What is its lower bound, in kg?

    1. The rounding unit is 1 kg. What is half of it?
    2. Subtract half the unit from 62.
    • That takes away a whole unit. Take away half a unit, which is 0.5.
    • That is the upper bound. The lower bound is below 62.

    Half of 1 kg is 0.5 kg. The lower bound is 62 − 0.5 = 61.5 kg.

    A crowd is 3400 to the nearest 100. What is its upper bound?

    1. The rounding unit is 100. What is half of it?
    2. Add half the unit to 3400.
    • That adds the whole 100. Add half of it, which is 50.
    • That uses half of 10. The rounding unit is 100, so half of it is 50.

    Half of 100 is 50. The upper bound is 3400 + 50 = 3450.

    • That uses half of 0.01. The rounding was to 1 decimal place, so half the unit is 0.05.

    Half of 0.1 is 0.05. The lower bound is 1.8 − 0.05 = 1.75 m.

    A reading is 12.45 volts, to 2 decimal places. What is its upper bound, in volts?

    1. The rounding unit is 0.01. What is half of it?
    2. Add half the unit to 12.45.
    • That adds a whole unit (0.01). Add half a unit.
    • That uses 0.05 as half the unit, and rounds. The rounding was to 2 decimal places, so half the unit is 0.005.

    Half of 0.01 is 0.005. The upper bound is 12.45 + 0.005 = 12.455 volts.

    A distance is 4300 m, to 2 significant figures. What is its lower bound, in m?

    1. Which place is the last significant figure in? That gives the rounding unit.
    2. The 3 is in the hundreds place, so the unit is 100. Take away half of it.
    • That takes away the whole 100. Take away half of it.
    • That uses half of 10. The last significant figure is in the hundreds place, so the unit is 100.
    • That is the upper bound, because you added half of 100. The lower bound takes it away.
    • That treats 4300 as if it was rounded to the nearest 1. It is rounded to 2 significant figures, so the unit is 100.

    The last significant figure is the 3, in the hundreds place. The unit is 100, so half the unit is 50. The lower bound is 4300 − 50 = 4250 m.

    A length is 9.0 cm to 1 decimal place. What is its upper bound?

    • 9.05
    • 9.1, which is 9.0 + 0.1
    • 9.5, which is 9.0 + 0.5
    1. What is the rounding unit for 1 decimal place?
    2. Half of that unit is what you add to 9.0.

    The unit is 0.1, so half the unit is 0.05. The upper bound is 9.0 + 0.05 = 9.05 cm.

    2. Error intervals

    An Error interval uses an Inequality to write every value a rounded number could really be. The lower bound is included, because it still rounds up to the number. The upper bound is not included, because it would round up to the next number.

    lower bound ≤ x < upper bound

    So the error interval for 7, to the nearest whole number, is 6.5 ≤ x < 7.5. The sign on the left has the line under it, and the sign on the right does not.

    2.35 ≤ l < 2.45: a filled circle at the lower bound, an open circle at the upper bound.

    Key idea: error interval: lower bound ≤ x < upper bound. Include the lower bound, leave out the upper bound.

    Worked example

    A mass, m kg, is 2.4 kg to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval for m.

    1. Half the unit is 0.05.
    2. Lower bound: 2.4 − 0.05 = 2.35. Upper bound: 2.4 + 0.05 = 2.45.
    3. 2.35 ≤ m < 2.45.

    Now you finish it

    A mass, m g, is 530 g to 2 significant figures. Write the error interval for m.

    1. The last digit is in the tens place, so half the unit is ___.
    2. Lower bound ___, upper bound ___.
    3. ___ ≤ m < ___.
    Check my answer

    Half the unit is 5, so 525 ≤ m < 535.

    Check your knowledge: error intervals

    x = 7, to the nearest whole number. Write the error interval for x. Type it like 2.5<=x<3.5.

    1. What is half the rounding unit?
    2. Go that far below 7 and that far above 7. Then use ≤ on the left and < on the right.
    • The lower bound 6.5 rounds up to 7, so it is included. Use ≤ on the left.
    • 7.5 would round up to 8, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    Half the unit is 0.5. Lower bound 6.5, upper bound 7.5. The error interval is 6.5 ≤ x < 7.5.

    A length is l = 2.4 m, to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval for l.

    1. The rounding unit is 0.1. What is half of it?
    2. Subtract it from 2.4 and add it to 2.4.
    • That goes a whole unit (0.1) each way. Go half a unit: 0.05.
    • 2.45 would round up to 2.5, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    Half the unit is 0.05. Lower bound 2.35, upper bound 2.45. The error interval is 2.35 ≤ l < 2.45.

    n = 560, to 2 significant figures. Write the error interval for n.

    1. The 6 is in the tens place. What is the rounding unit?
    2. The unit is 10, so half of it is 5.
    • That goes 10 each way. Half the unit is 5.
    • 565 would round up to 570, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    The last significant figure is in the tens place, so the unit is 10 and half of it is 5. 555 ≤ n < 565.

    t = 0.085, to 2 significant figures. Write the error interval for t.

    1. Which place is the last significant figure (the 5) in?
    2. It is in the thousandths place, so the rounding unit is 0.001.
    • That uses a unit of 0.01. The last digit is in the thousandths place.
    • 0.0855 would round up, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    The 5 is in the thousandths place, so the unit is 0.001 and half of it is 0.0005. Lower bound 0.0845, upper bound 0.0855. 0.0845 ≤ t < 0.0855.

    A mass is m = 15 kg, to the nearest 5 kg. Write the error interval for m.

    1. The rounding unit is 5 kg this time. What is half of it?
    2. Go that far below 15 and above 15.
    • That uses 1 as the unit. The question rounds to the nearest 5 kg, so half the unit is 2.5.
    • That goes a whole unit (5) each way. Go half a unit.

    The unit is 5, so half the unit is 2.5. Lower bound 12.5, upper bound 17.5. 12.5 ≤ m < 17.5.

    A cable is cut to l = 3.60 m, correct to the nearest centimetre. Write the error interval for l, in metres.

    1. 1 centimetre is how many metres?
    2. The rounding unit is 0.01 m. Go half of it each way from 3.60.
    • That goes 0.05, half of 0.1. The rounding unit is 1 cm = 0.01 m, so half of it is 0.005.
    • 3.605 m would round up to 3.61, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    1 cm = 0.01 m, so half the unit is 0.005 m. Lower bound 3.595, upper bound 3.605. 3.595 ≤ l < 3.605.

    3. Truncated numbers

    Truncating a number means cutting off the digits after a place, without rounding. A calculator that shows 7.38 as 7.3 to 1 decimal place has truncated it. The number cannot have been rounded up, so the bounds are not half a unit each way.

    • The lower bound is the truncated number itself. It is included.
    • The upper bound is one whole unit above. It is not included.

    4.6 truncated to 1 d.p.   →   4.6 ≤ x < 4.7

    Numbers from 4.6 (included) up to 4.7 (not included) truncate to 4.6.

    Key idea: truncating cuts digits off, so the true number is at least the truncated number and below one whole unit more. Rounding goes half a unit each way. Truncating goes a whole unit up, and none down.

    Worked example

    A timer shows 4.6 seconds, truncated to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval for the time t.

    1. The unit is 0.1, so the upper bound is one whole unit above.
    2. Lower bound: 4.6. Upper bound: 4.6 + 0.1 = 4.7.
    3. 4.6 ≤ t < 4.7.

    Now you finish it

    A number is 23, truncated to a whole number. Write the error interval for the number, n.

    1. The lower bound is ___.
    2. The upper bound is one whole unit more: ___.
    3. ___ ≤ n < ___.
    Check my answer

    23 ≤ n < 24. For example 23.9 truncates to 23, but 23.9 rounds to 24.

    Check your knowledge: truncation

    x = 5.2, truncated to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval for x.

    1. Truncating cuts digits off. What is the smallest number that gives 5.2?
    2. The upper bound is one whole unit above (the unit is 0.1).
    • That is the interval for rounding. When a number is truncated, the true value is at least 5.2.
    • The lower bound 5.2 is possible (5.2 truncated is 5.2), so it is included.

    A truncated number is at least 5.2 and below 5.2 + 0.1. So 5.2 ≤ x < 5.3.

    A number is 12 after it has been truncated to a whole number. What is the smallest it could be?

    1. Truncating cuts off the part after the decimal point.
    2. Which is the smallest number that becomes 12 when the decimals are cut off?
    • That is the lower bound for rounding. When a number is truncated, nothing is rounded down.

    Cutting the decimals off 12.0 gives 12, and any smaller number gives 11 or less. So the smallest is 12.

    A number is 12 after it has been truncated to a whole number. What is its upper bound?

    1. A number such as 12.99 truncates to 12.
    2. What is the first number that would truncate to 13?
    • That is the upper bound for rounding. 12.9 truncates to 12 as well, so the bound is higher.
    • That is a value just below the bound. The bound itself is the first whole unit up.

    12.9, 12.99 and 12.999 all truncate to 12. The first number that does not is 13, so the upper bound is 13.

    A number is 3.4 when truncated to 1 decimal place. Which error interval is correct?

    • 3.4 ≤ x < 3.5
    • 3.35 ≤ x < 3.45, because half the unit is 0.05
    • 3.3 ≤ x < 3.5, because the unit is 0.1 both ways
    1. Truncating never rounds down, only cuts off.
    2. The number is at least 3.4. How far up can it go before it would truncate to 3.5?

    Truncated to 1 decimal place, the number is at least 3.4 and below 3.5.

    A stopwatch shows 14.8 seconds, truncated to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval for the real time t.

    1. The stopwatch cuts digits off, it does not round.
    2. The time is at least 14.8, and below one whole unit more.
    • That is the interval for rounding. The stopwatch truncates, so the real time is at least 14.8.

    The real time is at least 14.8 and below 14.8 + 0.1. So 14.8 ≤ t < 14.9.

    4. Bounds of sums and differences

    Now use the bounds in a calculation. Think about what makes the answer as big as possible, or as small as possible.

    • Sum a + b. Biggest: upper bound of a + upper bound of b. Smallest: lower bound of a + lower bound of b.
    • Difference a − b. Biggest: upper bound of a minus the lower bound of b. Smallest: lower bound of a minus the upper bound of b.

    Key idea: for a − b, to make the answer as big as possible you start with the biggest a and take away the smallest b. To make it as small as possible, take away the biggest b from the smallest a.

    Worked example

    a = 8 and b = 5, both to the nearest whole number. Find the greatest and smallest values of a + b and a − b.

    1. a: 7.5 ≤ a < 8.5. b: 4.5 ≤ b < 5.5.
    2. Greatest a + b: 8.5 + 5.5 = 14. Smallest a + b: 7.5 + 4.5 = 12.
    3. Greatest a − b: 8.5 − 4.5 = 4. Smallest a − b: 7.5 − 5.5 = 2.

    Worked example: lengths

    Two planks are 2.4 m and 1.7 m, each to 1 decimal place. Find the greatest possible difference in length.

    1. The longer plank: upper bound 2.45 m. The shorter plank: lower bound 1.65 m.
    2. Greatest difference: 2.45 − 1.65 = 0.8 m.

    Now you finish it

    A pipe is 3.2 m long and a piece 1.5 m long is cut off. Both lengths are to 1 decimal place. Find the greatest possible length left.

    1. Biggest pipe: upper bound ___.
    2. Smallest piece: lower bound ___.
    3. Greatest left: ___ − ___ = ___.
    Check my answer

    3.25 − 1.45 = 1.8 m.

    Check your knowledge: sums and differences

    a = 8 and b = 5, both to the nearest whole number. What is the greatest possible value of a + b?

    1. To make a sum as big as possible, use the biggest possible a and b.
    2. Use the upper bounds of both.
    • That is 8 + 5. Use the upper bounds, 8.5 and 5.5.
    • That is the smallest possible sum, from the lower bounds. The question asks for the greatest.

    Upper bounds: a = 8.5 and b = 5.5. Greatest a + b = 8.5 + 5.5 = 14.

    a = 8 and b = 5, both to the nearest whole number. What is the smallest possible value of a + b?

    1. To make a sum as small as possible, use the smallest possible a and b.
    2. Use the lower bounds of both.
    • That is the greatest possible sum, from the upper bounds. The question asks for the smallest.

    Lower bounds: a = 7.5 and b = 4.5. Smallest a + b = 7.5 + 4.5 = 12.

    p = 20.4 and q = 7.9, both to 1 decimal place. What is the greatest possible value of p − q?

    1. To make a difference as big as possible, start with the biggest p.
    2. Then take away the smallest possible q.
    • That is 20.4 − 7.9 with no bounds. Use the upper bound of p and the lower bound of q.
    • That is the smallest possible difference. For the greatest, take away the smallest q, not the biggest.

    Upper bound of p = 20.45. Lower bound of q = 7.85. Greatest p − q = 20.45 − 7.85 = 12.6.

    p = 20.4 and q = 7.9, both to 1 decimal place. What is the smallest possible value of p − q?

    1. To make a difference as small as possible, start with the smallest p.
    2. Then take away the biggest possible q.
    • That is the greatest possible difference. For the smallest, take away the biggest q.
    • That is 20.4 − 7.9 with no bounds. Use the lower bound of p and the upper bound of q.

    Lower bound of p = 20.35. Upper bound of q = 7.95. Smallest p − q = 20.35 − 7.95 = 12.4.

    Two lengths of conduit are 1.2 m, to 1 decimal place, and 0.85 m, to 2 decimal places. What is the greatest possible total length, in m?

    1. Write the upper bound of each length. They are rounded to different accuracies.
    2. 1.2 to 1 d.p. has half unit 0.05. 0.85 to 2 d.p. has half unit 0.005.
    • That is 1.25 + 0.85. It uses the upper bound for one length only. Use the upper bound of both.
    • That uses 0.05 as half the unit for both. 0.85 is to 2 d.p., so its half unit is 0.005.

    Upper bounds: 1.2 → 1.25 and 0.85 → 0.855. Greatest total = 1.25 + 0.855 = 2.105 m.

    A pipe is 2.5 m long, to 1 decimal place. A piece 0.8 m long, to 1 decimal place, is cut off. What is the smallest possible length left, in m?

    1. For the smallest length left, start with the smallest pipe.
    2. Then take away the biggest possible piece.
    • That is 2.5 − 0.8 with no bounds. Use the lower bound of the pipe and the upper bound of the piece.
    • That uses the upper bound for the pipe and the lower bound for the piece, which gives the greatest length left.

    Lower bound of the pipe = 2.45 m. Upper bound of the piece = 0.85 m. Smallest left = 2.45 − 0.85 = 1.6 m.

    5. Bounds of products and quotients

    For positive numbers, the rules are as follows.

    • Product a × b. Biggest: upper bound × upper bound. Smallest: lower bound × lower bound.
    • Quotient a ÷ b. Biggest: upper bound of a divided by the lower bound of b. Smallest: lower bound of a divided by the upper bound of b.

    Key idea: to make a division a ÷ b as big as possible, divide the biggest a by the smallest b. To make it as small as possible, divide the smallest a by the biggest b.

    Worked example: area

    A rectangle is 8 cm by 5 cm, both to the nearest cm. Find the greatest and smallest possible area.

    1. Length: 7.5 ≤ l < 8.5. Width: 4.5 ≤ w < 5.5.
    2. Greatest area: 8.5 × 5.5 = 46.75 cm².
    3. Smallest area: 7.5 × 4.5 = 33.75 cm².

    Worked example: a quotient

    a = 8 and b = 5, both to the nearest whole number. Find the greatest and smallest value of a ÷ b.

    1. Greatest: upper a over lower b: 8.5 ÷ 4.5 = 1.888….
    2. Smallest: lower a over upper b: 7.5 ÷ 5.5 = 1.363….

    Now you finish it

    A rectangle is 6.4 cm by 3.2 cm, both to 1 decimal place. Find the smallest possible area.

    1. Smallest length: ___. Smallest width: ___.
    2. Smallest area: ___ × ___.
    Check my answer

    6.35 × 3.15 = 20.0025 cm².

    Check your knowledge: products and quotients

    A tile is 20 cm by 10 cm, both to the nearest cm. What is the greatest possible area, in cm²?

    1. Write the upper bound of each side.
    2. Multiply the two upper bounds.
    • That is 20 × 10 with no bounds. Use the upper bounds, 20.5 and 10.5.
    • That adds 0.5 to the 10 only (20 × 10.5). Add 0.5 to both sides.

    Upper bounds: 20.5 and 10.5. Greatest area = 20.5 × 10.5 = 215.25 cm².

    A tile is 20 cm by 10 cm, both to the nearest cm. What is the smallest possible area, in cm²?

    1. Write the lower bound of each side.
    2. Multiply the two lower bounds.
    • That is 20 × 10 with no bounds. Use the lower bounds, 19.5 and 9.5.
    • That takes 0.5 off the 10 only (20 × 9.5). Take 0.5 off both sides.

    Lower bounds: 19.5 and 9.5. Smallest area = 19.5 × 9.5 = 185.25 cm².

    A rectangle is 6.4 cm by 3.2 cm, both to 1 decimal place. What is the smallest possible perimeter, in cm?

    1. A perimeter adds sides, so use the lower bound of each side.
    2. Perimeter = 2 × (length + width).
    • That is 2 × (6.4 + 3.2) with no bounds. Use the lower bounds, 6.35 and 3.15.
    • That uses the upper bounds, which gives the greatest perimeter.

    Lower bounds: 6.35 and 3.15. Smallest perimeter = 2 × (6.35 + 3.15) = 2 × 9.5 = 19 cm.

    x = 15 and y = 4, both to the nearest whole number. What is the greatest possible value of x ÷ y? Give your answer to 2 decimal places.

    1. To make a division as big as possible, divide the biggest x by the smallest y.
    2. Use the upper bound of x and the lower bound of y.
    • That is the smallest possible value (smallest x over biggest y). The question asks for the greatest.
    • That is 15 ÷ 4 with no bounds. Use 15.5 ÷ 3.5.

    Upper bound of x = 15.5. Lower bound of y = 3.5. Greatest x ÷ y = 15.5 ÷ 3.5 = 4.4285… ≈ 4.43.

    x = 15 and y = 4, both to the nearest whole number. What is the smallest possible value of x ÷ y? Give your answer to 2 decimal places.

    1. To make a division as small as possible, divide the smallest x by the biggest y.
    2. Use the lower bound of x and the upper bound of y.
    • That is the greatest possible value. For the smallest, use the smallest x and the biggest y.
    • That is 15 ÷ 4 with no bounds. Use 14.5 ÷ 4.5.

    Lower bound of x = 14.5. Upper bound of y = 4.5. Smallest x ÷ y = 14.5 ÷ 4.5 = 3.2222… ≈ 3.22.

    A 24.5 m cable, to 1 decimal place, is cut into pieces of 3.5 m, to 1 decimal place. What is the greatest possible value of 24.5 ÷ 3.5? Give your answer to 2 decimal places.

    1. Divide the biggest possible cable length by the smallest possible piece.
    2. Upper bound of 24.5, over lower bound of 3.5.
    • That is 24.5 ÷ 3.5 with no bounds. Use 24.55 ÷ 3.45.
    • That is the smallest possible value (smallest cable length over biggest piece length). The question asks for the greatest.

    Upper bound of the cable = 24.55 m. Lower bound of a piece = 3.45 m. Greatest value = 24.55 ÷ 3.45 = 7.1159… ≈ 7.12.

    6. Bounds in a formula

    The same ideas work in any Formula. Decide whether each letter should be as big or as small as possible, then use its upper or lower bound.

    speed = distance ÷ time

    • Greatest speed: the greatest distance and the least time. A big distance divided by a small time gives a big speed.
    • Smallest speed: the least distance and the greatest time.

    Key idea: look at the formula. A quantity that is divided (the distance in distance ÷ time) makes the answer bigger when it is bigger. A quantity you divide by (the time) makes the answer smaller when it is bigger.

    Worked example

    A car travels 120 m, to the nearest 10 m, in 8.4 s, to 1 decimal place. Find the greatest and smallest possible speed, to 2 decimal places.

    7. How accurately can you give the answer?

    A calculator might show a speed of 8.0 exactly. But if the distance and time were rounded, you cannot be sure of all those digits. To decide how many significant figures you can fairly give:

    • Work out the upper and lower bounds of the answer.
    • Round both to 1 s.f., then 2 s.f., then 3 s.f., and so on.
    • Find the most significant figures at which both bounds give the same number. That number is the answer you can justify.

    Key idea: if the upper bound and lower bound round to the same value, the true answer must round to that value too. Any more figures would be a guess.

    Worked example

    A car goes 36 m, to the nearest metre, in 4.5 s, to 1 decimal place. Find the speed to a degree of accuracy you can justify.

    1. Upper bound of speed = 36.5 ÷ 4.45 = 8.2022….
    2. Lower bound of speed = 35.5 ÷ 4.55 = 7.8021….
    3. To 2 s.f. they are 8.2 and 7.8. These are different, so we cannot give 2 s.f.
    4. To 1 s.f. they are both 8. So the speed is 8 m/s, to 1 significant figure.

    Flip it: run it backwards

    So far you start with a rounded number and find its limits. Now go the other way: you are given the limits, and you find the number that was rounded, and how it was rounded.

    Forwards

    7, to the nearest whole number. Limits?

    Half the unit each way: 6.5 ≤ x < 7.5.

    Backwards

    6.5 ≤ x < 7.5. What was rounded?

    Halfway between the bounds: (6.5 + 7.5) ÷ 2 = 7.

    • The rounded number is halfway between the lower and upper bounds. Add them and halve, or take away half the unit from the upper bound.
    • The gap between the bounds is one whole rounding unit. A gap of 1 means “nearest whole number”. A gap of 0.1 means “1 decimal place”.
    • If you know the upper bound of a calculation, work backwards through the calculation to find the upper bound of the starting number.

    Key idea: number = the middle of the interval. Rounding unit = upper bound − lower bound.

    Worked example

    The error interval for a length is 4.45 ≤ l < 4.55. The length was rounded to 1 decimal place. What is the length?

    1. Add the bounds and halve: (4.45 + 4.55) ÷ 2 = 4.5.
    2. Check: the gap is 0.1, which fits 1 decimal place.
    3. The length is 4.5.

    Now you finish it

    The upper bound of a mass is 62.5 kg. It was rounded to the nearest kg. What is the mass?

    1. The unit is 1, so half the unit is ___.
    2. Mass = upper bound − half the unit = ___ − ___.
    Check my answer

    62.5 − 0.5 = 62 kg.

    Finding an error in someone else’s working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

    Tom says: a length of 5.4 cm, to 1 decimal place, has an upper bound of 5.5 cm. What went wrong?

    • He should have added 0.5, because half a centimetre is the rule
    • Nothing is wrong, because 5.4 rounds up to 5.5

    2. Half of that is what you add to 5.4.

    The unit is 0.1, so half the unit is 0.05. The upper bound is 5.4 + 0.05 = 5.45 cm.

    Ava says: the greatest possible value of a − b is the upper bound of a minus the upper bound of b. What went wrong?

    • She should take away the lower bound of b
    • She should use the lower bound of a, to make the answer big
    • Nothing is wrong, because upper bounds always give the greatest answer
    1. To make a difference big, start with a big number.
    2. Then take away as little as you can.

    Greatest a − b = upper bound of a − lower bound of b. You take away the smallest b possible.

    Ben says: the error interval for 12, to the nearest whole number, is 11.5 < x ≤ 12.5. What went wrong?

    • The bounds are wrong, because they should be 11 and 13

    2. 11.5 rounds up to 12. 12.5 rounds up to 13.

    Why it works

    • Rounding makes a range, not a single value. A rounded number stands for a whole range of true values. The half-unit rule comes from the middle of the range being the rounded number, and its width being one unit.
    • The lower bound is in and the upper bound is out. The number halfway between two rounded values has to go to one of them. The convention is that it goes up. So 6.5 rounds up to 7, and 7.5 rounds up to 8.
    • To get the biggest answer, pick the best end of each number. In a sum or product you want both numbers big. In a difference you want the first big and the second small. In a quotient you want the number being divided big and the number you divide by small.
    • Why both bounds must agree. The true answer is somewhere between the lower and upper bounds. If both round to the same number, the true answer rounds to it too. If they do not, the extra digits are guesses.
    • Truncation only goes one way. Cutting digits off can only make a number smaller, so the true value is at least the truncated number and less than one whole unit more.

    Common traps

    • Using the whole rounding unit instead of half of it.
    • Using half of the wrong unit, for example half a metre for a number rounded to 1 decimal place.
    • Using the upper bound of both numbers in a difference. The number being taken away needs its lower bound for the greatest answer.
    • Writing the error interval with the wrong signs: lower bound ≤ x < upper bound.
    • Treating a truncated number as if it were rounded.
    • Giving an answer to more significant figures than the bounds support.

    • That is 24 × 1.90 with no bounds. Use the upper bound of the length, 24.5 m.
    • That uses the lower bound, 23.5 m, which gives the smallest cost.

    Upper bound of the length = 24.5 m. Greatest cost = 24.5 × 1.90 = £46.55.

    A copper pipe has an outside diameter of d = 15 mm, to the nearest millimetre. Write the error interval for d.

    1. The rounding unit is 1 mm. What is half of it?
    2. Go that far below and above 15, with ≤ on the left and < on the right.
    • That uses half of 0.1. The rounding is to the nearest millimetre, so half the unit is 0.5.
    • 15.5 would round up to 16, so it is not included. Use < on the right.

    Half the unit is 0.5 mm. Lower bound 14.5, upper bound 15.5. 14.5 ≤ d < 15.5.

    [3 marks] A carpenter has a board 2.40 m long, to the nearest centimetre. She cuts shelves 0.60 m long, to the nearest centimetre. What is the smallest possible value of the board length ÷ the shelf length? Give your answer to 3 significant figures. Can she be certain of getting 4 shelves?

    1. For the smallest quotient, divide the smallest board length by the biggest shelf length.
    2. Half the unit is 0.005 m, since the rounding is to the nearest centimetre.
    3. Lower bound of the board over upper bound of a shelf.
    • That is 2.40 ÷ 0.60 with no bounds. Use 2.395 ÷ 0.605.
    • That is the greatest possible value (biggest board length over smallest shelf length). The question asks for the smallest.

    Lower bound of the board = 2.395 m. Upper bound of a shelf = 0.605 m. Smallest value = 2.395 ÷ 0.605 = 3.9587… ≈ 3.96. This is less than 4, so she cannot be certain of getting 4 shelves.

    • M1 for the lower bound of the board, 2.395
    • M1 for the upper bound of a shelf, 0.605, and dividing
    • A1 for 3.96 (so she cannot be certain of 4 shelves)

    A bolt has diameter 11.8 mm, to 1 decimal place, and fits in a hole of diameter 12.0 mm, to 1 decimal place. What is the smallest possible gap between the bolt and the hole, in mm?

    1. The gap is the hole diameter minus the bolt diameter.
    2. For the smallest gap, use the smallest hole and the biggest bolt.
    • That is 12.0 − 11.8 with no bounds. Use the lower bound of the hole and the upper bound of the bolt.
    • That is the greatest possible gap. For the smallest, use the smallest hole and the biggest bolt.

    Lower bound of the hole = 11.95 mm. Upper bound of the bolt = 11.85 mm. Smallest gap = 11.95 − 11.85 = 0.1 mm.

    [4 marks] The current in a circuit is I = V ÷ R. The voltage V is 230 V, to the nearest 10 V, and the resistance R is 46 ohms, to the nearest ohm. Work out the current to a degree of accuracy that is justified, in amps.

    1. Greatest current: greatest voltage over least resistance. Smallest current: least voltage over greatest resistance.
    2. Work out both bounds, then round both to 2 s.f. and to 1 s.f.
    3. Give the answer at the most significant figures where both bounds agree.
    • That is the upper bound to 2 s.f. The bounds differ at 2 s.f., so give 1 s.f.
    • That is the lower bound to 2 s.f. The upper bound is 5.2 to 2 s.f., so 2 s.f. is not justified.

    Upper bound = 235 ÷ 45.5 = 5.1648…. Lower bound = 225 ÷ 46.5 = 4.8387…. To 2 s.f.: 5.2 and 4.8, which differ. To 1 s.f.: both 5. So the current is 5 A to 1 s.f.

    • M1 for 235 ÷ 45.5 (the upper bound of the current)
    • M1 for 225 ÷ 46.5 (the lower bound of the current)
    • A1 for 5.16 and 4.84 (to 2 decimal places)
    • C1 for both bounds rounding to 5 at 1 s.f., so 5 A

    [3 marks] A plumber fills a tank of 150 litres, to the nearest 10 litres, using a pump that delivers 12 litres per minute, to the nearest litre per minute. What is the greatest possible time to fill the tank? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in minutes.

    1. Time = volume ÷ rate. For the greatest time, use a big volume and a small rate.
    2. Upper bound of the volume over the lower bound of the rate.
    3. Half the unit for the volume is 5, and for the rate is 0.5.
    • That is 150 ÷ 12 with no bounds. Use 155 ÷ 11.5.
    • That is the smallest possible time (smallest volume over greatest rate). The question asks for the greatest.

    Upper bound of the volume = 155 litres. Lower bound of the rate = 11.5 litres per minute. Greatest time = 155 ÷ 11.5 = 13.478… ≈ 13.5 minutes.

    • M1 for the upper bound of the volume, 155
    • M1 for the lower bound of the pump rate, 11.5, and dividing
    • A1 for 13.5 (13.478… before rounding)
