Coordinates and straight-line graphs

A pair of coordinates pins a point on a grid, and an equation like y = 2x + 1 draws a straight line. Learn to plot points and lines, to read the gradient and where a line crosses the y-axis, and to write the equation of a line from its graph.

Where you will use this: the fall on a waste pipe, a wheelchair ramp, a plumber’s quote against hours, and the speed on a distance-time graph.

Words you need

Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

Coordinates
A pair of numbers (x, y) that says where a point is on a grid: x is how far along, y is how far up.
Gradient
How steep a line is: rise divided by run. A line that goes up to the right has a positive gradient, and one that goes down has a negative gradient.
y-intercept
The value of y where a line crosses the y-axis (where x = 0). In y = mx + c it is c.
Midpoint
The point exactly halfway along a line segment. Add the x-coordinates and halve, and do the same for y.

Reading and writing coordinates

Always write (x, y): along first, then up or down.

Right and up are positive. Left and down are negative.

The origin is (0, 0), where the two axes cross.

In an equation, y = mx + c: m is the gradient and c is where the line crosses the y-axis.

1. Coordinates

A pair of Coordinates tells you exactly where a point is on a grid. The grid has two axes: the x-axis goes across and the y-axis goes up. They cross at the Origin, which is (0, 0).

Write a point as (x, y). The x-coordinate comes first and says how far along: right is positive, left is negative. The y-coordinate comes second and says how far up or down: up is positive, down is negative. A way to remember: along the corridor, then up the stairs.

The axes split the grid into four quadrants. They are numbered 1 to 4 going anticlockwise from the top right. In quadrant 1 both x and y are positive. In quadrant 2 (top left) x is negative and y is positive. In quadrant 3 (bottom left) both are negative. In quadrant 4 (bottom right) x is positive and y is negative.

Key idea: (x, y) means along first, then up or down. If a point is left of the y-axis, its x is negative. If it is below the x-axis, its y is negative.

Try it: which quadrant is the point (−4, −1) in? (Left and down.)

Worked example

Write the coordinates of point B on the grid above.

  1. Start at the origin. B is to the left, so x is negative. It is 4 units left: x = −4.
  2. B is above the x-axis, 3 units up: y = 3.
  3. B is (−4, 3). It is in quadrant 2.

Now you finish it

2. The midpoint of a line

The Midpoint of a line is the point exactly halfway along it. To find it, take the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates: add them, then halve.

midpoint = ((x1 + x2) ÷ 2, (y1 + y2) ÷ 2)

Key idea: the midpoint is the mean of the two points. Work on the x-coordinates and the y-coordinates one at a time. Take care with negative numbers when you add.

Try it: what is halfway between 2 and 10 on a number line? Use the same idea for x and for y.

Worked example

Find the midpoint of (−4, 2) and (6, −8).

  1. x: (−4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 2 ÷ 2 = 1.
  2. y: (2 + (−8)) ÷ 2 = −6 ÷ 2 = −3.
  3. The midpoint is (1, −3).

Now you finish it

Find the midpoint of (−3, −1) and (5, 7).

  1. x: (−3 + 5) ÷ 2 = ___ ÷ 2 = ___.
  2. y: (−1 + 7) ÷ 2 = ___ ÷ 2 = ___.
  3. The midpoint is (___, ___).
Check my answer

x: 2 ÷ 2 = 1. y: 6 ÷ 2 = 3. The midpoint is (1, 3).

Check your knowledge: midpoints

  2. Do the same for the y-coordinates.
  • That looks like adding the coordinates but not halving.

  2. Halve each total.

(−6 + 2) ÷ 2 = −4 ÷ 2 = −2. (3 + (−5)) ÷ 2 = −2 ÷ 2 = −1. The midpoint is (−2, −1).

M is the midpoint of AB. A is (1, −2) and M is (4, 1). Find B. Type it like (2, 5).

  1. Going from A to M is exactly halfway to B.
  2. So B is the same step again from M. How far along and up is the step from A to M?

  • That is the step from A to M. Make the same step again from M.

From A to M: 4 − 1 = 3 along and 1 − (−2) = 3 up. Do the same step again from M: (4 + 3, 1 + 3) = (7, 4). Check: the midpoint of (1, −2) and (7, 4) is (4, 1). ✓

  2. Find the middle of the x-values and the middle of the y-values.
  • That looks like adding the coordinates but not halving.
  • That looks like the gaps (9 − 1 and 6 − 2), not the midpoint.

(1 + 9) ÷ 2 = 5 and (2 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4. The support is at (5, 4).

3. Plotting a line from its equation

An equation like y = 2x − 1 is a rule. Pick any x, put it in, and the rule gives you y. Each pair (x, y) is a point, and all the points lie on one straight line.

  1. Draw a table of values. Choose x-values that include negative numbers, zero and positive numbers, such as −2 to 2.
  2. Work out y for each x.
  3. Plot each point (x, y).
  4. Join the points with a ruler and extend the line across the grid.

Key idea: a rule with just x (no x²) and y makes a straight line. The points should go up (or down) in even steps. If one point is out of line, check its arithmetic.

You can also test whether a point is on a line: put its x into the equation. If you get its y, it is on the line. (3, 5) is on y = 2x − 1 because 2 × 3 − 1 = 5.

4. Gradient and y-intercept

The Gradient of a line says how steep it is. You find it with a right-angled triangle under the line.

gradient = rise ÷ run

The run is how far you go along. The rise is how far you go up in that same step. Between two points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2), the gradient is (y2 − y1) ÷ (x2 − x1).

Read the line from left to right. If it climbs, the gradient is positive. If it falls, the gradient is negative, because the rise is a fall.

The y-intercept is the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis. At that point x = 0.

Key idea: gradient = rise ÷ run, in that order, reading left to right. Up is positive and down is negative. Always check the sign against the picture.

Try it: a line goes up 6 while going along 3. What is its gradient? (Rise ÷ run.)

Worked example: between two points

Find the gradient of the line through (1, 2) and (4, 11).

  1. Rise: 11 − 2 = 9.
  2. Run: 4 − 1 = 3.
  3. Gradient = 9 ÷ 3 = 3.

Worked example: a line that falls

Find the gradient of the line through (−1, 5) and (3, −3).

  1. Rise: −3 − 5 = −8 (a fall of 8).
  2. Run: 3 − (−1) = 4.
  3. Gradient = −8 ÷ 4 = −2.

Now you finish it

Find the gradient of the line through (2, 1) and (6, 9).

  1. Rise: 9 − 1 = ___.
  2. Run: 6 − 2 = ___.
  3. Gradient = ___ ÷ ___ = ___.
Check my answer

Rise 8, run 4, gradient = 8 ÷ 4 = 2.

  2. Write rise over run, then multiply the top and bottom to get whole numbers and simplify.

  2. Work out 0 − 9 on the top and 4 − 1 on the bottom.

  • That looks like run ÷ rise. Gradient is rise ÷ run.

(0 − 9) ÷ (4 − 1) = −9 ÷ 3 = −3.

5. Reading the equation y = mx + c

Every straight line that is not vertical has an equation of the form y = mx + c.

  • m is the gradient: the number multiplying x.
  • c is the y-intercept: the number on its own.

So y = 3x + 2 has gradient 3 and crosses the y-axis at 2. If x stands alone, as in y = x + 4, it is 1x, so the gradient is 1. If the equation is not in this form, rearrange it first: 2y = 8x + 6 becomes y = 4x + 3 when you divide every term by 2.

Writing the equation from a graph

  1. Read c: where does the line cross the y-axis?
  2. Find m: draw a triangle and work out rise ÷ run.
  3. Write y = mx + c with your numbers.

Parallel lines

Lines that are Parallel slope at the same steepness, so they have the same gradient. They have different y-intercepts, or they would be the same line. y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3 are parallel.

Key idea: in y = mx + c, read m from the number that multiplies x (with its sign) and c from the number on its own. Parallel lines: same m.

Try it: write down the gradient and the y-intercept of y = 5x − 3. (m = ?, c = ?)

Worked example

A line crosses the y-axis at (0, −1) and goes up 3 for every 1 along. Write its equation.

  1. c = −1, because it crosses the y-axis at −1.
  2. m = rise ÷ run = 3 ÷ 1 = 3.
  3. The equation is y = 3x − 1.

Worked example: rearranging first

Find the gradient and the y-intercept of the line 3x + y = 7.

  1. Take 3x from both sides: y = 7 − 3x, which is y = −3x + 7.
  2. The gradient is −3 and the y-intercept is 7.

Now you finish it

  2. m = ___ ÷ ___ = ___ (it goes down, so it is negative).
  3. The equation is y = ___x + ___.

6. Perpendicular lines Higher

Two lines at a right angle are Perpendicular. Their gradients multiply to give −1. To find the gradient of a perpendicular line, flip the gradient upside down and change its sign.

  • Gradient 2 (which is 2 over 1) gives −1 over 2. Check: 2 × (−1 over 2) = −1.
  • Gradient −3 over 4 gives 4 over 3. Check: −3 over 4 × 4 over 3 = −1.

This is because turning a line through 90° swaps the rise and the run, and one of them changes direction.

Worked example

Line L is y = 2x + 1. Find the gradient of a line perpendicular to L.

  1. The gradient of L is 2.
  2. Flip it to get 1 over 2, and change the sign: −1 over 2.

Now you finish it

Find the gradient of a line perpendicular to a line with gradient 5.

  1. 5 is 5 over 1. Flip it: ___.
  2. Change the sign: ___.
Check my answer

Flip 5 over 1 to get 1 over 5, then change the sign: −1 over 5. Check: 5 × (−1 over 5) = −1.

Check your knowledge: perpendicular lines Higher

  1. Perpendicular gradients multiply to give −1.
  2. Flip the fraction and change the sign.

  • That changes the sign but does not flip it. Do both.

  • y = x/2 + 3
  • y = −2x + 3
  1. The gradient of y = 2x + 3 is 2.
  2. Find a gradient that multiplies with 2 to give −1.

The gradient of y = −x/2 is −1/2, and 2 × (−1/2) = −1, so the lines are perpendicular.

7. Where two lines cross

Two straight lines that are not parallel cross at exactly one point. That point lies on both lines, so its x and y fit both equations. That makes it the answer to a pair of Simultaneous equations. The graph is the picture of the algebra.

The lines y = x + 1 and y = 7 − x cross at (3, 4). Check: 3 + 1 = 4 and 7 − 3 = 4. ✓ To find the crossing point by algebra instead of a drawing, see Simultaneous equations. A shortcut for two lines written as y = … is to set the right-hand sides equal, because at the crossing point they give the same y.

Key idea: the crossing point is a solution of both equations. Check any answer by putting it into both.

Try it: is (2, 4) on both y = 2x and y = 6 − x? (Test each.)

Worked example

Show that the lines y = 2x and y = 9 − x cross at (3, 6).

  1. Put x = 3 into y = 2x: y = 6. ✓
  2. Put x = 3 into y = 9 − x: y = 6. ✓
  3. Both give y = 6, so (3, 6) is on both lines: that is where they cross.

Now you finish it

8. Graphs from real life

Many real situations are straight-line graphs. The same ideas apply: the gradient is the rate of change, and the y-intercept is the starting value.

Cost against hours

A plumber charges a £60 call-out fee plus £45 for every hour. The cost C (in pounds) for h hours is C = 45h + 60.

  • The y-intercept, 60, is the call-out fee: what you pay for 0 hours.
  • The gradient, 45, is the cost of each extra hour: £45 per hour.

Distance against time

On a distance-time graph, the gradient is the speed: distance ÷ time. A steeper line is a faster journey. A flat line means no movement (the vehicle has stopped). A line that goes back down means travelling back towards the start.

Key idea: on a real-life graph, say what the gradient means (for example £ per hour or km per hour) and what the y-intercept means (the cost or distance at the start).

Try it: in C = 45h + 60, what is the cost for 2 hours? (Put h = 2.)

Worked example

Use the graph above to find the cost of a 4-hour job, and the cost of each extra hour.

  1. Four hours: the point (4, 240), so the cost is £240. (Check: 45 × 4 + 60 = 240.)
  2. From (0, 60) to (4, 240) the cost rises by 180 over 4 hours.
  3. Gradient = 180 ÷ 4 = 45, so each extra hour costs £45.

Flip it: from the equation to the line, and back

So far you have mostly gone from an equation to a picture. Now go the other way.

Equation from the line

a line → y = mx + c

Read c where it crosses the y-axis. Find m from rise ÷ run.

Key idea: you need two things to pin down a line: its gradient and one point on it (the y-intercept is the easiest point). If you are given two points instead, find the gradient first, then use one point to find c.

Worked example: the equation through two points

Find the equation of the line through (1, 3) and (3, 7).

  1. Gradient: (7 − 3) ÷ (3 − 1) = 4 ÷ 2 = 2. So y = 2x + c.
  2. Put in (1, 3): 3 = 2 × 1 + c, so c = 1.
  3. The equation is y = 2x + 1. Check (3, 7): 2 × 3 + 1 = 7. ✓

Spot the mistake

Finding an error in someone else’s working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

Sam says: the gradient of the line through (0, 1) and (2, 5) is 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. What went wrong?

  • He used run ÷ rise instead of rise ÷ run.
  • Nothing is wrong. The gradient is run ÷ rise.
  • The gradient should be negative.
  1. Work out the rise and the run between the two points.
  2. Gradient is the rise divided by the run, in that order.

The rise is 5 − 1 = 4 and the run is 2 − 0 = 2. Gradient = 4 ÷ 2 = 2. Sam turned it upside down.

Mia says: the line y = 3 − 2x has gradient 3 and y-intercept −2, because she read the numbers in the order they are written. What went wrong?

  • The gradient multiplies x, so it is −2, and c is 3.
  • Nothing is wrong. In y = mx + c, the first number is m.
  • She should have swapped the signs of both numbers, to −3 and 2.
  1. Write the equation in the form y = mx + c, with the x term first.
  2. Which number multiplies x, and which stands on its own?

y = 3 − 2x is the same as y = −2x + 3. The gradient m is −2 and the y-intercept c is 3.

Why it works

  • m is the gradient. Each time x goes up by 1, y goes up by m. That steady change is what makes the graph a straight line.
  • c is the y-intercept. On the y-axis x = 0, so y = m × 0 + c = c.
  • Parallel lines have the same gradient. Equal steepness means the lines never get closer or further apart.
  • The crossing point fits both equations. It is on both lines, so its coordinates work in both. That is exactly what “simultaneous” means.

Common traps

  • Writing (y, x) instead of (x, y).
  • Working out gradient as run ÷ rise. It is rise ÷ run.
  • Forgetting the minus sign when a line goes down.
  • Reading c from where the line crosses the x-axis. c is on the y-axis.
  • In y = 7 − 3x, reading the gradient as 7. Rewrite it as y = −3x + 7 first.
  • Thinking that parallel lines need the same c. They need the same m.
