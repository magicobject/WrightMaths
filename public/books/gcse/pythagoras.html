Pythagoras' theorem

One rule about right-angled triangles that finds missing lengths, and runs both ways: find the long side, find a short side, or test whether a triangle has a right angle at all.

Where you will use this: checking a corner is square with a 3-4-5 triangle, working out how long a diagonal pipe, rafter or ladder must be, and measuring across a room without a tape long enough to reach.

Words you need

Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

Hypotenuse
The longest side of a right-angled triangle. It is always opposite the right angle.
Right angle
An angle of exactly 90°, like the corner of a page. It is marked with a small square.
Pythagoras' theorem
In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides: a² + b² = c².
Pythagorean triple
Three whole numbers that fit a² + b² = c².
Converse
The reverse of a statement: swap "if" and "then". A converse is not automatically true, but for Pythagoras it is.
Square root
The positive number that, multiplied by itself, gives the number you started with. Written with the sign √.

Warm-up: what do you already know?

A 5 m ladder leans against a vertical wall. Its foot is 3 m from the wall. How high up the wall does the ladder reach, in metres? (Hint: it is a nice whole number.)

  1. The ladder, the wall and the ground make a right-angled triangle. Which side is the ladder?
  2. Try 3² + ?² = 5². What square number added to 9 makes 25?
  • That is 3 + 5. The ladder is the longest side, so the wall must be shorter than 5 m.
  • That is 5 − 3. Lengths do not subtract like that in a right-angled triangle: work with squares.
  • That is √(9 + 25). But the ladder (5 m) is the longest side, so the answer must be shorter than 5 m: subtract the squares.

9 + ?² = 25, so ?² = 16 and ? = 4. The ladder reaches 4 m up the wall. You just used Pythagoras.

1. The theorem

A triangle with one right angle is a right-angled triangle. Its longest side, always opposite the right angle, is the Hypotenuse. Pythagoras' theorem says the squares on the two shorter sides add up to the square on the hypotenuse.

a² + b² = c²

Key idea: it only works for right-angled triangles, and the c must be the hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle.

Worked example: find the hypotenuse

A right-angled triangle has shorter sides 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. a² + b² = c², so 6² + 8² = c².
  2. 36 + 64 = 100, so c² = 100.
  3. c = √100 = 10 cm.
  4. Sense check: 10 is longer than 6 and 8 ✓

Units: if the sides are in cm, then a², b² and c² are areas in cm² (square units). The square root takes c back to cm.

Many triangles do not give a whole number. Then use a calculator and round as asked. Keep the full calculator value while you work and round only at the end. A set of three whole numbers that fits the rule, like 3, 4, 5 or 5, 12, 13, is a Pythagorean triple.

A proof you can check Higher

9 + 16 = 25 is one example. A Proof shows that a rule is true for every case, not just one. Here is a proof of Pythagoras' theorem using areas.

Take any right-angled triangle with short sides a and b and hypotenuse c. Take four identical copies of this triangle. Draw a big square with side a + b. Now fit the four triangles inside it in two different ways.

Is the tilted shape really a square?

All four of its sides are c, because each one is the hypotenuse of one of the four identical triangles. Now check its angles. The three angles of a triangle add to 180° and one of them is 90°, so the two acute angles of each triangle add to 90°. At each corner of the tilted shape, two different acute angles and one angle of the shape sit together on a straight line, which is 180°. So the angle of the shape is 180° − 90° = 90°. Four equal sides and four right angles make a square, so its area is c².

The proof in steps

  1. The big square is the same size in both pictures. Its side is a + b, so its area is (a + b)².
  2. Arrangement 1: big square = a² + b² + (four triangles).
  3. Arrangement 2: big square = c² + (four triangles).
  4. The big squares are equal and the four triangles are the same in both, so a² + b² + (four triangles) = c² + (four triangles).
  5. Take away the four triangles from both sides: a² + b² = c².

The same proof in algebra

The big square has area (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². Each triangle has area 1 over 2ab, so four triangles have area 4 × 1 over 2ab = 2ab. Arrangement 2 gives (a + b)² = c² + 2ab. So a² + 2ab + b² = c² + 2ab. Take away 2ab from both sides to leave a² + b² = c².

Key idea: nothing in the proof depends on the numbers 3 and 4. It works for any right-angled triangle, which is why it is a proof and not just an example.

Check your knowledge: the proof Higher

A big square has side 7 cm. Four right-angled triangles with short sides 3 cm and 4 cm sit inside it, leaving a tilted square in the middle. Find the area of the tilted square, in cm².

  1. Area of the tilted square = area of the big square − area of the four triangles.
  2. One triangle has area 1 over 2 × 3 × 4.
  • That is the whole big square. Take away the four triangles as well.
  • You took away one triangle (6), not four.
  • You used 3 × 4 = 12 for each triangle. A triangle is half of that.

Big square: 7 × 7 = 49. One triangle: 1 over 2 × 3 × 4 = 6, so four triangles = 24. Tilted square: 49 − 24 = 25 cm².

What is the side length of the tilted square in the last question, in cm?

  1. You know its area. Undo the squaring.
  • That is the area. The side is the square root of the area.
  • That is 25 ÷ 2. Take the square root.

√25 = 5 cm. This is the hypotenuse of the 3, 4, 5 triangle, as the theorem says: 3² + 4² = 5².

Which statement is the key reason that a² + b² = c² for every right-angled triangle?

  • The big square has the same area in both pictures, and both use the same four triangles
  • 3² + 4² = 5², so the rule must hold for all triangles
  • The triangles in the second picture are bigger than in the first
  1. What is the same in both pictures? What does that let you cancel?

The big square and the four triangles are the same in both pictures, so what is left over must have the same area: a² + b² = c².

Four triangles with short sides 5 cm and 12 cm sit inside a big square of side 17 cm, leaving a tilted square. Find the area of the tilted square, in cm².

  1. Big square minus four triangles.
  • That is the whole big square. Take away the four triangles.
  • You took away only two triangles. There are four.

17² = 289. One triangle: 1 over 2 × 5 × 12 = 30, so four triangles = 120. Tilted square: 289 − 120 = 169 cm², so its side is 13 cm: the 5, 12, 13 triple.

2. Finding a shorter side

If you know the hypotenuse and one other side, rearrange the rule: a² = c² − b². You subtract the squares.

Key idea: the hypotenuse is always the longest side. So when you find a short side, your answer must be smaller than the hypotenuse. If it is bigger, you added when you should have subtracted.

Worked example

The hypotenuse is 13 cm and one shorter side is 5 cm. Find the other side.

  1. a² + 5² = 13², so a² = 13² − 5².
  2. a² = 169 − 25 = 144.
  3. a = √144 = 12 cm.
  4. Sense check: 12 is shorter than 13 ✓

Now you finish it

The hypotenuse is 10 cm and one shorter side is 6 cm.

  1. a² = 10² − 6² = 100 − 36 = ___.
  2. a = √___ = ___.
Check my answer

a² = 64 and a = 8 cm. (A 6, 8, 10 triangle: double the 3, 4, 5.)

  • That is the square of the answer. Take the square root.

625 − 49 = 576, so the side is √576 = 24 cm.

Flip it: the theorem backwards

So far you have used the rule to find a length. Flip it, and use it to test a triangle.

Forwards

"The triangle is right-angled, so a² + b² = c²."

Find the missing length.

Backwards

"a² + b² = c², so the triangle is right-angled."

Decide whether the angle is 90°.

Key idea: the backwards statement is called the Converse. It is not true for every theorem, but it is true for Pythagoras (it is proved later with the cosine rule). Builders use it: lay out a 3, 4, 5 triangle and the corner is a perfect right angle.

Worked example

Is a triangle with sides 7, 24 and 25 right-angled?

  1. Test the longest side as c: 7² + 24² = 49 + 576 = 625.
  2. 25² = 625. They match, so yes.

If the two sides of the test do not match, the triangle is not right-angled.

When the sides do not match

The test also says what kind of triangle you have. In the test, c must be the longest side. Add the squares of the two shorter sides and compare with c². (In any triangle the longest side is shorter than the other two added together, which is called the triangle inequality. So sides of 3, 4 and 8 cannot make a triangle at all, because 8 is more than 3 + 4 = 7.)

  • If a² + b² = c², the triangle is right-angled.
  • If a² + b² > c², the triangle is acute: every angle is less than 90°.
  • If a² + b² < c², the triangle is obtuse: the biggest angle is more than 90°.

Why: if the angle between the two short sides is wider than a right angle, the third side is longer than it would be for a right angle, so c² is bigger than a² + b². The triangle in the next set with sides 5, 6 and 8 is an example (25 + 36 = 61 is less than 64).

3. Using it in the real world

Look for a right angle hiding in the problem. Sketch it, mark the right angle, and decide whether you are finding the hypotenuse or a shorter side.

Worked example: distance on a grid

Point A is (1, 2) and point B is (5, 5). Find the distance AB.

  1. Across: 5 − 1 = 4. Up: 5 − 2 = 3.
  2. These are the shorter sides of a right-angled triangle, and AB is the hypotenuse.
  3. AB² = 4² + 3² = 25, so AB = 5.

In general, for any two points, d² = (change in x)² + (change in y)². It does not matter whether a change is positive or negative, because squaring removes the sign.

Worked example: a hidden right angle

An isosceles triangle has two sides of 13 cm and a base of 10 cm. Find its height.

  1. The height splits the base in half, so one half is 5 cm.
  2. Each half makes a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 13 and a shorter side 5.
  3. h² = 13² − 5² = 144, so h = 12 cm.

Check your knowledge: in context

Find the distance between (0, 0) and (3, 4).

  1. Across 3, up 4, then the hypotenuse.
  • That is 3 + 4, the distance if you could only travel along the grid. The straight-line distance is shorter.

√(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

A rectangle is 9 cm by 12 cm. How long is its diagonal, in cm?

  1. The diagonal splits the rectangle into two right-angled triangles. It is the hypotenuse.
  • That is 9 + 12. Square, add, then square root.

81 + 144 = 225, and √225 = 15 cm.

A 6.5 m ladder leans against a vertical wall with its foot 2.5 m from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach, in m?

  1. The ladder is the hypotenuse. You want a shorter side, so subtract.
  2. 6.5² = 42.25 and 2.5² = 6.25.
  • You added the squares. The wall is shorter than the ladder, so subtract.

42.25 − 6.25 = 36 and √36 = 6 m.

4. Pythagoras in three dimensions Higher

The longest straight line inside a cuboid runs from one corner to the opposite corner. Find it in two steps, using Pythagoras twice: first the diagonal of the base, then a right-angled triangle that stands up from that diagonal.

Key idea: for a cuboid with sides l, w and h, the base diagonal d has d² = l² + w². The longest line x has x² = d² + h², so x² = l² + w² + h².

Worked example

A cuboid measures 3 cm by 4 cm by 12 cm. Find the longest straight line inside it.

  1. Base diagonal: 3² + 4² = 25, so the diagonal is 5 cm.
  2. The line from a base corner up to the opposite top corner is the hypotenuse of a triangle with short sides 5 and 12.
  3. x² = 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169, so x = 13 cm.

Shortcut check: 9 + 16 + 144 = 169. ✓

Exam style

Show your working. The marks tell you how many steps to write.

(a) [3 marks] A television screen is 40 cm wide and 30 cm tall. Work out the length of its diagonal, in cm.

  1. The diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle.
  2. 1600 + 900 = ?
  • That is 40 + 30. Square each, add, then take the square root.
  • That is the square. Take the square root.

c² = 40² + 30² = 1600 + 900 = 2500, so c = 50 cm. (A 3, 4, 5 triangle times 10.)

  • M1 for using Pythagoras: c² = 40² + 30²
  • M1 for 1600 + 900 = 2500
  • A1 for c = 50

(b) [3 marks] A flagpole is held upright by a wire 13 m long from the top to a point on the ground 5 m from the base of the pole. How tall is the pole, in m?

  1. The wire is the hypotenuse. You want a shorter side: subtract.
  • You added the squares. The pole is shorter than the wire.
  • That is 13 − 5. Lengths do not subtract: work with squares.

13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144, so the pole is 12 m tall.

  • M1 for using Pythagoras to find a shorter side: 13² − 5²
  • M1 for 169 − 25 = 144
  • A1 for 12

(c) [3 marks] Work out the area of the right-angled triangle formed by the pole, the ground and the wire, in m².

  1. The pole and the ground meet at a right angle, so they are the base and the height.
  2. Area of a triangle = 1 over 2 × base × height. Use 5 and your answer to (b).
  • That is 5 × 12, the area of a rectangle. A triangle is half of that.
  • That uses the wire (13) as a base. Use the two sides that meet at the right angle: 5 and 12.

1 over 2 × 5 × 12 = 30 m².

  • M1 for using the pole (12 m) and the ground (5 m) as the base and height
  • M1 for 1 over 2 × 5 × 12
  • A1 for 30

Why it works

  • Squares are areas. The first figure shows a square on each side. The two smaller areas add up to the big one: 9 + 16 = 25. That is one example. The proof above shows why it works for every right-angled triangle.
  • Adding vs subtracting. The hypotenuse is the biggest, so it stands alone on one side. Finding it, you add. Finding anything else, you subtract.
  • Square root undoes square. The theorem gives c², and the square root undoes the squaring to give c.

Common traps

  • Adding the lengths instead of the squares.
  • Stopping at c² and forgetting the square root.
  • Adding when you are finding a shorter side.
  • Using Pythagoras on a triangle with no right angle.
  • Rounding too early.

Check your knowledge: on the job

Real jobs from the building trades: plumbing, electrics and carpentry. Prices are typical, so check them with your own supplier.

To check that a corner of a room is square, a fitter marks a point 60 cm along one wall and a point 80 cm along the other wall. For the corner to be a right angle, how far apart, in centimetres, should the two marks be?

  1. Look at the triangle. Which side is the one you are finding, the longest side or a shorter side?
  2. The two marks and the corner make a right-angled triangle. Use a² + b² = c².
  3. Work out 60² + 80², then take the square root.
  • You added the two lengths. Square them, add, then take the square root.
  • You subtracted. The distance between the marks is the longest side, so add the squares.

60² + 80² = 3600 + 6400 = 10 000. The square root of 10 000 is 100, so the marks should be 100 cm apart. (This is a 3-4-5 triangle made 20 times bigger: 60 : 80 : 100.)

A pipe has to run diagonally across a rectangular floor, from one corner to the opposite corner. The floor is 2.4 m long and 1.8 m wide. How long is the diagonal pipe, in metres?

  1. Sketch the floor and draw the diagonal. What shape do you get?
  2. The diagonal is the longest side of a right-angled triangle with sides 2.4 m and 1.8 m.
  3. Work out 2.4² + 1.8² and take the square root.
  • You added 2.4 and 1.8. Square each length first, add, then take the square root.
