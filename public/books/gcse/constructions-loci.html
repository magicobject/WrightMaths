Constructions and loci

A Construction is an accurate drawing made with a ruler, a protractor and compasses. A Locus (loci) is every point that follows one rule, such as “exactly 3 m from the tree”. Learn to draw both, then run it backwards: say the rule from the picture.

Where you will use this: setting out a fence line, a patio or a mitre joint, siting a socket or a CCTV camera so it covers the right area, and reading plans and elevations on any building job.

Words you need

Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

Construction
An accurate drawing made with only a ruler, a protractor and compasses. The construction arcs and lines are kept to show the method.
Locus (loci)
The set of all the points that follow one rule. It can be a line, a curve or a whole region. The plural is loci.
Perpendicular bisector
The line that cuts another line exactly in half, at a right angle. Every point on it is the same distance from the two ends.
Angle bisector
The line that cuts an angle exactly in half. Every point on it is the same distance from the two arms of the angle.

Drawing rules

Use a sharp pencil. Keep your construction arcs and lines: they show your method, so never rub them out.

Exams usually allow a small error, about 2 mm on a length and 2 degrees on an angle. Measure carefully and check each step.

You cannot draw on this screen, so the questions here ask you to work out lengths and angles, put steps in order, or say which construction or region you can see. Do the real drawing on paper with your own equipment.

1. Drawing accurately

A Construction uses only a ruler, a protractor and a pair of compasses. Each tool does one job.

  • Ruler: draws straight lines and measures lengths. Put the 0 mark exactly on the start of the line.
  • Protractor: measures and draws angles. Put the centre hole on the corner and the zero line along one arm.
  • Compasses: draw circles and arcs. Set the radius with the ruler, then do not change it until you have finished that arc.

A protractor has two scales of numbers. Use the scale that starts at 0 on your arm. If it is not obvious which, ask: is the angle acute or obtuse? Then pick the number that fits.

Key idea: before you read a protractor, say whether the angle is acute (less than 90°) or obtuse (more than 90°). If your reading does not match, you are on the wrong scale.

Worked example: reading an angle

The zero line is along the right-hand arm. The other arm points to a mark between 60 and 70, closer to 65. What is the angle?

  1. The zero line is on the right, so use the scale that starts at 0 on the right.
  2. The slanting arm is less than a right angle, so the angle is acute. That fits a reading near 65.
  3. The angle is 65°.

Now you finish it

  2. Find 50 on the scale that starts at 0 on AB, and make a small ___.

2. Constructing triangles

Three pieces of information fix a triangle. There are three common kinds. Letters show which parts you know: S for a side, A for an angle.

SSS: three sides

  1. Draw the longest side with a ruler. Call the ends A and B.
  2. Set the compasses to the second length. With the point on A, draw an arc.
  3. Set the compasses to the third length. With the point on B, draw an arc that crosses the first. Call the crossing C.
  4. Join C to A and to B. Keep the arcs.

SAS: two sides and the angle between them

  1. Draw the first side AB.
  2. Use the protractor at A to mark the angle. Draw a line from A through the mark.
  3. Measure the second side along that line from A, and mark C.
  4. Join C to B.

ASA: one side and the two angles at its ends

  1. Draw the side AB.
  2. At A, mark the first angle and draw a long line through it.
  3. At B, mark the second angle and draw a line to cross the first.
  4. The crossing is C.

Key idea: three lengths only make a triangle if the two shorter sides add up to more than the longest side. If they do not, the two arcs never meet.

Worked example: SSS

Construct triangle ABC with AB = 6 cm, AC = 5 cm and BC = 4 cm.

  1. Draw AB = 6 cm with a ruler.
  2. Compasses to 5 cm, point on A, draw an arc above AB.
  3. Compasses to 4 cm, point on B, draw an arc to cross it. Mark C.
  4. Join AC and BC. Check by measuring: the sides should be 5 cm and 4 cm.

Now you finish it

Construct triangle ABC with AB = 6 cm, angle A = 40° and angle B = 65°.

  1. Draw AB = ___ cm.
  2. At A, draw a line at ___° to AB. At B, draw a line at ___° to BA.
  3. The lines cross at C. The angle at C should be 180 − 40 − 65 = ___°.
Check my answer

AB = 6 cm. The lines are at 40° (at A) and 65° (at B). The angle at C is 180 − 105 = 75°. Measure it to check your drawing.

3. Bisectors and perpendiculars

To bisect something is to cut it exactly in half. Both of these constructions work by making two arcs of the same radius, so every point you mark is the same distance from where you started.

The perpendicular bisector of a line

The Perpendicular bisector of AB cuts AB in half at a right angle.

  1. Open the compasses to more than half the length of AB.
  2. With the point on A, draw an arc above and below AB.
  3. Keep the same radius. With the point on B, draw arcs to cross the first two.
  4. Join the two crossings with a ruler. This line crosses AB at its middle, M.

Key idea: the arcs must use the SAME radius, and it must be more than half of AB, or they will not cross.

The bisector of an angle

The Angle bisector splits an angle into two equal angles.

  1. Put the compass point on the corner O. Draw an arc that cuts both arms.
  2. Put the point on each crossing in turn. With the same radius, draw two arcs that cross inside the angle.
  3. Draw a line from O through where those arcs cross.

The perpendicular from a point to a line

This gives the shortest distance from a point P to a line.

  1. With the point on P, draw an arc that cuts the line in two places.
  2. From each of those two places, with the same radius, draw arcs that cross on the other side of the line.
  3. Draw a line from P through the crossing. It meets the line at a right angle.

To draw a perpendicular at a point ON the line, mark two points the same distance either side of it with compasses. Then bisect the gap between them, as for the perpendicular bisector.

Worked example: bisecting an angle

An angle is 70°. You bisect it. How big is each part?

  1. Bisect means cut exactly in half.
  2. 70 ÷ 2 = 35.
  3. Each part is 35°.

Now you finish it

4. Loci: points that follow a rule

A Locus (loci) (plural: loci) is the set of all the points that follow one rule. It can be a line, a curve or a region.

There are four standard loci to know.

  • 1. A fixed distance from a point: a circle (or the inside of one). Centre the compasses on the point.
  • 2. A fixed distance from a line segment: a track shape. Two straight lines parallel to AB, joined by a half circle at each end.
  • 3. The same distance from two points A and B: the perpendicular bisector of AB.
  • 4. The same distance from two lines that meet at a corner: the bisector of the angle between them.

The same distance from two parallel lines is a line halfway between them.

Key idea: “exactly” gives the line or curve. “Less than” or “within” gives the inside. “More than” gives the outside. Draw the boundary dashed if the points on it do not count.

Worked example: a sprinkler

A sprinkler waters every point within 4 m of it. What is the area watered? Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

  1. Within 4 m of a point is the inside of a circle with radius 4 m.
  2. Area = π × 4² = 50.2655…
  3. The area watered is 50.3 m² to 1 d.p.

5. Regions and scale drawings

A region is an area of points that fit one or more rules. To find it, follow each rule in turn. The region is where every rule is true at the same time.

A Scale drawing shows real lengths smaller, using a scale. With a scale of 1 cm to 2 m, every 1 cm on the paper stands for 2 m in real life. Measure on the drawing, then multiply by the scale to get the real distance. To draw something, divide the real length by the scale.

In this garden, a dashed line is the same distance from A and from B. The solid curve is exactly 4 m from A. Points on A's side of the dashed line are closer to A. Points inside the curve are within 4 m of A.

Key idea: to find a region, test a point. If a point in a region follows ALL the rules, the whole region follows them.

Worked example

Which region is closer to A than to B, and also within 4 m of A?

  1. Closer to A than to B: A's side of the dashed line, so P or R.
  2. Within 4 m of A: inside the curve, so P or Q.
  3. Both rules are true only in P.

6. Plans and elevations

A flat drawing of a 3D shape shows how it looks from one direction. We use three views.

  • The Plan is the view from directly above.
  • The front Elevation is the view from the front. It shows the size across the front and the height.
  • The side elevation is the view from the side (the right-hand side unless the question says otherwise).

Only the edges you can see are drawn. Lengths on the views are the real lengths. Here is a triangular prism with its triangular end at the front.

7. Why the perpendicular bisector works Higher

Draw arcs of the same radius from A and B. They cross at X and Y. Join X to A, X to B, Y to A and Y to B. Every one of those four lines is a radius of the same size, so AX = BX = AY = BY.

Two triangles are Congruent if they are exactly the same shape and size. If all three sides of one match all three sides of the other (SSS), the triangles are congruent. Triangles AXY and BXY have AX = BX, AY = BY, and the side XY in common. So they are congruent.

That makes the angles at X equal, so the line XY bisects the angle AXB. Triangle AXB has two equal sides, so it is isosceles. In an isosceles triangle the bisector of the angle at the top meets the base at its middle at a right angle. So XY bisects AB at 90°.

Key idea: SSS works because three sides fix a triangle. If two triangles have three equal sides, every angle matches too.

Why it works

  • Compasses keep a distance fixed. Every point on an arc is the same distance from its centre. That is the whole idea behind every construction.
  • Equal arcs from two ends give the middle. A point the same distance from A and from B must lie on the line that cuts AB in half at right angles.
  • An angle bisector is a locus too. It is the set of points the same distance from the two arms.
  • A circle is a locus. All the points a fixed distance from the centre. That is why the area of the watered patch is a circle area.
  • Plans and elevations are shadows. Imagine the light shining on a solid from above, the front or the side: the shadow is the view.

Common traps

  • Changing the compass setting part-way through the construction. Keep it fixed for each pair of arcs.
  • Reading the wrong scale on a protractor. Check whether the angle is acute or obtuse.
  • Rubbing out construction arcs. They carry the marks.
  • Using the same end of the base for both arcs in SSS.
  • Leaving out the half circles at the ends of a track-shaped locus.
  • Forgetting the scale: measure on the drawing, then multiply to get the real length.
