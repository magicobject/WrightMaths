    Conditional probability

    Conditional probability is the chance of something happening given that something else has already happened. Learn to use tables, trees and Venn diagrams to find it, to test whether events depend on each other, and then run it backwards: the chance of B given A, and the chance of A given B.

    Where you will use this: the chance a part is faulty when a test says it is, a second pick from a box with no putting back, and any time a result changes the odds.

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Conditional probability
    The probability of an event when you already know another event has happened. P(B | A) is read “the probability of B given A”. The event you already know about is the denominator of the fraction.
    Two-way table
    A table that sorts the same people or things in two ways at once, with rows for one grouping, columns for the other and totals at the ends.
    Venn diagram
    A diagram of overlapping circles in a box. Each circle is a set, the overlap holds what is in both sets, and the space outside the circles holds what is in neither.
    Independent events
    Events where one does not change the chance of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

    Reading P(B | A)

    The bar | is read “given”. P(B | A) means the probability of B, given that A has happened.

    The event after the bar is the one you already know. It is the denominator of the fraction.

    P(A and B) means both happen. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are usually not the same.

    1. “Given that”

    Sometimes you know something before you work out a probability. Knowing it changes which things you count. A two-way table is a good way to see this.

    Class of 60: how students get to school
    WalkBusTotal
    Boys141630
    Girls181230
    Total322860

    If you pick a student at random from all 60, P(walk) = 32 over 60. But if you are told the student is a girl, you only look at the girls. The chance a girl walks is 18 over 30. That is a conditional probability, written P(walk | girl).

    Key idea: P(B | A) = the number who are both A and B ÷ the number who are A. The “given” group, counted in full, is the denominator of the fraction.

    Try it: from the table, P(bus | boy) is the boys who take the bus, out of all the boys. What fraction is that?

    Worked example

    Use the table. Find P(bus | boy) and P(boy | bus).

    1. P(bus | boy): the boys are given, and there are 30 of them. 16 take the bus, so P = 16 over 30 = 8 over 15.
    2. P(boy | bus): the bus riders are given, and there are 28 of them. 16 are boys, so P = 16 over 28 = 4 over 7.
    3. The two answers are different, because the denominators count different groups.

    Now you finish it

    1. The bus riders are given. How many are there? ___
    2. How many of them are girls? ___
    3. P(girl | bus) = ___ over ___, simplified to ___.
    Check my answer

    There are 28 bus riders and 12 are girls, so P(girl | bus) = 12 over 28 = 3 over 7.

    2. Trees without replacement

    Take two counters from a bag without putting the first back. The second pick depends on the first, because the bag has changed. The branches on the second level are conditional probabilities.

    A bag has 5 red (R) and 3 blue (B) counters. The first counter is red with probability 5 over 8. If it is red, the bag now has 4 red and 3 blue, so P(second red | first red) = 4 over 7. Multiply along the branches.

    P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

    Key idea: along a path, multiply the first branch by the second branch, where the second branch is the probability given the first. For “and then” without replacement, the denominator of the second fraction is one smaller.

    Worked example

    Using the tree, find P(both red) and P(at least one blue).

    1. P(RR) = 5 over 8 × 4 over 7 = 20 over 56 = 5 over 14.
    2. “At least one blue” is everything except RR.
    3. P(at least one blue) = 1 − 5 over 14 = 9 over 14.
    4. Check: RB + BR + BB = 15 over 56 + 15 over 56 + 6 over 56 = 36 over 56 = 9 over 14. ✓

    Now you finish it

    Using the same tree, find P(blue then blue).

    1. P(first blue) = ___ over 8.
    2. After one blue, ___ counters are left, ___ of them blue.
    3. P(BB) = ___ × ___ = ___, simplified to ___.
    Check my answer

    3 over 8 × 2 over 7 = 6 over 56 = 3 over 28.

    3. Venn diagrams and the formula

    A Venn diagram shows how groups overlap. Here are 30 students: 18 play football, 12 play tennis and 5 play both (those 5 are counted in the 18 and in the 12). Work out the regions first: football only is 18 − 5 = 13, tennis only is 12 − 5 = 7, and the 5 outside both circles come from 30 − 13 − 5 − 7.

    “Given football” means look only inside the football circle (13 + 5 = 18). Of those, 5 are in the overlap, so P(tennis | football) = 5 over 18.

    The same idea written as a formula:

    P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A)

    Check with the diagram: P(football and tennis) = 5 over 30 and P(football) = 18 over 30, so P(tennis | football) = 5 over 30 ÷ 18 over 30 = 5 over 18.

    Key idea: P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A). It is the same as “the overlap over the given group”. The formula works with counts or with probabilities.

    Worked example

    Use the Venn diagram. Find P(football | tennis) and P(football | not tennis).

    1. Tennis is given: the tennis circle holds 5 + 7 = 12. The overlap is 5, so P(football | tennis) = 5 over 12.
    2. Not tennis is given: outside the tennis circle holds 13 + 5 = 18. Of these, 13 play football, so P(football | not tennis) = 13 over 18.

    4. Independent or dependent?

    Two events are independent if knowing one has happened does not change the chance of the other. Then P(B | A) = P(B). If knowing A does change the chance of B, the events are dependent.

    Parts made by two machines
    FaultyNot faultyTotal
    Machine X10190200
    Machine Y15285300
    Total25475500

    In the table, P(faulty | Machine X) = 10 over 200 = 1 over 20. P(faulty | Machine Y) = 15 over 300 = 1 over 20. And P(faulty) overall = 25 over 500 = 1 over 20. All three are equal, so which machine made the part does not change the chance of a fault: the events are independent.

    Key idea: test for independence by comparing P(B | A) with P(B). If they are equal, the events are independent and P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). If they differ, the events are dependent. Picks with replacement are independent. Picks without replacement are dependent.

    Worked example

    On the day shift 12 of 100 parts are faulty. On the night shift 4 of 100 parts are faulty. Are “faulty” and “day shift” independent?

    1. P(faulty | day) = 12 over 100 = 0.12.
    2. P(faulty) = 16 over 200 = 0.08.
    3. 0.12 is not 0.08, so the events are dependent: the day shift has more faults.

    5. A test that is nearly always right Higher

    Quality checks, and many other kinds of tests, raise a surprising question. If a test says “faulty”, how likely is the part to be faulty?

    Suppose 1000 parts are tested. The test flags a part when it says the part is faulty. 20 of the parts are faulty, and the test flags 18 of them. Of the 980 good parts, the test wrongly flags 49. Counting is easier than fractions here.

    The test is quite good at finding faulty parts: P(flagged | faulty) = 18 over 20 = 0.9. But 18 + 49 = 67 parts are flagged, and only 18 of them are faulty: P(faulty | flagged) = 18 over 67, about 0.27. Most flagged parts are fine, because there are so many more good parts than faulty ones. The same idea matters for any screening test where the thing being looked for is rare.

    Key idea: P(flagged | faulty) and P(faulty | flagged) are different questions with different denominators. Use counts out of a round number, like 1000, to see which group is the denominator.

    Worked example

    A scanner is tried on 1000 sockets. 10 are faulty and the scanner flags 9 of them. It also flags 40 of the 990 good sockets. One of the flagged sockets is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is faulty?

    1. Flagged sockets: 9 + 40 = 49.
    2. Flagged and faulty: 9.
    3. P(faulty | flagged) = 9 over 49, about 0.18.

    Now you finish it

    The same scanner: what is the probability that a faulty socket is flagged?

    2. Flagged faulty sockets: ___.
    3. P(flagged | faulty) = ___ over ___ = ___.
    Check my answer

    9 over 10 = 0.9. Compare with P(faulty | flagged) = 9 over 49: different denominators, very different answers.

    Check your knowledge: when a test says yes

    A test flags faulty parts. It was tried on 1000 parts: 20 faulty and 980 good. Use the tree to find the total number of parts that were flagged.

    1. Flagged parts come from two places on the tree.
    2. Add the flagged faulty parts and the flagged good parts.
    • That is only the flagged faulty parts. Good parts can be flagged too.
    • That is only the flagged good parts. Add the flagged faulty parts as well.

    18 faulty parts and 49 good parts were flagged: 18 + 49 = 67.

    • The test must be broken, because it keeps flagging good parts

    • The two probabilities are always the same

    1. Use the same group: all the flagged parts.
    2. How many of the flagged parts are good?
    • That is the false-alarm rate among good parts. Here the group is all flagged parts.
    • That is the chance a flagged part is faulty. You want the good ones.

    Flip it: the chance of B given A, and of A given B

    The two conditional probabilities are linked by the same overlap, P(A and B), but they have different denominators.

    B given A

    P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A)

    The denominator is the event that is given: A.

    A given B

    P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)

    Now B is given, so the denominator is P(B).

    You can also go backwards through the multiplication rule. If P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A), then P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A), and P(A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B | A).

    Key idea: the same overlap, divided by different groups, gives different answers. Say out loud which event is given, and put its probability or count on the denominator.

    Worked example

    P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6 and P(A and B) = 0.12. Find P(B | A) and P(A | B), and say whether A and B are independent.

    1. P(B | A) = 0.12 ÷ 0.3 = 0.4.
    2. P(A | B) = 0.12 ÷ 0.6 = 0.2.
    3. P(B | A) = 0.4 is not P(B) = 0.6, so A and B are not independent. (Check: 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.18, which is not 0.12.)

    Now you finish it

    P(A) = 0.2, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A and B) = 0.08. Find P(B | A) and P(A | B).

    1. P(B | A) = 0.08 ÷ ___ = ___.
    2. P(A | B) = 0.08 ÷ ___ = ___.
    Check my answer

    P(B | A) = 0.08 ÷ 0.2 = 0.4. P(A | B) = 0.08 ÷ 0.5 = 0.16. They are different, and neither equals P(B) or P(A) respectively, so the events are not independent.

    Check your knowledge: running it backwards

    P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6 and P(A and B) = 0.12. Find P(B | A).

    1. P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A).
    2. Divide 0.12 by 0.3.
    • That is P(A | B), which divides by P(B). For P(B | A) the denominator is P(A).
    • That is P(A and B). You still need to divide by P(A).

    P(B | A) = 0.12 ÷ 0.3 = 0.4.

    P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6 and P(A and B) = 0.12. Find P(A | B).

    1. P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B).
    2. Divide 0.12 by 0.6.
    • That is P(B | A), which divides by P(A). For P(A | B) the denominator is P(B).
    • That adds the two numbers. Divide P(A and B) by P(B).

    P(A | B) = 0.12 ÷ 0.6 = 0.2. This is different from P(B | A) = 0.4.

    P(A) = 0.5 and P(B | A) = 0.4. Find P(A and B).

    1. Rearrange: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A).
    2. Multiply 0.5 by 0.4.
    • That adds. P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A).
    • That divides 0.4 by 0.5. To find P(A and B), multiply.

    P(A and B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2.

    P(B | A) = 0.4 and P(A and B) = 0.2. Find P(A).

    1. P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A).
    2. To undo × 0.4, divide 0.2 by 0.4.
    • That multiplies. You need to find P(A) from P(A and B) = P(A) × 0.4, so divide.
    • That is P(A and B). Divide it by P(B | A) = 0.4.

    0.2 = P(A) × 0.4, so P(A) = 0.2 ÷ 0.4 = 0.5.

    In a class of girls and boys, P(walk | girl) = 3 over 5 and there are 30 girls. How many girls walk to school?

    1. 3 over 5 of the girls walk.
    2. Find 3 over 5 of 30.
    • That divides 30 by 3 over 5. The probability is the fraction of girls who walk, so multiply.
    • That is 1 over 5 of 30. You need three fifths, so multiply 6 by 3.

    P(walk | girl) is the fraction of the girls who walk, so 3 over 5 × 30 = 18 girls.

    A bag has 6 counters, some red and the rest blue. Two are taken without replacement and P(both red) = 1 over 5. How many red counters are in the bag?

    1. If r counters are red, P(both red) = r over 6 × (r − 1) over 5.
    2. Try small numbers of red counters, starting from 2. Which one makes 1 over 5?
    • Test it: 2 over 6 × 1 over 5 = 2 over 30, which is 1 over 15, not 1 over 5.
    • Test it: 4 over 6 × 3 over 5 = 12 over 30, which is 2 over 5, not 1 over 5.

    Try r = 3: 3 over 6 × 2 over 5 = 6 over 30 = 1 over 5. ✓ So there are 3 red counters. (r = 2 gives 1 over 15 and r = 4 gives 12 over 30 = 2 over 5.)

    Spot the mistake

    Finding an error in someone else’s working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

    Sam says: in the class table, P(boy | bus) = 16 over 60, because there are 60 students. What went wrong?

    • The denominator should be the 28 students on the bus, not all 60
    • Nothing is wrong, because you always divide by the whole class of 60 students.
    • The numerator should be 28
    1. What does “given bus” tell you about which students to look at?
    2. The denominator of the fraction is the given group.

    You are told the student rides the bus, so look only at the 28 bus riders. 16 of them are boys: P(boy | bus) = 16 over 28 = 4 over 7.

    Mia says: a bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two counters are taken at random without replacement, so P(both red) = 5 over 8 × 5 over 8. What went wrong?

    • After one red is out, the second pick is 4/7, not 5/8
    • Nothing is wrong, because 5/8 is the chance of red every single time.
    • She should have added the two probabilities instead of multiplying them
    1. Is the second counter taken from the same bag as the first?
    2. How many counters are left, and how many of them are red?

    Without replacement, the second pick is from 7 counters with 4 red. P(both red) = 5 over 8 × 4 over 7 = 5 over 14.

    Kit says: the test flags 90% of faulty parts, so if a part is flagged there is a 90% chance it is faulty. What went wrong?

    • The two probabilities have different denominators, so they are usually not equal
    • Nothing is wrong. If 90% of faulty parts are flagged then 90% of flagged parts must be faulty.
    • He should have used 10% instead, because 100% − 90% = 10%
    1. Which group is the denominator of P(flagged | faulty)? Which is the denominator of P(faulty | flagged)?
    2. Think about the flagged good parts as well.

    90% is P(flagged | faulty): the share of faulty parts that are flagged. P(faulty | flagged) is the share of flagged parts that are faulty. It depends on how many good parts are also flagged, so it can be much lower.

    Higher [3 marks] A test is tried on 1000 parts. 40 are faulty and 960 are good. The test flags 36 of the faulty parts and 48 of the good parts. A flagged part is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is faulty? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/10.

    Why it works

    • “Given” shrinks the world. Once you know A has happened, only the outcomes inside A are possible. So A becomes the whole, and the denominator of the fraction.
    • The formula is the table in disguise. P(A and B) ÷ P(A) = (both ÷ total) ÷ (A ÷ total). The totals cancel, leaving both ÷ A, which is the overlap over the given group.
    • Branches multiply because each is a share of a share. A share 4 over 7 of the 5 over 8 that were red is the share 5 over 8 × 4 over 7 of all outcomes.
    • Without replacement means dependent. The first pick changes the bag, so the second branches are not the same as the first.
    • Independence is symmetric. If knowing A does not change B, then knowing B does not change A. That is why P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) works both ways.
    • The two directions differ. P(B | A) and P(A | B) have the same numerator but different denominators, so they are usually not equal.

    Common traps

    • Dividing by the whole total instead of the given group.
    • Mixing up P(B | A) and P(A | B).
    • Using the same denominator on the second branch when nothing is put back.
    • Leaving the overlap out of the given circle on a Venn diagram.
    • Saying events are independent because there are items in both groups, without comparing P(B | A) with P(B).
    • Assuming a test that finds 90% of faulty parts means 90% of flagged parts are faulty.
    • Forgetting to simplify a fraction when the question asks for its simplest form.

    Higher [3 marks] Events A and B are such that P(B | A) = P(B). Show that P(A | B) = P(A). (This shows that if B does not depend on A, then A does not depend on B.) Assume P(B) is not 0.
