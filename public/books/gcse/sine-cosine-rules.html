    Sine and cosine rules

    Right-angled trigonometry only works when there is a right angle. The sine rule and the cosine rule work in any triangle. They let you find a missing side or angle, and the area from two sides and the angle between them.

    Where you will use this: roof trusses that are not right-angled, a surveyor measuring across a river or a pond, the area of a triangular plot, and a ship or a walker following bearings.

    Sine rule
    For any triangle: a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C. Use it when you know a side and the angle opposite it.
    Cosine rule
    For any triangle: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A. Use it when you know all three sides, or two sides and the angle between them.
    Included angle
    The angle between two given sides of a triangle.
    Ambiguous case
    When the sine rule finds an angle there can be two answers, θ and 180° − θ, because they have the same sine.

    1. Labelling a triangle

    The sine rule and the cosine rule need you to name every part in the same way. In triangle ABC:

    • The corners (angles) are the capital letters A, B and C.
    • The sides are the small letters a, b and c.
    • Side a is opposite angle A. Side b is opposite angle B. Side c is opposite angle C.

    Key idea: a side and the angle opposite it are a matching pair: a with A, b with B, c with C. The sine rule is all about matching pairs.

    When a question gives the corners other letters, such as P, Q and R, the same idea holds. The side opposite P is the side that does not touch P.

    Also remember that the three angles of any triangle add up to 180°. If you know two angles, you know the third.

    3. The sine rule: finding a side

    In any triangle, a side divided by the sine of its opposite angle is the same number for all three sides.

    Sine rule: a over sin A = b over sin B = c over sin C

    You never use all three parts at once. You use two of them: the matching pair you know, and the pair that has your unknown.

    1. Write down the matching pair you know, such as a and A.
    2. Write down the pair with your unknown, such as b and B.
    3. Put them equal. To find a side, put the unknown side on top, so you get a plain multiply and divide.

    Key idea: bigger angles face bigger sides, so a side and the sine of its angle grow together. The rule just says that growth is in the same proportion all the way round the triangle.

    Worked example: two angles and a side

    In triangle ABC, angle A = 40°, angle B = 65° and side a = 12 cm. Find side b to 1 decimal place.

    1. a and A are a matching pair. b goes with B.
    2. b over sin 65° = 12 over sin 40°.
    3. b = 12 × sin 65° ÷ sin 40° = 12 × 0.9063… ÷ 0.6427… = 16.91… ≈ 16.9 cm.

    When you are given two angles, find the third

    If the unknown side's angle is not given, first use the fact that angles in a triangle add up to 180°. Then use the rule.

    Worked example: find the third angle first

    Angle A = 35°, angle B = 82° and side a = 9.4 cm. Find side c to 1 decimal place.

    1. Angle C = 180° − 35° − 82° = 63°.
    2. c over sin 63° = 9.4 over sin 35°.
    3. c = 9.4 × sin 63° ÷ sin 35° = 14.6021… ≈ 14.6 cm.

    4. The sine rule: finding an angle

    To find an angle, turn the sine rule upside down so the unknown angle is on top.

    sin A over a = sin B over b = sin C over c

    1. Write the pair you know and the pair with your unknown angle.
    2. Rearrange so that sin of the unknown angle is on its own.
    3. Press sin−1 as the very last step to turn the sine back into an angle.

    Worked example

    In triangle ABC, a = 9 cm opposite A = 50°, and b = 7 cm. Find angle B to 1 decimal place.

    1. sin B over 7 = sin 50° over 9.
    2. sin B = 7 × sin 50° ÷ 9 = 0.5958….
    3. B = sin−1(0.5958…) = 36.5705… ≈ 36.6°.

    Now you finish it

    In triangle ABC, a = 12 cm opposite A = 64°, and b = 10 cm. Find angle B to 1 decimal place.

    1. sin B over ___ = sin ___° over ___.
    2. sin B = ___ × sin ___° ÷ ___ = ___.
    3. B = sin−1(___) = ___°.
    Check my answer

    sin B = 10 × sin 64° ÷ 12 = 0.7489…, so B = sin−1(0.7489…) = 48.5033… ≈ 48.5°.

    A word of warning: two angles can share a sine

    Two different angles can have the same sine. For example sin 30° = 0.5 and sin 150° = 0.5. Your calculator only ever shows the smaller one. If the diagram shows an angle that is bigger than 90°, take your answer away from 180°. In the questions here, the side lengths and the picture make it clear. This is called the ambiguous case. If you want to avoid it, the cosine rule never has this problem.

    Key idea: the sine rule finds an angle from a matching pair. The last step is always the inverse button. Check the answer is sensible: the biggest side faces the biggest angle.

    5. The cosine rule: finding a side

    Use the cosine rule when you know two sides and the angle between them (the included angle), and you want the third side.

    a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A

    The side on the left, a, is opposite the angle A. The two sides b and c are the ones that make up angle A.

    Why it looks like Pythagoras

    If A = 90°, then cos 90° = 0, so the last part disappears and you get a² = b² + c². The − 2bc cos A part is a correction for when the angle is not 90°.

    Typing it into the calculator

    1. Work out a² first, and write it down. Do 2 × b × c × cos A as one multiplication, then take it away from b² + c².
    2. Take the square root last. Do not round before this.
    3. If A is bigger than 90°, cos A is negative. Taking away a negative number adds, so a² gets bigger. That is correct.

    Worked example

    In triangle ABC, b = 7 cm, c = 9 cm and A = 52°. Find side a to 1 decimal place.

    1. The angle 52° is between b and c. Use the cosine rule.
    2. a² = 7² + 9² − 2 × 7 × 9 × cos 52° = 49 + 81 − 126 × 0.6156… = 52.4266….
    3. a = √52.4266… = 7.2406… ≈ 7.2 cm.

    Now you finish it

    In triangle ABC, b = 8 cm, c = 6 cm and A = 60°. Find side a to 1 decimal place.

    1. a² = 8² + 6² − 2 × 8 × 6 × cos ___°.
    2. cos 60° = 0.5, so a² = ___ + ___ − ___ = ___.
    3. a = √___ = ___ cm.
    Check my answer

    6. The cosine rule: finding an angle

    If you know all three sides, rearrange the cosine rule to get an angle. Put the side opposite the angle you want as a.

    cos A = b² + c² − a² over 2bc

    1. Decide which angle you want. Call the side opposite it a.
    2. Work out the top: b² + c² − a². Work out the bottom: 2 × b × c.
    3. Divide, then press cos−1 as the last step.

    On the calculator, use brackets: ( b² + c² − a² ) ÷ ( 2 × b × c ). Without brackets the calculator only divides the last term.

    Key idea: a negative answer for cos A is not a mistake. It means the angle is obtuse (bigger than 90°). The longest side is always opposite the biggest angle.

    7. Area of a triangle: 1 over 2ab sin C

    You know that area = 1 over 2 × base × height. But often you do not know the height. You can use two sides and the angle between them instead.

    Area = 1 over 2 × a × b × sin C

    The angle C must be the included angle: the one between the two sides a and b.

    Why it works

    In the right-angled triangle on the right, sin C = h over b, so the height is h = b sin C. Then the area is 1 over 2 × a × h = 1 over 2 × a × b sin C.

    Worked example

    2. Area = 1 over 2 × 9 × 11 × sin 40° = 49.5 × 0.6427….
    3. Area = 31.8179… ≈ 31.8 cm².

    8. Mixed problems and bearings

    Real problems rarely say which rule to use. Draw the triangle, label what you know, and choose. Sometimes you need two steps: for example a side from the sine rule, and then a height from right-angled trig.

    Joining the rules together

    • If a triangle has a right angle, SOH CAH TOA and Pythagoras are the quick way. Use the new rules for the triangle that has none.
    • A shape with four sides can be cut into two triangles by a diagonal. Find the diagonal in one triangle, then use it in the other.
    • Keep every digit on the calculator between steps. Round only the final answer.

    Bearings

    A Bearing is a direction, measured as an angle clockwise from north. Always write it with three figures: north is 000°, east is 090°, south is 180° and west is 270°. A bearing of 040° is written 040°, not 40°. (There is more on bearings on the Bearings page.)

    1. Draw a north line at every point where the direction changes.
    2. Mark each bearing as an angle going clockwise from the north line.
    3. To find an angle inside the triangle, remember that all the north lines are parallel. The back bearing (the way back) is the bearing plus or minus 180°.

    Worked example: two legs of a voyage

    A ship sails 8 km from P on a bearing of 040° to Q. It then sails 11 km on a bearing of 115° to R. Find the distance PR to 1 decimal place.

    1. At Q, the bearing back to P is 040° + 180° = 220°. The bearing on to R is 115°.
    2. Angle PQR = 220° − 115° = 105°. Two sides (8 and 11) and the angle between them: cosine rule.
    3. PR² = 8² + 11² − 2 × 8 × 11 × cos 105° = 185 − 176 × (−0.2588…) = 230.5521….
    4. PR = √230.5521… = 15.1839… ≈ 15.2 km.

    Key idea: draw a north line at each point and the bearings turn into angles inside the triangle. The angle between two legs of a journey is found from a back bearing.

    Check your knowledge: on the job

    Real jobs from the building trades: electrics, plumbing and carpentry. Prices are typical, so check them with your own supplier.

    An electrician is fitting sockets. The consumer unit (the fuse box) is at A. Socket B is 6.4 m from A and socket C is 5.1 m from A. The angle BAC is 112°. She runs twin and earth cable in a straight line from B to C, at £2.40 per metre. Work out the length BC to 2 decimal places, then the cost of cable for that length, in pounds. Type the cost like 42.75.

    1. BC is opposite the angle at A, and the two known lengths are on either side of A. Which rule?
    2. a² = 5.1² + 6.4² − 2 × 5.1 × 6.4 × cos 112°. Find a to 2 decimal places.
    3. Cost = length × 2.40. Use the length to 2 decimal places.
    • That is the length BC. The question asks for the cost, so multiply by the price per metre.

    a² = 5.1² + 6.4² − 2 × 5.1 × 6.4 × cos 112° = 91.4243…, so BC = √91.4243… = 9.5616… ≈ 9.56 m. Cost = 9.56 × 2.40 = £22.94. (Using the unrounded length gives £22.95, which is also accepted.)

    A plumber runs a heating pipe from the boiler B to a radiator at A (3.8 m), then on to the cylinder C (2.9 m). The angle BAC at the radiator is 118°. A direct pipe from B to C would be shorter. How much shorter is the direct pipe than the route through A? Give your answer in metres, to 1 decimal place.

    2. The route through A is 3.8 + 2.9 = 6.7 m. The direct pipe is BC.
    3. Take BC away from 6.7.

    • That is the length of the route through the radiator. Find the direct length and take it away.

    BC² = 3.8² + 2.9² − 2 × 3.8 × 2.9 × cos 118° = 33.1971…, so BC = 5.7616… m. The route through A is 3.8 + 2.9 = 6.7 m. The difference is 6.7 − 5.7616… = 0.9383… ≈ 0.9 m.

    • That looks like + 2bc cos A instead of −. The rule has a minus. (If cos is negative, the minus makes it add: that is the rule working.)
    • That looks like the calculator is in radian mode. Look for D or DEG on the screen and check that sin 30 gives 0.5.

    Cosine rule: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A = 3.6² + 4.2² − 2 × 3.6 × 4.2 × cos 98° = 34.8085…. a = √34.8085… = 5.8998… ≈ 5.90 m.

    2. Area = 1 over 2 × 6.1 × 5.2 × sin 74°. Round it to 2 decimal places.

    A farmer fences a triangular plot. Two sides are 38 m and 52 m, and the angle between them is 75°. Find the length of the third side to 1 decimal place. Then find the cost of fencing all the way round at £24.50 per metre, to the nearest pound. Type the cost as a whole number, like 2500.

    2. a² = 38² + 52² − 2 × 38 × 52 × cos 75°. Then add all three sides to get the perimeter.

    [3 marks] In triangle ABC, AC = 6.2 cm, AB = 7.5 cm and angle BAC = 56°. Work out the length of BC. Give your answer in centimetres, to 3 significant figures.

    1. BC is opposite the angle at A, and the sides either side of A are known.

    [4 marks] A surveyor measures a baseline AB = 250 m. The angle at A to a point C across a lake is 58°, and the angle at B is 67°. Work out the distance AC. Give your answer in metres, to 3 significant figures.

    1. You know two angles and a side. Find the third angle first.
    2. AC is opposite angle B = 67°. AB = 250 is opposite angle C.
    3. AC over sin 67° = 250 over sin C.
    • That is the distance BC, opposite angle A. AC is opposite B = 67°.
    • The sine rule is upside down here. The unknown side goes on top.

    Angle C = 180° − 58° − 67° = 55°. AC over sin 67° = 250 over sin 55°, so AC = 250 × sin 67° ÷ sin 55° = 280.93… ≈ 281 m to 3 significant figures.

    • M1 for angle C = 180° − 58° − 67° = 55°
    • M1 for AC over sin 67° = 250 over sin 55°
    • M1 for AC = 250 × sin 67° ÷ sin 55°
    • A1 for 281 m (3 significant figures)

    Why it works

    • The sine rule keeps a steady ratio. In every triangle, a side divided by the sine of its opposite angle is the same for all three sides. (That shared value is the diameter of the circle that passes through all three corners.) A bigger angle faces a bigger side, and the sine measures how much.
    • The cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction. If the angle is 90°, cos A = 0 and the correction disappears. If the angle is less than 90°, the correction takes away a bit. If it is more, cos A is negative and the correction adds a bit.
    • The area formula is half base times height. The height is b sin C, so 1 over 2 × a × b sin C is the same thing in disguise.
    • Back bearings work because north lines are parallel. The bearing there and the bearing back differ by a half turn, 180°.

    Common traps

    • Calculator in radians instead of degrees.
    • Using the sine rule when there is no matching pair, or the cosine rule when there is.
    • Putting the unknown on the bottom of the sine rule, or forgetting the inverse button when finding an angle.
    • In the cosine rule, multiplying the whole bracket by cos A, or forgetting the square root at the end.
    • Using an angle that is not between the two sides in the area formula.
    • Rounding in the middle of a two-step problem.
    • Giving a bearing without three figures, or using the bearing itself as the angle in the triangle instead of the back bearing.

    Stretch: beyond GCSE. In triangle ABC, A = 40°, a = 8 cm and b = 11 cm. The sine rule gives sin B = 11 sin 40° over 8. There are two different triangles that fit these measurements, one with an acute B and one with an obtuse B. Find the larger possible value of B, in degrees, to 1 decimal place.

    1. Work out sin B first. The calculator gives the acute angle, B1.
    2. Another angle has the same sine: 180° minus the first.
    3. Check that 40° plus your larger B is less than 180°. If it is, the second triangle really exists.
    • That is the acute angle, which is the smaller of the two. The other angle with the same sine is 180° minus it.

    sin B = 11 × sin 40° ÷ 8 = 0.8838…. The calculator gives B = 62.1082…°. Another angle with the same sine is 180° − 62.1082…° = 117.8917… ≈ 117.9°. Both fit, because 40° + 117.9° = 157.9° is less than 180°. This is the ambiguous case: the short side a = 8 can swing to two places.

    [2 marks] Show that when angle A = 90°, the cosine rule a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A becomes Pythagoras’ theorem.

    1. What is cos 90°? Try it on your calculator.
    2. Put that into the rule and see which term disappears.

    cos 90° = 0, so 2bc cos 90° = 2bc × 0 = 0. Then a² = b² + c² − 0 = b² + c², which is Pythagoras’ theorem with a as the hypotenuse.

    • M1 for cos 90° = 0, so 2bc cos A = 0
    • C1 for a² = b² + c² − 0 = b² + c², which is Pythagoras

    [3 marks] Non-calculator A triangle has sides 3 cm, 5 cm and 7 cm. Show that its largest angle is exactly 120°.

    1. The largest angle faces the longest side. Which angle is that?
    2. Use cos C = a² + b² − c² over 2ab with a = 3, b = 5, c = 7.
    3. Work out the fraction. Which angle has that cosine? You may know cos 60° = 1 over 2.

    The largest angle is C, opposite 7 cm. cos C = 3² + 5² − 7² over 2 × 3 × 5 = 9 + 25 − 49 over 30 = −15 over 30 = −1 over 2. The angle with cosine −1 over 2 is 120° (cos 60° = 1 over 2, and cos 120° = −cos 60°). So C = 120° exactly.

    • M1 for cos C = 3² + 5² − 7² over 2 × 3 × 5
    • M1 for 9 + 25 − 49 over 30 = −15 over 30 = −1 over 2
    • C1 for C = 120°, because cos 120° = −1 over 2

    Stretch: beyond GCSE. A triangle has sides of 6 cm and 9 cm and an area of 20 cm². The angle between the two sides could be acute or obtuse. Find the larger possible value of this angle, in degrees, to 1 decimal place.

    2. sin C = 20 ÷ 27. The calculator gives the acute angle.
    3. The other angle with the same sine is 180° minus the acute one.
