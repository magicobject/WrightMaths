    Circles

    A circle has a distance round it (the circumference) and a space inside it (the area). Both come from the radius and the number π. Learn the two formulas, use them on half and quarter circles, then run them backwards to find the radius.

    Where you will use this: the length of tape round a pipe, the cable on a drum, the top of a round table, and the cross-section of a pipe.

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Radius
    The distance from the centre of a circle to its edge. The plural is radii.
    Diameter
    A straight line across a circle that passes through the centre. Its length is twice the radius.
    Circumference
    The distance all the way round the edge of a circle. It is the perimeter of a circle.
    Pi (π)
    The number you get when you divide the circumference of any circle by its diameter. It is about 3.14159, and its decimal digits never end or repeat.

    Units and rounding

    π is about 3.14159. Use the π button on your calculator, not 3.14, unless the question says so.

    Circumference is a length (cm, m). Area is in square units (cm², m²). Volume is in cubic units (cm³, m³).

    Do not round until the last step. Then give the number of decimal places the question asks for. Money is 2 decimal places.

    1. The parts of a circle

    A Circle is a round shape where every point on the edge is the same distance from the centre. Here are the words you need.

    • The centre is the middle point.
    • The Radius goes from the centre to the edge.
    • The Diameter goes across the circle through the centre. It is twice as long as the radius.
    • The Circumference is the distance all the way round the edge. It is the perimeter of a circle.
    • A Chord is a straight line joining two points on the edge. The diameter is the longest chord.
    • An Arc is part of the circumference. A Semicircle is half a circle.

    diameter = 2 × radius    radius = diameter ÷ 2

    Key idea: radius and diameter are easy to swap by accident. Before you use any circle formula, ask yourself: is the number I was given the radius or the diameter?

    Worked example

    A bike wheel has a diameter of 70 cm. What is its radius?

    1. The radius is half of the diameter.
    2. 70 ÷ 2 = 35.
    3. The radius is 35 cm.

    Now you finish it

    A pipe has a radius of 1.5 cm. What is its diameter?

    1. The diameter is twice the radius.
    2. 2 × ___ = ___ cm.
    Check my answer

    2 × 1.5 = 3, so the diameter is 3 cm.

    2. Circumference

    Measure the circumference of any circle, then divide it by the diameter. You always get the same answer: a little more than 3. That number is called pi and is written π.

    π is about 3.14159. The digits never end and never repeat, so π is Irrational number. Use the π button on your calculator. Only use 3.14 if the question tells you to.

    circumference C = π × d    or    C = 2 × π × r

    The two formulas are the same, because d = 2r. Use the first when you have the diameter and the second when you have the radius.

    Key idea: a quick check: the circumference is a bit more than 3 times the diameter. If a circle has a diameter of 10 cm and your circumference is 6 cm or 60 cm, something has gone wrong.

    For a calculation like this, give your answer to 1 decimal place unless the question says something else. Do not round until the very end.

    Worked example: diameter given

    A circle has a diameter of 10 cm. Find its circumference to 1 decimal place.

    1. Formula: C = π × d.
    2. Substitute: C = π × 10 = 31.4159…
    3. To 1 d.p., C = 31.4 cm.
    4. Check: a bit more than 3 × 10 = 30. ✓

    Worked example: radius given

    A circle has a radius of 4.5 cm. Find its circumference to 1 decimal place.

    1. Formula: C = 2 × π × r.
    2. Substitute: C = 2 × π × 4.5 = 28.2743…
    3. To 1 d.p., C = 28.3 cm.

    Now you finish it

    3. Area of a circle

    The area of a circle depends on the radius. Square the radius first, then multiply by π.

    area A = π × r²    where r² means r × r

    Key idea: area uses the RADIUS squared. If you are given the diameter, halve it first. And square only the r, not the π: work out r² first, then multiply by π.

    The units of an area are square units, such as cm² or m².

    Worked example: radius given

    A circle has a radius of 6 cm. Find its area to 1 decimal place.

    1. Formula: A = π × r².
    2. Substitute: A = π × 6² = π × 36 = 113.0973…
    3. To 1 d.p., A = 113.1 cm².

    Worked example: diameter given

    A circle has a diameter of 14 cm. Find its area to 1 decimal place.

    1. The radius is half of the diameter: 14 ÷ 2 = 7.
    2. A = π × 7² = π × 49 = 153.9380…
    3. To 1 d.p., A = 153.9 cm².

    Now you finish it

    A circle has a radius of 3 cm. Find its area to 1 decimal place.

    1. A = π × r² = π × ___² = π × ___.
    2. On the calculator, A = ___ cm² to 1 d.p.
    Check my answer

    3² = 9, and π × 9 = 28.2743…, which is 28.3 cm² to 1 d.p.

    Check your knowledge: area of a circle

    A circle has a radius of 5 cm. Work out its area to 1 decimal place. Answer in cm².

    1. The formula is A = π × r².
    2. Work out 5² = 25 first, then multiply by π.
    • That is the circumference (2 × π × 5). Area uses π × r².
    • That squares (π × 5). Square the 5 first, then multiply by π.

    A = π × 5² = π × 25 = 78.5398… ≈ 78.5 cm².

    • That is the circumference. Area uses π × r².

    Radius = 16 ÷ 2 = 8. A = π × 8² = π × 64 = 201.0619… ≈ 201.1 cm².

    1. Area uses square units, so one of the formulas has a square in it.
    2. Which one squares r?

    The area of a circle is π × r². The other two formulas give the circumference.

    4. Semicircles and quarter circles

    A Semicircle is half a circle. A quarter circle is a quarter of one. Find the value for the whole circle first, then take that fraction of it.

    • Area: semicircle = 1 over 2 × π × r². Quarter circle = 1 over 4 × π × r².
    • Curved edge: semicircle = 1 over 2 × π × d. Quarter circle = 1 over 4 × 2 × π × r.

    Key idea: the PERIMETER of a semicircle or a quarter circle also includes the straight edges. A semicircle has one straight edge, equal to the diameter. A quarter circle has two straight edges, each equal to the radius.

    Worked example: a semicircle

    A semicircle has a diameter of 10 cm. Find its area and its perimeter to 1 decimal place.

    1. Radius = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm.
    2. Area = 1 over 2 × π × 5² = 1 over 2 × π × 25 = 39.2699… ≈ 39.3 cm².
    3. Curved edge = 1 over 2 × π × 10 = 15.7080… cm.
    4. Perimeter = curved edge + straight edge = 15.7080… + 10 = 25.7080… ≈ 25.7 cm.

    Now you finish it

    A quarter circle has a radius of 4 cm. Find its area to 1 decimal place.

    1. A = 1 over 4 × π × 4² = 1 over 4 × π × ___.
    2. On the calculator, A = ___ cm² to 1 d.p.
    Check my answer

    1 over 4 × π × 16 = 12.5664…, which is 12.6 cm² to 1 d.p.

    A semicircle has a radius r. What is its perimeter?

    • πr, the curved edge only
    • 1 over 2πr², the area of a semicircle
    • πr + 2r
    1. The perimeter has a curved edge and a straight edge.
    2. The curve is half of 2πr. The straight edge is the diameter.

    The curved edge is half of 2πr, which is πr. The straight edge is the diameter, 2r. So the perimeter is πr + 2r.

    Circles inside other shapes

    Real shapes mix circles with rectangles. Find each part, then add or subtract.

    • Rectangle with a semicircle on one end: add the areas. For the perimeter, leave out the edge where the shapes join.
    • A ring (a circle with a circle taken out): area = big circle − small circle = πR² − πr², where R is the radius of the big circle and r is the radius of the small circle. This is the cross-section of a pipe.

    Careful with a ring: work out the two circle areas and subtract. Do not square the difference of the radii.

    Worked example: a pipe

    A pipe has an outer radius of 3 cm and an inner radius of 2 cm. Find the area of its cross-section (the metal) to 1 decimal place.

    1. Outer circle = π × 3² = 28.2743… cm².
    2. Inner circle (the hole) = π × 2² = 12.5664… cm².
    3. Metal = 28.2743… − 12.5664… = 15.7080… ≈ 15.7 cm².

    Check your knowledge: circles inside other shapes

    2. Rectangle = width × height. Semicircle = 1 over 2 × π × r², with r half of the width.

    • That adds a whole circle. Only half a circle sits on top.

    A circular lawn has a radius of 5 m. In the middle is a circular pond with a radius of 1 m. How much grass is there? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in m².

    Flip it: run it backwards

    So far you know the radius or diameter and you find the circumference or area. Now go the other way: you know the circumference or area, and you find the radius or diameter.

    Forwards

    diameter d = 10 cm, circumference C = ?

    Multiply: C = π × 10 = 31.4.

    Backwards

    circumference C = 31.4 cm, diameter d = ?

    Divide: d = 31.4 ÷ π = 10.

    • From the circumference: d = C ÷ π and r = C ÷ (2π), or halve the diameter.
    • From the area: r² = A ÷ π, then take the square root to get r. Square roots undo squares.

    Key idea: when you work backwards from an area, you get r² first. The last step is the square root. Then check by going forwards.

    Worked example: from circumference

    A circle has a circumference of 40 cm. Find its diameter and radius to 1 decimal place.

    1. C = π × d, so d = C ÷ π.
    2. d = 40 ÷ π = 12.7324… ≈ 12.7 cm.
    3. The radius is half of the diameter: 12.7324… ÷ 2 = 6.3662… ≈ 6.4 cm.

    Worked example: from area

    A circle has an area of 100 cm². Find its radius to 1 decimal place.

    1. A = π × r², so r² = A ÷ π.
    2. r² = 100 ÷ π = 31.8310…
    3. Now take the square root: r = √31.8310… = 5.6419… ≈ 5.6 cm.
    4. Check forwards: π × 5.6² is about 98.5, close to 100. ✓

    Now you finish it

    A circle has a circumference of 62.8 cm. Find its diameter to 1 decimal place.

    2. On the calculator, d = ___ cm to 1 d.p.

    C = π × d, and π never changes. So if C is doubled, d is doubled too.

    5. Arc length and sector area Higher

    A Sector is a slice of a circle, like a slice of pizza. An Arc is the curved edge of the slice. If the angle at the centre is θ degrees, then the slice is θ over 360 of the whole circle.

    arc length = θ over 360 × 2πr    sector area = θ over 360 × πr²

    Key idea: find the fraction of the circle first, then multiply it by the full circumference (for the arc) or the full area (for the sector).

    Worked example

    A sector has an angle of 60° and a radius of 3 cm. Find its arc length and area to 1 decimal place.

    1. Fraction of the circle: 60 over 360 = 1 over 6.
    2. Arc = 1 over 6 × 2 × π × 3 = 1 over 6 × 18.8496… = 3.1416… ≈ 3.1 cm.
    3. Area = 1 over 6 × π × 3² = 1 over 6 × 28.2743… = 4.7124… ≈ 4.7 cm².

    Now you finish it

    A sector has an angle of 90° and a radius of 5 cm. Find its arc length to 1 decimal place.

    1. Fraction: 90 over 360 = 1 over ___.
    2. Arc = ___ × 2 × π × 5 = ___ cm.
    Check my answer

    90 over 360 = 1 over 4. Arc = 1 over 4 × 2 × π × 5 = 1 over 4 × 31.4159… = 7.8540… ≈ 7.9 cm.

    Check your knowledge: arcs and sectors Higher

    A sector has an angle of 90° and a radius of 8 cm. Work out its arc length. Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in cm.

    1. What fraction of a full circle is 90°?
    2. Arc = that fraction × the full circumference, 2πr.
    • That is the whole circle (circumference or area both give 50.3). Take a quarter.

    90 over 360 = 1 over 4. Full circumference = 2 × π × 8 = 50.2655…. Arc = 1 over 4 × 50.2655… = 12.5664… ≈ 12.6 cm.

    What is the area of this sector? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in cm².

    1. What fraction of a full circle is 120°?
    2. Sector area = that fraction × πr².
    • That is the whole circle. The sector is only a third of it.
    • That is the arc length. The sector area uses πr².

    120 over 360 = 1 over 3. Full circle = π × 9² = 254.4690…. Sector = 1 over 3 × 254.4690… = 84.8230… ≈ 84.8 cm².

    A sector has an angle of 45° and a radius of 10 cm. Work out its arc length to 1 decimal place, in cm.

    1. 45° out of 360° is what fraction?
    2. Multiply that fraction by 2πr.
    • That is the whole circumference. The arc is only an eighth of it.
    • That is the sector area. The arc length uses the circumference.

    45 over 360 = 1 over 8. Arc = 1 over 8 × 2 × π × 10 = 1 over 8 × 62.8319… = 7.8540… ≈ 7.9 cm.

    A sector has an angle of 72° and a radius of 5 cm. Work out its area to 1 decimal place, in cm².

    1. 72° out of 360° simplifies to a unit fraction.
    2. Multiply it by πr².
    • That is the whole circle. The sector is only part of it.
    • That is the arc length. The sector area uses πr².

    72 over 360 = 1 over 5. Area = 1 over 5 × π × 5² = 1 over 5 × 78.5398… = 15.7080… ≈ 15.7 cm².

    An arc has a length of 10 cm and comes from a circle of radius 12 cm. What is the angle at the centre? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in degrees.

    1. The arc is a fraction of the full circumference 2πr. Find that fraction first.
    2. Then the angle is that fraction of 360°.
    • That is the fraction of the circle. Multiply it by 360 to get the angle.
    • That multiplies by 180. A full circle is 360°.

    Circumference = 2 × π × 12 = 75.3982…. Fraction = 10 ÷ 75.3982… = 0.1326…. Angle = 0.1326… × 360 = 47.7465… ≈ 47.7°.

    6. Answers in terms of π

    Sometimes the exam asks for an exact answer “in terms of π”. Do not use the π button. Leave π as a letter and write it after the number.

    The answer is exact, because rounding π would make it a little wrong. A circle of radius 5 cm has a circumference of 2 × π × 5 = 10π cm, and an area of π × 5² = 25π cm².

    Key idea: in terms of π: work out the number part and write π after it, like 25π. Square the radius, but do not square the π.

    Worked example

    A circle has a diameter of 14 cm. Give its circumference and area in terms of π.

    1. Circumference = π × d = 14π cm.
    2. The radius is 7 cm, so area = π × 7² = 49π cm².

    7. Cylinders

    A Cylinder is a prism with a circle for its cross-section. So its volume is the area of the circular cross-section times the height (or length).

    cylinder: volume = π × r² × h

    Worked example

    A cylinder has a radius of 3 cm and a height of 10 cm. Find its volume to 1 decimal place.

    1. Area of the circle = π × 3² = 28.2743… cm².
    2. Volume = 28.2743… × 10 = 282.7433…
    3. The volume is 282.7 cm³ to 1 d.p.

    Now you finish it

    A cylinder has a radius of 5 cm and a height of 2 cm. Find its volume to 1 decimal place.

    1. Area of circle = π × 5² = ___.
    2. Volume = ___ × 2 = ___ cm³.
    Check my answer

    π × 25 = 78.5398…, and 78.5398… × 2 = 157.0796… ≈ 157.1 cm³.

    Check your knowledge: in terms of π and cylinders

    A circle has a radius of 5 cm. Its circumference is ___π cm. Type the number that goes in front of the π, like 12.

    1. Use C = 2 × π × r.
    2. Work out 2 × 5 and leave the π as a letter.
    • That looks like π × 5. The formula with a radius has 2 × π × r.
    • That is the number in the area, 25π. This question asks for the circumference.

    C = 2 × π × 5 = 10π, so the number is 10.

    A circle has a radius of 6 cm. Its area is ___π cm². Type the number that goes in front of the π.

    1. Use A = π × r².
    2. Work out 6² and leave the π as a letter.
    • That is 2 × 6, the number in the circumference, 12π. This question asks for the area.
    • That is just the radius. Square it first.

    A = π × 6² = 36π, so the number is 36.

    A circle has a diameter of 14 cm. Its area is ___π cm². Type the number that goes in front of the π.

    1. Is 14 cm the radius or the diameter?
    2. Halve it to get the radius, then square it.
    • That squares the diameter, 14. Halve it to get the radius first.
    • That is the number in the circumference, 14π. This question asks for the area.

    Radius = 7 cm. A = π × 7² = 49π, so the number is 49.

    A semicircle has a radius of 4 cm. Its area is ___π cm². Type the number that goes in front of the π.

    1. Find the area of the whole circle in terms of π.
    2. A semicircle is half of that.
    • That is the whole circle, 16π. A semicircle is half of it.

    Whole circle = π × 4² = 16π. Half of it = 8π, so the number is 8.

    A cylinder has a radius of 4 cm and a height of 10 cm. Work out its volume to 1 decimal place, in cm³.

    1. The volume is the area of the circular end times the height.
    2. Work out π × 4² × 10.
    • That does not square the radius. Use π × r² × h, with 4² = 16.

    Area of the end = π × 4² = 50.2655… cm². Volume = 50.2655… × 10 = 502.6548… ≈ 502.7 cm³.

    A hot water tank is a cylinder with a diameter of 0.6 m and a height of 1.2 m. 1 m³ is 1000 litres. How many litres does it hold? Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

    1. Halve the diameter to get the radius, in m.
    2. Find the volume in m³, then multiply by 1000 to change it to litres.
    • That is the volume in m³. Multiply by 1000 to get litres.
    • That uses the diameter, 0.6, as the radius. Halve it first.

    Radius = 0.3 m. Volume = π × 0.3² × 1.2 = 0.33929… m³. Litres = 0.33929… × 1000 = 339.292… ≈ 339.3.

    A cylinder has a volume of 500 cm³ and a height of 10 cm. What is its radius? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in cm.

    1. Volume = π × r² × h. Divide the volume by π × h to find r².
    2. Then take the square root.
    • That is r². Take the square root to find r.

    r² = 500 ÷ (π × 10) = 15.9155…. r = √15.9155… = 3.9894… ≈ 4.0 cm.

    A cylinder has a radius of 3 cm and a height of 5 cm. Its volume is ___π cm³. Type the number that goes in front of the π.

    1. Use V = π × r² × h.
    2. Work out 3² × 5 and leave the π as a letter.
    • That does not square the radius: 3 × 5. Use r² = 9.
    • That looks like 2 × 3 × 5. Volume needs r² × h.

    V = π × 3² × 5 = 45π, so the number is 45.

    Spot the mistake

    Finding an error in someone else's working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

    Dan says: a circle has a diameter of 10 cm, so its area is π × 10² = 314.2 cm². What went wrong?

    • He used the diameter, not the radius
    • He should have used 2πr instead of πr² for an area
    • Nothing is wrong, because the formula uses the diameter squared
    1. Which formula does Dan use? Which number does it need, the radius or the diameter?
    2. What is the radius of this circle?

    The formula needs the radius, 5 cm. A = π × 5² = 78.5 cm².

    Eve says: a semicircle has a radius of 5 cm, so its perimeter is 1 over 2 × 2 × π × 5 = 15.7 cm. What went wrong?

    • Nothing is wrong. The perimeter is half of the circumference.
    • She left out the straight edge of 10 cm.
    • She should have used πr².
    1. Draw the semicircle and trace the whole edge with a pencil.
    2. What did Eve leave out?

    Her 15.7 cm is the curved edge only. Add the straight edge, 10 cm: 25.7 cm.

    Ida says: a patio has a radius of 2 m and paving costs £42 per m². She rounds the area to 12.57 and gets £527.94. The answer sheet says £527.79. Why do they differ?

    • She rounded the area too early
    • The answer sheet must have used a different radius for its working
    • £42 should be turned into pence before it is multiplied
    1. Work out π × 4 on your calculator, then multiply by 42 without rounding.
    2. Compare it with 12.57 × 42.

    The exact area is 12.566… m². Multiplying by 42 gives 527.79 to 2 d.p. Rounding the area to 12.57 first pushes the answer up to 527.94. Round only at the end.

    [4 marks] A circular pond has a radius of 3 m. A path 1 m wide goes all the way round the pond. Show that the area of the path is 22 m², correct to the nearest whole number.

    1. The path is a ring. What is the radius of the pond plus the path?
    2. Find the area of the big circle and the area of the pond.
    3. Subtract, then round to a whole number.

    [4 marks] A circular patio has an area of 50 m². A border is laid right round the edge of the patio. Work out the length of the border. Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in m.

    2. r² = 50 ÷ π. Then take the square root.
    3. The border is the circumference, 2πr. Use the unrounded radius.
    • That is r². Take the square root to find r, then find the circumference.
    • That is the radius. The border is the circumference, 2πr.

    r² = 50 ÷ π = 15.9155…. r = √15.9155… = 3.9894… m. Border = 2 × π × 3.9894… = 25.0663… ≈ 25.1 m.

    • M1 for r² = 50 ÷ π
    • M1 for the square root, r = 3.99…
    • M1 for 2 × π × r
    • A1 for 25.1

    Higher [3 marks] A sector of a circle has an angle of 40° and a radius of 9 cm. Work out the area of the sector. Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in cm².

    1. What fraction of a full circle is 40°?
    2. Find the area of the whole circle, πr².
    3. Multiply the fraction by the whole circle.
    • That is the whole circle. The sector is only part of it.
    • That is the arc length. The sector area uses πr².

    Fraction = 40 over 360 = 1 over 9. Whole circle = π × 9² = 254.4690…. Sector = 1 over 9 × 254.4690… = 28.2743… ≈ 28.3 cm².

    • M1 for 40 over 360 (or 1 over 9) as the fraction
    • M1 for π × 9² (= 254.47…)
    • A1 for 28.3

    Why it works

    • π is a ratio. For every circle, circumference ÷ diameter is the same number, about 3.14159. A big circle and a small circle are the same shape at different sizes, so that ratio never changes. C = πd is that fact written as a formula.
    • The area uses r². Cut a circle into thin slices like a pizza and lay them head to tail. They make a shape close to a rectangle. Its height is the radius, r, and its length is half of the circumference, πr. Area = πr × r = πr².
    • Doubling the radius makes the area 4 times bigger. The radius is squared, and 2² = 4.
    • Backwards means square roots. If A = πr², undo it in two steps: divide by π, then take the square root.
    • A sector is a fraction of a circle. The angle θ out of 360° tells you the fraction, and then everything else is the same as the full circle.

    Common traps

    • Using the diameter in the area formula. Halve it first.
    • Squaring π instead of r, or writing r² as r × 2.
    • Mixing up the circumference (a length, cm) with the area (a space, cm²).
    • Leaving out the straight edges in the perimeter of a semicircle or a quarter circle.
    • Rounding in the middle of a calculation. Keep the full value and round at the end.
    • Forgetting the last square root when working backwards from an area.

    Going further

    A piece of string 40 cm long makes the outline of a square with sides of 10 cm. The string is then reshaped into a circle. What is the area of the circle? Give your answer to 1 decimal place, in cm². Is it bigger than the area of the square?

    1. The string is 40 cm long, so the circle has a circumference of 40 cm.
    2. Find the radius from the circumference, then the area.
    • That is the area of the square. The question asks for the circle.
    • That is the diameter. Use the radius to find the area.

    C = 40, so r = 40 ÷ (2π) = 6.3662… cm. A = π × 6.3662…² = 127.3240… ≈ 127.3 cm². That is bigger than the square (100 cm²). Of all shapes with the same perimeter, the circle holds the most area.

    The radius of a circle is doubled. How many times bigger is the new area? (Try it with a radius of 3 cm, then of 5 cm. Does the answer change?)

    1. Work out the area for a radius of 3 cm, then for 6 cm.
    2. Divide the new area by the old one.
    • The radius doubles, but the area uses the radius squared. It grows by more than that.

    Radius 3: A = π × 9 = 9π. Radius 6: A = π × 36 = 36π. 36π ÷ 9π = 4. For any radius r, doubling gives π(2r)² = 4πr², which is always 4 times the original. A circle with twice the radius has 4 times the area.

    A rope is pulled tight round a circle (a ball, or the Earth). The rope is then made exactly 1 m longer, and held at the same height above the circle all the way round. How far is the rope above the surface? Give your answer to 2 decimal places, in m. (The answer is the same for any size of circle.)

    1. The rope was the circumference, 2πr. The longer rope is 2πr + 1.
    2. The new rope is a circle with a bigger radius, R. How much bigger is the radius if the circumference is 1 m longer?
    • That looks like the extra length. Divide it by 2π: radius = circumference ÷ 2π.
    • That is 1 ÷ π, the extra diameter. The gap is the extra radius, so divide by 2π.

    Old circumference = 2πr. New circumference = 2πr + 1 = 2πR. So 2π(R − r) = 1, and R − r = 1 ÷ (2π) = 0.1592… ≈ 0.16 m. The gap is about 16 cm, and the radius of the circle does not matter.
