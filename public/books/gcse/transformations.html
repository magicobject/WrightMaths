    Transformations

    A transformation moves a shape or changes its size. You will slide it (translation), flip it (reflection), turn it (rotation) and make it bigger or smaller (enlargement). Then you will do it the other way round: look at a shape before and after, and describe exactly what happened.

    Where you will use this: repeating a pattern along a wall or a floor, laying tiles, a bathroom plan that is a mirror image of its neighbour, and scaling a drawing up or down.

    Words you need

    Tap a teal word anywhere on the page to see this again.

    Translation
    A transformation that slides every point the same distance in the same direction. The shape does not turn, flip or change size.
    Reflection
    A transformation that flips a shape over a mirror line. Each point and its image are the same distance from the line, on opposite sides.
    Rotation
    A transformation that turns a shape about a fixed point called the centre. To describe it you give the angle, the direction (clockwise or anticlockwise) and the centre.
    Enlargement
    A change of size where every length is multiplied by the same scale factor. The shape stays the same.

    Typing coordinates and vectors

    Type a point as a pair in brackets, x first and then y, like (3, 2). For negative numbers use a minus sign, like (-2, 5).

    Type a translation vector the same way, with the top number first, like (3, -2). The brackets are optional, and so are the spaces.

    On every grid in this topic, one square is one unit. Your picture does not need to be exact, but the numbers do.

    1. Moving shapes: object and image

    A Transformation moves a shape or changes its size. The shape you start with is the object. The shape you finish with is the Image. We label the image with a small dash: the corner A becomes A′, said “A prime”.

    There are four transformations to know.

    • A Translation slides the shape. It does not turn or flip.
    • A Reflection flips the shape over a mirror line.
    • A Rotation turns the shape about a point.
    • An Enlargement makes the shape bigger or smaller.

    After a translation, reflection or rotation, the image is Congruent to the object: the same size and the same shape. After an enlargement, the image has the same shape but a different size, so the two shapes are similar.

    Key idea: only an enlargement changes lengths. Every angle stays the same in all four transformations.

    Worked example

    A rectangle is 6 cm long and 2 cm wide. It is rotated about a point. What is the perimeter of the image?

    1. A rotation gives a congruent image, so the sides stay 6 cm and 2 cm.
    2. Perimeter = 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 16.
    3. The perimeter of the image is 16 cm.

    Now you finish it

    2. Translation

    A Translation slides every point of a shape the same distance in the same direction. The shape does not turn, flip or change size.

    We describe a translation with a column vector: two numbers, one above the other, in brackets.

    ab   means   a to the right, b up

    • The top number is the move across. Positive is right, negative is left.
    • The bottom number is the move up or down. Positive is up, negative is down.

    So 3−2 means 3 to the right and 2 down. It is the same order as coordinates: across first, then up.

    Key idea: to find an image point, add the vector to the object point. Add the top number to x, and the bottom number to y.

    Worked example

    Triangle ABC has corners A(1, 1), B(3, 1) and C(1, 4). Translate it by 4−2. Write the coordinates of A′, B′ and C′.

    1. Add 4 to each x-coordinate. Add −2 (which means take away 2) to each y-coordinate.
    2. A(1, 1) goes to (5, −1).
    3. B(3, 1) goes to (7, −1).
    4. C(1, 4) goes to (5, 2).
    5. Check on the grid: every corner has moved 4 right and 2 down. ✓

    Now you finish it

    3. Reflection

    A Reflection flips a shape over a mirror line. Each point of the image is the same distance from the mirror line as the object point, but on the other side. The line between a point and its image meets the mirror at right angles.

    • A vertical mirror line has an equation like x = 4. Every point on it has x = 4.
    • A horizontal mirror line has an equation like y = 2. Every point on it has y = 2.
    • The x-axis is the line y = 0. The y-axis is the line x = 0.

    Key idea: count the squares from the point to the mirror line, then count the same number of squares on the other side. A point that is on the mirror line stays where it is.

    Worked example

    Triangle ABC has corners A(1, 1), B(3, 1) and C(1, 4). Reflect triangle ABC in the line x = 4. Write the coordinates of A′, B′ and C′.

    1. A is 3 squares to the left of the line, so A′ is 3 squares to the right: (7, 1).
    2. B is 1 square to the left, so B′ is 1 square to the right: (5, 1).
    3. C is 3 squares to the left, so C′ is (7, 4).
    4. The y-coordinates do not change, because the mirror line is vertical. ✓

    3b. Reflecting in the line y = x Higher

    The line y = x goes through (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 2) and so on. Reflecting in it swaps the two coordinates.

    reflect in y = x: (x, y) → (y, x)     reflect in y = −x: (x, y) → (−y, −x)

    Check with the point (3, 1), which is below the line y = x. Its image (1, 3) is above the line, and the point and its image are the same distance from the line.

    2. A(1, 2) goes to (2, 1).
    3. B(4, 2) goes to (2, 4).
    4. C(4, 4) is on the line, so it stays at (4, 4).

    4. Rotation

    A Rotation turns a shape about a fixed point called the centre of rotation. To describe a rotation you need three things.

    • The angle, such as 90° (a quarter turn) or 180° (a half turn).
    • The direction: clockwise (the way a clock’s hands go) or anticlockwise.
    • The centre, as a point such as (0, 0).

    Start with the centre at the Origin. These rules turn a point (x, y):

    180° (clockwise or anticlockwise): (x, y) → (−x, −y)

    90° clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x)     90° anticlockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x)

    A turn of 270° clockwise is the same as 90° anticlockwise. If you forget a rule, test it on (1, 0): a quarter turn clockwise takes it from the right of the origin to straight below it, at (0, −1).

    Key idea: in exams you can use tracing paper. Trace the shape, press a pencil point on the centre, and turn the paper. For a centre that is not the origin, use the arm from the centre to the point: turn the arm, then add the result back to the centre.

    Worked example: centre at the origin

    Triangle ABC has corners A(2, 1), B(5, 1) and C(2, 3). Rotate triangle ABC 90° clockwise about the origin.

    1. Use (x, y) → (y, −x).
    2. A(2, 1) goes to (1, −2).
    3. B(5, 1) goes to (1, −5).
    4. C(2, 3) goes to (3, −2).
    5. Check: A is at 2 right and 1 up. A quarter turn clockwise puts it 1 right and 2 down. ✓

    Worked example: centre not at the origin

    Point P is at (5, 3). Rotate the point P 90° clockwise about the centre (2, 1).

    1. The arm from the centre to P goes 3 right and 2 up. Write it as (3, 2).
    2. Turn the arm 90° clockwise with (x, y) → (y, −x): (3, 2) becomes (2, −3). That is 2 right and 3 down.
    3. Add it to the centre (2, 1): (2 + 2, 1 + (−3)) = (4, −2).
    4. P′ is (4, −2).

    5. Enlargement

    An Enlargement changes the size of a shape. You need the scale factor and the centre of enlargement.

    • The scale factor says how many times bigger the lengths become. Scale factor 3 triples every length.
    • The centre is the fixed point. Each image point is on a straight line from the centre through its object point, and its distance from the centre is multiplied by the scale factor.
    • A scale factor between 0 and 1, such as 1 over 2, makes the shape smaller. It is still called an enlargement.

    image point = centre + scale factor × (arm from the centre to the object point)

    If the centre is the origin, multiply both coordinates by the scale factor: (x, y) → (kx, ky).

    Key idea: lengths are multiplied by the scale factor. Angles do not change. Type a fraction scale factor as 1/2.

    Worked example: centre at the origin

    Triangle ABC has corners A(1, 1), B(3, 1) and C(1, 2). Enlarge triangle ABC by scale factor 2, centre (0, 0).

    1. Multiply every coordinate by 2.
    2. A(1, 1) goes to (2, 2).
    3. B(3, 1) goes to (6, 2).
    4. C(1, 2) goes to (2, 4).
    5. Check: each image corner is on the straight line from the centre through its object corner, and is twice as far from the centre. ✓

    Worked example: centre not at the origin

    Triangle ABC has corners A(2, 1), B(4, 1) and C(2, 3). Enlarge triangle ABC by scale factor 2, centre (1, 1).

    1. For each corner, find the arm from the centre (1, 1), double it, and add it back.
    2. A: the arm is (1, 0). Doubled: (2, 0). Add to (1, 1): (3, 1).
    3. B: the arm is (3, 0). Doubled: (6, 0). Add to (1, 1): (7, 1).
    4. C: the arm is (1, 2). Doubled: (2, 4). Add to (1, 1): (3, 5).

    Worked example: a fraction scale factor

    Triangle ABC has corners A(2, 2), B(6, 2) and C(2, 6). Enlarge triangle ABC by scale factor 1 over 2, centre (0, 0).

    1. Multiply every coordinate by 1 over 2. That is the same as halving.

    8. Symmetry, tiles and patterns

    A shape has symmetry when a transformation maps it onto itself.

    • A line of symmetry is a mirror line that maps the shape onto itself. Fold along it and the two halves match.
    • A shape has rotational symmetry if a turn of less than a full turn about its centre maps it onto itself. The order is the number of ways it fits in one full turn. Order 1 means no rotational symmetry.
    • Square: 4 lines, order 4. Rectangle (not a square): 2 lines, order 2.
    • Equilateral triangle: 3 lines, order 3. Isosceles triangle: 1 line, order 1.
    • Parallelogram (not a rectangle or rhombus): 0 lines, order 2. Rhombus: 2 lines, order 2. Kite: 1 line, order 1.
    • A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry and order n.

    smallest turn that fits = 360° ÷ order

    Patterns are built from transformations. A Tessellation covers a flat surface with copies of a tile with no gaps and no overlaps. Squares, equilateral triangles and regular hexagons all tessellate on their own. A border is a motif moved along by a translation again and again. A tiled floor often turns or flips each tile as well.

    Key idea: a line of symmetry is a reflection that leaves the shape looking unchanged. A rotational symmetry is a rotation that leaves the shape looking unchanged.

    Reflection in the y-axis sends (x, y) to (−x, y), so (2, 5) goes to (−2, 5). (A translation of (−4, 0) also does it for this one point. That is why we use a whole shape to describe a transformation.)

    Spot the mistake

    Finding an error in someone else’s working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

    Dan says: the point (1, 2) is reflected in the line x = 3, so the image is (4, 2). What went wrong?

    • He added 3 to x instead of reflecting
    • Nothing is wrong. The image of (1, 2) really is (4, 2).
    • He should have changed the y-coordinate as well as x
    1. How far is (1, 2) from the line x = 3?
    2. The image is the same distance on the other side.

    The point is 2 left of x = 3, so the image is 2 right of it: x = 5. The image is (5, 2).

    Eve says: rotating (2, 1) by 90° clockwise about the origin gives (−1, 2). What went wrong?

    • She turned anticlockwise, not clockwise
    • She used a half turn instead of a quarter turn
    • Nothing is wrong. Her answer is right for this turn.
    1. Which rule is for clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x) or (−y, x)?
    2. Try the rule on (2, 1).

    Clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x), so (2, 1) goes to (1, −2). Eve’s (−1, 2) is what you get turning anticlockwise.

    Ida says: the point (3, 2) is enlarged by scale factor 2 about the centre (1, 1), so the image is (6, 4). What went wrong?

    • She doubled from the origin, but the centre is (1, 1)
    • She should have added 2 to each coordinate
    • Nothing is wrong. Scale factor 2 doubles both coordinates.
    1. Where is the centre? Does she use it?
    2. Find the arm from (1, 1) to (3, 2), double it, and add it back to the centre.

    The arm is (2, 1). Doubled: (4, 2). Add to the centre: (1 + 4, 1 + 2) = (5, 3).

    Fay says: a shape moves 2 squares left and 3 squares down, so the translation vector is 23. What went wrong?

    • Both numbers need minus signs
    • The two numbers should be swapped round
    • Nothing is wrong. Both numbers are the right size.
    1. Right and up are positive. What are left and down?
    2. Write the across move on top and the up/down move underneath.

    Left 2 is −2 across. Down 3 is −3 up. The vector is −2−3.

    Check your knowledge: mixed review

    The point (−3, 4) is reflected in the x-axis. Write the image. Type it like (2, 5).

    1. The x-axis is the horizontal line y = 0.
    2. Only the sign of y changes.
    • That reflects in the y-axis. The x-axis is horizontal, so y changes.
    • That changes both signs, which is a half turn about the origin.

    The reflection changes the sign of y: (−3, −4).

    The point (2, −3) is rotated 90° anticlockwise about the origin. Write the image, like (2, 5).

    1. Anticlockwise uses (x, y) → (−y, x).
    2. Here x = 2 and y = −3, so −y = 3.
    • That is 90° clockwise. Anticlockwise uses (x, y) → (−y, x).
    • That is a half turn. A quarter turn also swaps the coordinates.

    (x, y) → (−y, x): (2, −3) goes to (3, 2). The image is (3, 2).

    The point (−2, 3) is enlarged by scale factor 2, centre (0, 0). Write the image, like (2, 5).

    1. Multiply both coordinates by 2.
    2. Keep the minus sign on the −2.
    • Both coordinates are multiplied by 2, not just x.
    • The sign of x should stay negative. A positive scale factor keeps the same sign.

    2 × (−2) = −4 and 2 × 3 = 6. The image is (−4, 6).

    The point (3, 2) is translated by −1−4. Write the image, like (2, 5).

    1. Add the top number to x and the bottom number to y.
    2. 3 + (−1) and 2 + (−4).
    • That subtracts the vector. A translation by this vector adds it.
    • That looks like the signs flipped on the point. Add the vector to the point.

    x: 3 − 1 = 2. y: 2 − 4 = −2. The image is (2, −2).

    How many lines of symmetry does a square have?

    1. Think of folding a square paper in half.
    2. Two folds go through opposite sides and two go through opposite corners.
    • That counts only the two folds through the sides. There are two through the corners as well.
    • A square can be folded in several ways. Count the straight and the diagonal folds.

    A square has 2 lines through the middles of opposite sides and 2 diagonals: 4 lines of symmetry.

    The point (2, 3) is reflected in the line y = −1. Write the image. Type it like (2, 5).

    1. The line y = −1 is horizontal. How far above it is the point?
    2. The point is 4 above the line. The image is 4 below.
    • That reflects in the x-axis (y = 0). The line here is y = −1.
    • That goes only 3 below the line. The point is 4 above y = −1, so go 4 below it.

    3 is 4 above −1. The image is 4 below −1, which is −5. The image is (2, −5).

    A drone starts at (0, 0). It is translated by 6−2 and then translated by 6−2 again. Where does it finish? Type it like (2, 5).

    1. Do one translation, then the second from there.
    2. Or add the two vectors first.
    • That is after only one translation. There are two.
    • The y-move is down 2 each time, so y is negative.

    After one move: (6, −2). After two: (12, −4). The drone finishes at (12, −4).

    Check your knowledge: on the job

    Real jobs from the building trades: electrics, plumbing, carpentry and tiling. Prices are typical, so check them with your own supplier.

    An electrician marks sockets on a wall plan, in cm. The first socket is at (30, 25). Each next socket is a translation of 450 from the one before. What is the position of the 4th socket? Type it like (2, 5).

    1. The 1st socket is at (30, 25). How many translations get you to the 4th?
    2. Add 45 to x three times.
    • That is 4 translations. The 1st socket is already in place, so the 4th needs 3.
    • That is the 2nd socket. Keep adding 45.

    x = 30 + 3 × 45 = 165. y stays 25. The 4th socket is at (165, 25).

    A plumber is fitting the bathrooms of two flats. On a plan in metres, the basin in flat A is at (1, 2). Flat B is the mirror image of flat A in the party wall, which is the line x = 5. Where is the basin in flat B? Type it like (2, 5).

    1. The party wall is the mirror line x = 5. How far is the basin from it?
    2. Go the same distance on the other side.
    • That adds 5 to x. The basin is 4 left of the wall, so its image is 4 right of the wall.
    • That reflects in the y-axis. The party wall is the line x = 5.

    The basin is 4 left of x = 5, so the image is 4 right: x = 9. The height stays 2. Flat B’s basin is at (9, 2).

    A carpenter draws a roof truss with an enlargement of scale factor 1 over 20. The drawing is 15 cm wide. How wide is the real truss? Answer in m.

    1. The real truss is bigger than the drawing. Multiply by 20.
    2. Change cm to m at the end: divide by 100.
    • That is the width in cm. Divide by 100 to change to metres.
    • That divides by 20. The real truss is bigger than the drawing, so multiply.

    15 × 20 = 300 cm. In metres: 300 ÷ 100 = 3 m.

    A tiler lays a square tile with an arrow on it. Each time, she turns the tile 90° clockwise. How many turns does it take before the arrow points the same way as at the start?

    1. A full turn is 360°.
    2. How many lots of 90° make 360°?
    • Two turns is 180°, which points the arrow the opposite way. Keep going until the total is 360°.

    360 ÷ 90 = 4. After 4 quarter turns the arrow points the same way again.

    [3 marks] An electrician has a plan in metres. A light is at L(2, 3) in the first room. The same layout is used in the next room, by rotating the plan 180° about the point (4, 3). Where is the light in the next room? Type it like (2, 5).

    1. Find the arm from the centre (4, 3) to the light.
    2. A half turn reverses the arm. Add the new arm to the centre.
    • That is a half turn about the origin. The centre is (4, 3).
    • That is the reversed arm only. Add it to the centre (4, 3).

    The arm from (4, 3) to (2, 3) is (−2, 0). A half turn reverses it to (2, 0). Add to the centre: (4 + 2, 3 + 0) = (6, 3).

    • M1 for the arm (−2, 0) from the centre
    • M1 for reversing it to (2, 0) and adding to (4, 3)
    • A1 for (6, 3)

    [3 marks] A paving pattern on a patio repeats by a translation of 0.6 m along the wall each time, one slab per repeat. The wall is 4.8 m long. Each slab costs £18.50. How much do the slabs cost? Give your answer in pounds to 2 decimal places, like 12.50.

    1. How many slabs fit along the wall? Divide the wall length by one repeat.
    2. Then multiply the number of slabs by £18.50.
    • That is the number of slabs. Multiply by £18.50 to get the cost.
    • That divides 0.6 by 4.8, the wrong way round. Divide the wall length by the repeat.

    4.8 ÷ 0.6 = 8 slabs. Cost = 8 × 18.50 = £148.00.

    • M1 for 4.8 ÷ 0.6
    • M1 for 8 × 18.50
    • A1 for 148.00

    1. Anticlockwise uses (x, y) → (−y, x).
    2. Here x = 4 and y = 3.

    • A1 for (−3, 4)

    [4 marks] A room is 4 m by 3 m. A plan of the room is a drawing made by an enlargement of the room with scale factor 1 over 50. What is the area of the room on the plan? Give your answer in cm².

    1. Change the room lengths to cm first.
    2. Divide each length by 50, then multiply to get the area.
    • That is the area of the room in m², not the area on the plan in cm².
    • That divides the room’s area, 12, by 50. Divide each length by 50 first, then find the area.

    4 m = 400 cm and 3 m = 300 cm. On the plan: 400 ÷ 50 = 8 cm and 300 ÷ 50 = 6 cm. Area = 8 × 6 = 48 cm².

    • M1 for dividing a length by 50

    Higher [3 marks] The point P(2, 3) is reflected in the x-axis. The image is then rotated 90° clockwise about the origin. Write the coordinates of the final image. Type them like (2, 5).

    1. Do the reflection first and write down the new point.
    2. Then use (x, y) → (y, −x) on that point.
    • That is only the rotation. Reflect first, as the question says, and then rotate.
    • That is only the reflection. Now rotate it.

    Reflect in the x-axis: (2, 3) goes to (2, −3). Rotate 90° clockwise with (x, y) → (y, −x): (2, −3) goes to (−3, −2).

    • M1 for (2, −3) from the reflection
    • M1 for using (y, −x) on it
    • A1 for (−3, −2)

    Why it works

    • A mirror line is halfway. Every point and its image are the same distance from the mirror, on opposite sides. That is why you count squares to the line and then count the same number again.
    • A translation is addition. Every point moves by the same vector, so you add the same two numbers to every point. Nothing turns, flips or stretches.
    • A rotation keeps distances from the centre. The arm from the centre to a point stays the same length and only changes direction. That is why you turn the arm and add it back to the centre.
    • An enlargement keeps the shape. Lines from the centre pass through matching corners, and every distance along them is multiplied by the same scale factor. Matching angles stay equal, so the shapes are similar.
    • Symmetry is a move that leaves the shape looking unchanged. A line of symmetry is a reflection that maps the shape onto itself, and rotational symmetry is a rotation that maps the shape onto itself.
    • Two moves can make one. Two reflections in parallel mirrors slide the shape, and two reflections in mirrors that cross turn it about the crossing point.

    Common traps

    • Writing a vector with the numbers the wrong way round. The top number is across, the bottom number is up.
    • Going the right distance but on the wrong side of the mirror line.
    • Turning the wrong way. Check with (1, 0): a quarter turn clockwise takes it to (0, −1).
    • Forgetting the centre when an enlargement or rotation is not about the origin.
    • Describing a transformation with only half of the details, or with two transformations at once.
    • Writing the scale factor upside down: it is image length ÷ object length.
