    Inequalities

    An inequality says one amount is bigger or smaller than another, instead of exactly equal. Learn the four signs, show answers on a number line, and solve inequalities like equations, with one new rule for negatives. Then run it backwards: build an inequality from its answer, and check an answer by trying numbers.

    Where you will use this: a van’s weight limit, a budget that must not be passed, and the tolerance on a pipe or a cable that has to be cut to length.

    Inequality
    A statement that one amount is bigger or smaller than another, using <, >, ≤ or ≥, instead of saying they are equal.
    Number line
    A straight line with numbers marked at equal spacing, increasing from left to right. It continues on the left of zero for negative numbers.
    Integer
    A whole number. It can be positive, negative or zero.

    An inequality with two signs, saying a value is between two numbers.

    Typing inequalities

    Type < for “less than” and > for “greater than”. Type <= for ≤ and >= for ≥. The answer box also accepts the ≤ and ≥ signs if you paste them.

    Write x>2 or 3<=x<7. Spaces do not matter. For a list of whole numbers, put commas between them, like 1,2,3, and write them in order from smallest to largest.

    Where the answer is “this or that”, write it like x<-2 or x>3.

    1. The four signs and what they say

    An equation says two things are equal. An Inequality says that one is bigger or smaller than the other. There are four signs.

    The four inequality signs
    SignIn wordsExampleWhat x can be
    <is less thanx < 54, 3, 2 and so on, but not 5
    >is greater thanx > 56, 7, 8 and so on, but not 5
    ≤is less than or equal tox ≤ 55, 4, 3 and so on (5 is allowed)
    ≥is greater than or equal tox ≥ 55, 6, 7 and so on (5 is allowed)

    The pointed end of the sign points at the smaller number. The line under ≤ and ≥ means “or equal to”, so the number itself is allowed.

    Words that mean each sign

    • < “less than”, “fewer than”, “under”, “below”.
    • > “greater than”, “more than”, “over”, “above”.
    • ≤ “at most”, “no more than”, “maximum”, “up to and including”.
    • ≥ “at least”, “no less than”, “minimum”, “from” (as in “from 12 years old”).

    Key idea: ask yourself “can it be exactly that number?” If yes, use ≤ or ≥. If no, use < or >.

    Worked example

    A lift can carry at most 800 kg. Write this as an inequality for the load, w kg.

    1. “At most” means 800 kg is the biggest the load can be.
    2. The load can be exactly 800 kg, so the sign includes “equal to”.
    3. w ≤ 800.

    Now you finish it

    Children under 12 get a free ticket. Write an inequality for the age, a years.

    1. “Under 12” means 12 is not included.
    2. Which sign leaves out 12? a ___ 12.
    Check my answer

    A child aged exactly 12 does not get a free ticket, so the sign is <: a < 12.

    2. Showing an inequality on a number line

    A Number line shows every number that x is allowed to be, not just one. You need three things.

    • The circle. An open circle (white inside) for < and >: the number is not allowed. A filled circle for ≤ and ≥: the number is allowed.
    • The direction. The line and its arrow go right for > and ≥ (bigger numbers). They go left for < and ≤ (smaller numbers).
    • The number. The circle sits on the number in the inequality.

    x > 2: open circle at 2 (2 is not allowed), arrow to the right.

    x ≤ −1: filled circle at −1 (−1 is allowed), arrow to the left.

    Key idea: open circle = the number is NOT allowed. Filled circle = the number IS allowed. The arrow points the same way as the pointed end of the sign.

    Worked example

    Show x ≥ 1 on a number line.

    1. Put a circle on 1.
    2. ≥ includes 1, so the circle is filled.
    3. The numbers are 1 or bigger, so draw the line and arrow to the right.

    Now you finish it

    Describe the number line for x < −2.

    1. The circle goes on ___.
    2. The sign < leaves out −2, so the circle is (open or filled?) ___.
    3. The numbers are smaller than −2, so the arrow goes (left or right?) ___.
    Check my answer

    An open circle on −2, with the line and arrow going left.

    3. Whole-number solutions

    An Integer is a whole number. It can be negative, zero or positive. Often a question asks you to list the integers that fit an inequality.

    • Picture the number line, or draw it.
    • Check the two ends. An open end is left out. A filled end is kept.
    • Write the whole numbers in order from smallest to largest, with commas.

    −2 < x ≤ 3. The whole numbers inside are −1, 0, 1, 2 and 3.

    Worked example

    x is an integer and 1 ≤ x < 6. List the possible values of x.

    1. 1 ≤ x means 1 is allowed (the line under the sign).
    2. x < 6 means 6 is not allowed.
    3. The values are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

    Now you finish it

    x is an integer and −3 < x ≤ 1. List the possible values of x.

    1. −3 is left out, so start at ___.
    2. 1 is allowed, so finish at ___.
    3. The values are ___.
    Check my answer

    −3 is not allowed and 1 is allowed: −2, −1, 0, 1.

    Check your knowledge: whole-number solutions

    x is an integer and 2 ≤ x < 7. List the values of x. Put commas between them, like 1,2,3.

    1. Is 2 allowed? Is 7 allowed?
    2. Start at the smallest allowed whole number and finish at the largest.
    • 7 is not allowed, because the sign at that end is < and not ≤.
    • 2 is allowed, because of the line under the sign at that end.

    2 ≤ x allows 2. x < 7 leaves out 7. The values are 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

    x is an integer and −3 < x ≤ 2. List the values of x. Put commas between them.

    1. The left end has <. Is −3 allowed?
    2. The right end has ≤. Is 2 allowed?
    • −3 is not allowed, because the sign at that end is <.
    • 2 is allowed, because of the line under the sign at that end.

    −3 is not allowed and 2 is allowed. The values are −2, −1, 0, 1, 2.

    x is an integer and x < 9. What is the largest possible value of x?

    1. Is 9 allowed?
    2. What is the whole number just below 9?
    • x < 9 does not allow 9.

    9 is not allowed, so the largest whole number is 8.

    x is an integer and x > −4.5. What is the smallest possible value of x?

    1. Place −4.5 on a number line, between two whole numbers.
    2. x has to be bigger than −4.5. Which whole number is the first one to the right of it?
    • −5 is smaller than −4.5, so it is not allowed. Go to the right.

    −4.5 is between −5 and −4. x must be bigger than −4.5, so the smallest whole number is −4.

    How many integers x make −2 ≤ x ≤ 2 true?

    1. Both ends are allowed.
    2. List them: −2, −1, and keep going up to 2.
    • You may have left out 0. Zero is a whole number, so it counts.

    The values are −2, −1, 0, 1, 2. That is 5 whole numbers.

    4. Solving linear inequalities

    To Solve an inequality, work just as you do with an equation. Do the same thing to both sides, using inverse operations. The sign stays the same, except when you multiply or divide by a negative number (you will meet that later on this page). The answer is a range of values, not one number.

    3x + 2 < 14   →   3x < 12   →   x < 4

    Check by trying numbers. Try one number from your answer: x = 3 gives 3 × 3 + 2 = 11, which is less than 14, so it works. Try the boundary: x = 4 gives 14, which is not less than 14, so 4 is left out. Try one outside: x = 5 gives 17, which is too big.

    Key idea: solve the inequality like an equation. Then check one number inside the answer, the boundary number, and one number outside.

    Worked example: two steps

    Solve 2x + 3 < 11.

    1. Subtract 3 from both sides: 2x < 8.
    2. Divide both sides by 2: x < 4.
    3. Check x = 3: 2 × 3 + 3 = 9, and 9 < 11. It works.

    Worked example: x on both sides

    Solve 5x − 2 ≥ 3x + 8.

    1. Take 3x from both sides to collect the x terms: 2x − 2 ≥ 8.
    2. Add 2 to both sides: 2x ≥ 10.
    3. Divide by 2: x ≥ 5.
    4. Check x = 5: 5 × 5 − 2 = 23 and 3 × 5 + 8 = 23, so they are equal, which ≥ allows.

    Now you finish it

    Solve 4x − 5 > 11.

    1. Add 5 to both sides: 4x > ___.
    2. Divide both sides by 4: x > ___.
    Check my answer

    4x > 16, so x > 4.

    Check your knowledge: solving inequalities

    Solve x + 5 > 12. Type it like x>3.

    1. What is added to x? Undo it.
    2. Take 5 away from both sides.
    • That adds 5 instead of taking it away. To undo + 5, subtract 5.
    • The sign in the question is >, with no line under it, so the answer keeps >.

    Subtract 5 from both sides: x > 12 − 5, so x > 7.

    Solve 3x ≤ 18. Type it like x<=4.

    1. x is multiplied by 3. Undo it.
    2. Divide both sides by 3.
    • That multiplies by 3. To undo × 3, divide by 3.
    • The sign in the question has the line under it, so the answer keeps ≤.

    Divide both sides by 3: x ≤ 18 ÷ 3, so x ≤ 6.

    Solve 2x − 7 < 5.

    1. Undo the subtraction first.
    2. Add 7 to both sides, then divide both sides by 2.
    • That subtracts 7 instead of adding it. To undo − 7, add 7.
    • That adds 7 but forgets to divide by 2.

    Add 7 to both sides: 2x < 12. Divide by 2: x < 6. Check x = 5: 2 × 5 − 7 = 3, and 3 < 5.

    Solve 5x + 4 ≥ 29.

    1. Undo the + 4 first.
    2. Subtract 4 from both sides, then divide by 5.
    • That adds 4 instead of subtracting it.
    • That subtracts 4 but forgets to divide by 5.

    Subtract 4: 5x ≥ 25. Divide by 5: x ≥ 5.

    Solve x over 3 + 2 > 6.

    1. Undo the + 2 first.
    2. Then undo the division by 3, by multiplying both sides by 3.
    • That stops one step early: x over 3 > 4. Multiply both sides by 3.
    • That adds 2 instead of subtracting it, then multiplies by 3. Take 2 from both sides first.
    • That multiplies 6 by 3 before subtracting the 2. Take 2 from both sides first.

    Subtract 2: x over 3 > 4. Multiply by 3: x > 12.

    Solve 7x − 3 < 4x + 9.

    1. Collect the x terms on one side, by subtracting 4x from both sides.
    2. You get 3x − 3 < 9. Now undo the − 3 and the 3x.
    • That stops at 3x < 12. Divide both sides by 3.
    • That subtracts 3 instead of adding it. To undo − 3, add 3.

    Subtract 4x: 3x − 3 < 9. Add 3: 3x < 12. Divide by 3: x < 4.

    5. Multiplying or dividing by a negative

    Here is the one new rule. Start with a true statement: 2 < 5. Now multiply both sides by −1.

    2 is to the left of 5. But −2 is to the right of −5. Multiplying by a negative flips the order, so −2 > −5.

    • When you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, reverse the sign: < becomes >, ≤ becomes ≥, and the other way round.
    • Adding or subtracting, even a negative number, never changes the sign.
    • Check by substitution afterwards, to catch a forgotten reversal of the sign.

    Key idea: if you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, the inequality sign turns round.

    Worked example

    Solve −3x > 12.

    1. Divide both sides by −3. This is dividing by a negative, so reverse the sign.
    2. x < −4.
    3. Check x = −5: (−3) × (−5) = 15, and 15 > 12. It works.
    4. Check x = −3 (outside the answer): (−3) × (−3) = 9, and 9 is not bigger than 12. It is correctly left out.

    Worked example: another way

    Solve 7 − 2x ≥ 1.

    1. Subtract 7 from both sides: −2x ≥ −6.
    2. Divide by −2 and reverse the sign: x ≤ 3.
    3. Check x = 0: 7 − 0 = 7, and 7 ≥ 1. It works. Check x = 5: 7 − 10 = −3, and −3 is not at least 1. Correctly left out.
    4. Another way: add 2x to both sides to get 7 ≥ 1 + 2x. That gives 6 ≥ 2x, so 3 ≥ x, which is the same as x ≤ 3. No negative division needed.

    Now you finish it

    Solve −2x ≤ 10.

    1. Divide both sides by −2.
    2. This divides by a negative, so ≤ becomes ___.
    3. x ___ −5.
    Check my answer

    Dividing by −2 reverses the sign: x ≥ −5. Check x = 0: 0 ≤ 10, true.

    Check your knowledge: negative numbers

    Solve −2x < 8.

    1. You will divide both sides by −2. What happens to the sign when you divide by a negative?
    2. Divide both sides by −2, and reverse the sign.
    • That forgets to reverse the sign when dividing by a negative.
    • That divides 8 by 2 but loses the minus sign. 8 ÷ −2 = −4.

    Divide both sides by −2 and reverse the sign: x > −4. Check x = 0: −2 × 0 = 0, and 0 < 8. It works.

    Solve −5x ≥ 20.

    1. Divide both sides by −5.
    2. The divisor is negative, so reverse the sign.
    • That forgets to reverse the sign.
    • 20 ÷ −5 is −4, not 4. Take care with the minus.

    Divide both sides by −5 and reverse the sign: x ≤ −4. Check x = −5: (−5) × (−5) = 25, and 25 ≥ 20.

    Solve 10 − x > 6.

    1. Subtract 10 from both sides.
    2. You get −x > −4. Now get rid of the minus in front of x.
    • That changes −x into x but forgets to reverse the sign.
    • Take care: −x > −4 means x < 4, because multiplying by −1 reverses the sign.

    Subtract 10: −x > −4. Multiply by −1 and reverse the sign: x < 4. Check x = 3: 10 − 3 = 7, and 7 > 6.

    Which of these is NOT a solution of 4 − 2x ≥ 10?

    • −10
    • −3
    • −1
    • −4.5
    1. Solve the inequality first, or test each option.
    2. 4 − 2x ≥ 10 gives −2x ≥ 6, then x ≤ −3.

    −2x ≥ 6, so x ≤ −3. The number −1 is bigger than −3, so it is not a solution. Check: 4 − 2 × (−1) = 6, and 6 is not at least 10.

    A water tank holds 500 litres. It then loses 15 litres every minute, so after t minutes it holds 500 − 15t litres. What is the largest whole number of minutes, t, for which the tank still holds at least 200 litres?

    1. “At least 200 litres” means 500 − 15t ≥ 200.
    2. Subtract 500 from both sides, then divide by −15 and reverse the sign.
    • The number of minutes cannot be negative. When you divide −300 by −15, the answer is positive.
    • That is 200 ÷ 15 (rounded). Solve 500 − 15t ≥ 200 step by step.

    500 − 15t ≥ 200. Subtract 500: −15t ≥ −300. Divide by −15 and reverse the sign: t ≤ 20. So the largest whole number of minutes is 20. Check: 500 − 15 × 20 = 200, which is at least 200.

    6. Double inequalities

    A Double inequality has two signs and says x is between two numbers. Read −2 < x ≤ 3 as “x is bigger than −2 and at most 3”. On the number line, the circles are at both ends and the line joins them.

    To solve one, do the same thing to all three parts. Write the answer with the smaller number on the left.

    3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9   →   2 < 2x ≤ 8   →   1 < x ≤ 4

    Worked example

    Solve 3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9.

    1. Subtract 1 from all three parts: 2 < 2x ≤ 8.
    2. Divide all three parts by 2: 1 < x ≤ 4.
    3. If x is an integer, the values are 2, 3, 4.

    Now you finish it

    Solve −4 < 2x − 2 ≤ 6.

    1. Add 2 to all three parts: −2 < 2x ≤ ___.
    2. Divide all three parts by 2: ___ < x ≤ ___.
    Check my answer

    −2 < 2x ≤ 8, so −1 < x ≤ 4.

    A tolerance is the amount a measurement is allowed to be out by, and it gives a double inequality. A pipe cut to 600 mm that is allowed to be 2 mm out either way has length x mm with 598 ≤ x ≤ 602.

    2. Then the right circle. The smaller number goes on the left in your answer.
    • The circle on −1 is filled, so −1 is allowed: use ≤ at that end.
    • The circle on 4 is open, so 4 is not allowed: use < at that end.

    Left: filled circle on −1, so ≤. Right: open circle on 4, so <. The numbers between are −1 ≤ x < 4.

    1. Do the same to all three parts.
    2. Divide all three parts by 2.
    • That multiplies by 2. To undo × 2, divide by 2.

    Divide all three parts by 2: 3 < x < 7.

    1. Add 1 to all three parts first.
    2. Then divide all three parts by 3.
    • The right end has <, so 4 is not allowed.
    • That adds 1 but forgets to divide all three parts by 3.

    Add 1: 6 ≤ 3x < 12. Divide by 3: 2 ≤ x < 4.

    Solve −3 < 2x + 5 ≤ 9.

    1. Subtract 5 from all three parts.
    2. You get −8 < 2x ≤ 4. Now divide all three parts by 2.
    • −4 is not allowed, because the sign at that end is <.
    • That stops at 2x. Divide all three parts by 2.

    Subtract 5: −8 < 2x ≤ 4. Divide by 2: −4 < x ≤ 2.

    1. What is the shortest allowed length? What is the longest?
    2. Both ends are allowed, so both signs have the line under them.
    • Exactly 598 and exactly 602 are allowed (up to 2 mm out), so use ≤ at both ends.
    • The 2 is how far the length can be out, not the shortest length. 600 − 2 = 598.

    Flip it: run it backwards

    So far you start with an inequality and find its answer. Now go the other way: you know the answer, and you build the inequality, or you test whether a number is an answer.

    Forwards

    2x + 3 < 11, x = ?

    Solve: x < 4.

    Backwards

    x < 4, so 2x + 3 < k, k = ?

    Put x = 4 in: 2 × 4 + 3 = 11, so k = 11.

    • To find a missing number in an inequality whose answer you know, put the boundary value into the left-hand side.
    • To test whether a number is a solution, put it in. Remember a number on the boundary only works if the sign has the line under it.
    • To check a solution you have found, test one number inside it, the boundary, and one number outside.

    Key idea: the boundary is the one number where both sides are equal. Substitute it to find a missing value, or to see whether it is allowed.

    Worked example: build it

    The solution of 3x − 1 ≤ k is x ≤ 4. Find k.

    1. The boundary is x = 4.
    2. Put it into the left side: 3 × 4 − 1 = 11.
    3. So k = 11. Check: 3x − 1 ≤ 11 gives 3x ≤ 12, so x ≤ 4. ✓

    Worked example: test a value

    Is x = 2 a solution of 5x − 3 > 6?

    1. Put x = 2 in: 5 × 2 − 3 = 7.
    2. Is 7 > 6? Yes.
    3. So x = 2 is a solution.

    Now you finish it

    The solution of 2x + 5 > k is x > 3. Find k.

    1. The boundary is x = ___.
    2. Put it in: 2 × ___ + 5 = ___.
    Check my answer

    2 × 3 + 5 = 11, so k = 11.

    Check your knowledge: flip it

    The solution of 2x + 1 < k is x < 5. Find k.

    1. The boundary is x = 5.
    2. Put x = 5 into 2x + 1.
    • That is the boundary value of x, not of k. Put it into the left-hand side.
    • That is 2 × 5 only. Do not forget the + 1.

    The boundary is x = 5. Put it in: 2 × 5 + 1 = 11, so k = 11. Check: 2x + 1 < 11 gives 2x < 10, so x < 5.

    The solution of 4x − 3 ≥ k is x ≥ 2. Find k.

    1. The boundary is x = 2.
    2. Put x = 2 into 4x − 3.
    • That is 4 × 2 only. Do not forget the − 3.
    • That is the boundary value of x, not of k.

    The boundary is x = 2. Put it in: 4 × 2 − 3 = 5, so k = 5. Check: 4x − 3 ≥ 5 gives 4x ≥ 8, so x ≥ 2.

    The inequality 3x + a < 10 has the solution x < 2. Find the number a.

    1. The boundary is x = 2, and the right side is 10.
    2. At the boundary, 3 × 2 + a = 10.
    • That is 3 × 2. The question asks for a, so work out 10 − 6.
    • That adds 6 to 10. a and 6 add up to 10, so take 6 away from 10.

    At the boundary x = 2: 3 × 2 + a = 10, so 6 + a = 10 and a = 4. Check: 3x + 4 < 10 gives 3x < 6, so x < 2.

    Which inequality has the solution x ≥ 3?

    • 3x ≥ 9
    • 2x + 1 ≥ 5, which gives x ≥ 2
    • x + 4 ≤ 7, which gives x ≤ 3
    1. Solve each one, or put x = 3 into each.
    2. The right answer has the boundary x = 3 and the sign pointing towards bigger numbers.

    3x ≥ 9 gives x ≥ 3. The second gives x ≥ 2, and the third gives x ≤ 3.

    Is x = 3 a solution of 4x − 5 > 7? Type yes or no.

    1. Put x = 3 into 4x − 5.
    2. Then compare your answer with 7. Be careful: the sign is >, not ≥.
    • 4 × 3 − 5 is exactly 7, and 7 is not greater than 7. The sign > does not allow equal.

    4 × 3 − 5 = 7. Is 7 > 7? No, they are equal. So x = 3 is not a solution (it is the boundary, which > leaves out).

    7. Quadratic inequalities Higher

    A Quadratic inequality has an x² in it, like x² − x − 6 < 0. You solve it with a sketch. You need to factorise a Quadratic; see Quadratic equations.

    • Step 1. Move everything to one side so the other side is 0.
    • Step 2. Factorise the quadratic, then solve the equation you get when it is set equal to 0, to find where the graph crosses the x-axis. These are the critical values.
    • Step 3. Sketch the graph. If the x² term is positive, the graph is a U-shape. Mark the two critical values on the x-axis.
    • Step 4. Read the sketch. For “< 0” you want the curve below the x-axis. For “> 0” you want it above.

    x² − x − 6 = (x − 3)(x + 2), so the critical values are 3 and −2. The curve is below the axis between them, so x² − x − 6 < 0 for −2 < x < 3.

    The curve is above the axis outside the critical values, so x² − x − 6 > 0 for x < −2 or x > 3.

    Key idea: for a U-shaped curve, “below the axis” (less than 0) is between the critical values, one inequality joined. “Above the axis” (greater than 0) is outside, two separate inequalities joined by “or”.

    Worked example

    Solve x² − 2x − 8 ≤ 0.

    1. Factorise: (x − 4)(x + 2) ≤ 0. The critical values are 4 and −2.
    2. Sketch a U-shape through −2 and 4.
    3. We want ≤ 0, the curve on or below the axis, which is between the critical values.
    4. −2 ≤ x ≤ 4. (Both ends are included because of ≤.)

    Worked example: a square

    Solve x² > 16.

    1. Make one side 0: x² − 16 > 0. This is (x − 4)(x + 4) > 0.
    2. The critical values are 4 and −4.
    3. We want the curve above the axis, so outside the critical values.
    4. x < −4 or x > 4.

    Now you finish it

    Solve x² − 5x + 6 < 0.

    1. Factorise: (x − ___)(x − ___) < 0.
    2. The critical values are ___ and ___.
    3. We want < 0: below the axis, so between them.
    Check my answer

    (x − 2)(x − 3) < 0, so 2 < x < 3.

    Higher Solve x² + 2x − 8 ≥ 0. Type it like x<=1 or x>=6.

    Spot the mistake

    Finding an error in someone else’s working helps you learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

    Dan says: −2x > 6, so dividing by −2 gives x > −3. What went wrong?

    • He should have divided 6 by 2 to get x > 3, ignoring the minus
    • He did not reverse the sign when dividing by a negative
    • Nothing is wrong, because x > −3 is the correct answer
    1. Try a number that fits his answer, like x = 0. Does it make the original true?
    2. What must you do to the sign when you divide by a negative number?

    Dividing by −2 turns the sign round: x < −3. Check x = −5: (−2) × (−5) = 10, which is bigger than 6.

    Eve says: x ≥ 4 is shown on a number line with an open circle on 4 and an arrow to the right. What went wrong?

    • The circle should be filled, because 4 is allowed
    • The arrow should point left, because 4 is a small number
    • Nothing is wrong, because every inequality uses an open circle
    1. What does the line under the sign mean?
    2. Open circle: the number is not allowed. Filled circle: the number is allowed.

    x ≥ 4 allows 4 itself, so the circle should be filled.

    Frank says: the integers with −2 ≤ x < 3 are −1, 0, 1, 2, 3. What went wrong?

    • He left out −2 and included 3
    • He should have listed every number from −3 to 3
    • He should have left out 0, because it is not a positive number
    1. Check the left end. Is −2 allowed?
    2. Then check the right end. Is 3 allowed?

    −2 ≤ x allows −2. x < 3 leaves out 3. The correct list is −2, −1, 0, 1, 2.

    Check your knowledge: mixed review

    Solve 4x − 9 ≤ 15.

    1. Undo the − 9 first.
    2. Add 9 to both sides, then divide by 4.
    • That subtracts 9 instead of adding it.
    • That adds 9 but forgets to divide by 4.

    Add 9: 4x ≤ 24. Divide by 4: x ≤ 6.

    Solve 12 − 3x < 0.

    1. Subtract 12 from both sides.
    2. You will divide by −3, a negative number.
    • That forgets to reverse the sign when dividing by −3.

    Subtract 12: −3x < −12. Divide by −3 and reverse the sign: x > 4. Check x = 5: 12 − 15 = −3, which is less than 0.

    x is an integer and −1 < x ≤ 3. List the values of x. Put commas between them.

    1. Is −1 allowed? Is 3 allowed?
    2. List the whole numbers in order from smallest to largest.
    • −1 is not allowed, because the sign at that end is <.

    −1 is left out and 3 is allowed: 0, 1, 2, 3.

    The line y = 2x + 1 is a straight line graph. For what values of x is y > 9? Type it like x>1.

    1. y > 9 means 2x + 1 > 9.
    2. Solve it like any other inequality.
    • That adds 1 instead of subtracting it (2x > 10). Subtract 1 first, then divide by 2.
    • The line goes up as x goes up, so y is bigger than 9 for bigger x.

    2x + 1 > 9. Subtract 1: 2x > 8. Divide by 2: x > 4. Check x = 5: y = 11, which is bigger than 9.

    Write “n is at most −2” as an inequality. Type it like n<=5.

    1. At most means the largest it can be is −2.
    2. Can n be exactly −2? Then the sign has the line under it.
    • That points the wrong way. At most −2 means −2 or smaller.
    • That leaves out −2 itself. At most includes the number.

    At most −2 means −2 or smaller: n ≤ −2.

    A taxi costs £3 plus £2 for each mile. Zoe has £25. What is the greatest whole number of miles she can travel?

    1. Write the cost of m miles, then say it must be at most 25.
    2. 3 + 2m ≤ 25. Solve it, then take the whole number below.
    • That is 25 − 3. Divide by 2 as well, because each mile costs £2.
    • Check: 12 miles costs 3 + 24 = £27, which is more than £25.

    [3 marks] Solve 7 − 3x > 22. Type it like x<2.

    1. Get the x term on its own first. Subtract 7 from both sides.
    2. You will divide by −3, a negative number. What happens to the sign?
    3. −3x > 15. Divide by −3 and turn the sign round.

    • M1 for dividing both sides by −3

    2. Subtract 1 from each part, then divide each part by 2.

    3. Your answer will not be a whole number. Does the van carry part of a box?

    85 + 38n ≤ 1200. Subtract 85: 38n ≤ 1115. Divide by 38: n ≤ 29.34…. The number of boxes must be a whole number, so the greatest is 29. Check: 85 + 38 × 29 = 1187, which is under 1200.

    • M1 for 38n ≤ 1115
    • M1 for n ≤ 29.3…
    • A1 for 29

    [3 marks] Solve 6x − 5 ≥ 2x + 11.

    1. Get the x terms on one side. Subtract 2x from both sides.
    2. You get 4x − 5 ≥ 11. Now add 5 to both sides.
    3. Then divide by 4.
    • That subtracts 5 from 11 instead of adding it.
    • You divided by 4, a positive number, so the sign does not change.

    [4 marks] Higher Solve x² − x − 12 > 0. Type it like x<2 or x>5.

    2. Find the critical values, then sketch a U-shape through them.
    3. > 0 means above the x-axis. Is that between the critical values or outside them?
    • That is the “below the axis” answer. > 0 asks for above, which is outside the critical values.
    • The critical values have swapped signs. (x − 4)(x + 3) is 0 at x = 4 and x = −3.

    • M1 for critical values −3 and 4
    • M1 for a U-shaped sketch, or “above the axis”, to pick the outside regions
    • A1 for x < −3 or x > 4

    [3 marks] Show that 3(2x − 1) > 4x + 7 has the solution x > 5.

    1. Expand the bracket first.
    2. Then collect the x terms on one side and the numbers on the other.

    3(2x − 1) = 6x − 3, so 6x − 3 > 4x + 7. Subtract 4x: 2x − 3 > 7. Add 3: 2x > 10. Divide by 2: x > 5, as required.

    • M1 for expanding: 6x − 3 > 4x + 7
    • M1 for collecting terms: 2x > 10
    • C1 for dividing by 2 and stating x > 5

    Why it works

    • The balance still works. If one side of a scale is heavier, adding the same weight to both sides, or taking the same weight away, keeps it heavier. That is why adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing by a positive number never change the sign.
    • A negative flips the number line. Multiplying by −1 turns every number into its opposite, so the order reverses. 2 is left of 5, but −2 is right of −5. That is why you reverse the sign.
    • An inequality has many answers. An equation like x + 2 = 5 has one answer. x + 2 > 5 is true for a whole range, so we show it on a number line or write it as a range.
    • Circles say who is invited. A filled circle is a number that is allowed. An open circle marks a number that is not allowed: it is just outside the party.
    • Many quadratic graphs cross the axis twice. A U-shaped curve that crosses the x-axis twice is below the x-axis between its two crossing points and above it outside them. That is why one kind of answer is a single range, and the other is two separate ranges.

    Common traps

    • Forgetting to reverse the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative.
    • Reversing the sign when you only add or subtract a number.
    • Using an open circle for ≤ or ≥, or a filled one for < or >.
    • Listing whole-number solutions and leaving out zero or the negatives.
    • Including a boundary number that the sign leaves out (or leaving one out that it allows).
    • In a quadratic inequality, writing one range when the answer is two ranges, or the other way round.

    Tom says: “If x > y, then x² > y².” Show that Tom is wrong.

    1. To show a rule is wrong you only need one example that breaks it. Try a negative number for y.
    2. Pick x = 2 and a negative y that is further from zero.
