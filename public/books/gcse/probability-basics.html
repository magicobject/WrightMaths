Probability

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. Learn to work it out, add and multiply chances, draw trees and Venn diagrams, and then run it backwards: the chance something happens, and the chance it does not.

Where you will use this: the weather forecast, faulty parts in a batch from a supplier, the odds in a board game, and how many spare tiles a builder should order.

Probability
A number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means impossible and 1 means certain.
Outcome
One possible result of an experiment or event, such as heads when you toss a coin.
Tree diagram
A diagram of branches showing all possible outcomes of two or more events, with probabilities on the branches. Multiply along the branches, add between the end results you want.
Independent events
Events where one does not change the chance of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Writing probabilities

Write a probability as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: 1 over 4, 0.25 or 25%.

It is never less than 0 and never more than 1. If you get 1.2, something has gone wrong.

Give fractions in their simplest form unless the question says otherwise.

1. The probability scale

Something that could happen is called an Outcome. Probability says how likely an outcome is, on a scale from 0 to 1.

  • 0 means impossible. A normal dice never shows a 9.
  • 1 means certain. The sun rises tomorrow.
  • 1 over 2 means an even chance, like a fair coin landing heads.
  • Close to 0 means unlikely. Close to 1 means likely.

Key idea: a probability is a Fraction, a Decimal or a Percentage between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). It can never be negative and never be bigger than 1.

Try it: a forecast says 35% chance of rain. As a decimal that is 0.35. Is rain likely or unlikely?

Worked example: reading a forecast

A forecast says there is a 30% chance of rain tomorrow. Write this as a decimal and as a fraction, and say how likely rain is.

  1. 30% means 30 out of 100, so as a decimal it is 30 ÷ 100 = 0.3.
  2. As a fraction it is 30 over 100, which simplifies to 3 over 10.
  3. 0.3 is less than 0.5, so rain is unlikely. It is not impossible, because 0.3 is more than 0.

Now you finish it

  1. 85% = 85 ÷ 100 = ___.
  2. Is that more or less than 0.5? ___
  3. So a dry day is ___ (likely or unlikely).
Check my answer

85 ÷ 100 = 0.85. That is more than 0.5 and close to 1, so a dry day is likely.

2. Equally likely outcomes

Outcomes are equally likely when each has the same chance, like the faces of a fair dice. Then you count.

P(A) = number of ways A can happen ÷ total number of outcomes

Key idea: count the sectors, not the different numbers. The bottom of the fraction is how many sectors there are in total (8), and the top is how many are the ones you want.

Worked example

Using the spinner above, find P(3). Then find P(even).

  1. There are 8 equal sectors, so the bottom is 8.
  2. Three sectors show a 3, so P(3) = 3 over 8.
  3. Even numbers are 2, 2 and 4. That is 3 sectors, so P(even) = 3 over 8.

Two sectors show a 2, so P(2) = 2 over 8 = 1 over 4.

3. The chance it does not happen

Either something happens or it does not. Between them they cover every outcome, so their probabilities add up to 1.

P(not A) = 1 − P(A)

Key idea: all the probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to 1. If you know every probability except one, subtract the others from 1.

Worked example

The probability that it rains tomorrow is 0.35. What is the probability that it does not rain? A supplier says the probability a pipe fitting is faulty is 3 over 50. What is the probability it is not faulty?

  1. P(no rain) = 1 − 0.35 = 0.65.
  2. Write 1 as 50 over 50. P(not faulty) = 50 over 50 − 3 over 50 = 47 over 50.
  3. Check: 0.35 + 0.65 = 1 and 3 over 50 + 47 over 50 = 1. ✓

Now you finish it

  1. The bus is either late or on time, so the two probabilities add to ___.
  2. P(on time) = 1 − ___ = ___.
Check my answer

P(on time) = 1 − 0.12 = 0.88.

5. Relative frequency and expected outcomes

Sometimes outcomes are not equally likely, or you cannot count them: a faulty machine, a bent coin. Then you do an experiment and use what happened.

relative frequency = number of times it happened ÷ number of trials

The more trials you do, the better the estimate. The relative frequency is an estimate of the probability.

Once you know the probability, you can say how many times you expect something to happen:

expected number = probability × number of trials

Key idea: an expected number is an average, not a promise. A fair coin tossed 10 times is expected to give 5 heads, but 4 or 6 would not surprise anyone.

Worked example

A machine makes bolts. In a test of 500 bolts, 12 are faulty. Estimate the probability that a bolt is faulty. How many faulty bolts would you expect in a batch of 4000?

  1. Relative frequency = 12 over 500 = 0.024.
  2. Expected number = 0.024 × 4000 = 96.
  3. You would expect about 96 faulty bolts in the batch of 4000.

6. Adding chances: mutually exclusive outcomes

Two outcomes are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen at the same time. On one roll of a dice, “a 2” and “a 5” are mutually exclusive.

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

Key idea: you may only add the probabilities when the outcomes cannot happen together. “Even” and “bigger than 3” can both be true (4 and 6), so you cannot just add those.

Worked example

A bag has 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green counters. Find the probability of picking red or green.

  1. There are 10 counters. P(red) = 5 over 10 and P(green) = 2 over 10.
  2. A counter cannot be both red and green, so add: 5 over 10 + 2 over 10 = 7 over 10.
  3. Check: P(not blue) = 1 − 3 over 10 = 7 over 10. ✓

Now you finish it

Check my answer

7. Multiplying chances: independent events

10. Venn diagrams

  • That rounds too soon. 0.98 × 0.98 = 0.9604.

0.98 × 0.98 = 0.9604.

  • A1 for 0.9604

A merchant finds that 1 in 40 taps are returned. A plumber orders 400 taps. How many returns would you expect?

  1. 1 in 40 is the fraction 1 over 40.
  2. Find 1 over 40 of 400.
  • You multiplied by 40. One fortieth of 400 means divide.
  • Check: 1 over 40 of 400 is 400 ÷ 40 = 10.

1 over 40 × 400 = 10 returns.

[2 marks] A roofer needs two dry days in a row. The probability of rain on any day is 0.25, and the days are independent. What is the probability that both days are dry?

  1. First find the probability that one day is dry.
  2. Both days dry means dry and dry: multiply.
  • That is 0.25 × 0.25, two wet days. You need two dry days, 0.75 each.
  • That adds 0.75 + 0.75. For “both”, multiply.

P(dry) = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75. P(both dry) = 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.5625.

  3. Add the two answers.

  • A bag has 60 counters. Each counter is red, blue or green.
  • P(red) = 0.3 and P(blue) = 0.45.

[3 marks] Work out how many of the counters are green.

  1. The three probabilities add to 1. Find P(green) first.
  2. Then use: number = probability × total.
  3. Multiply P(green) by 60.

  • That is the red and blue counters together. Find P(green) = 0.25 first.

  • M1 for 0.25 × 60

[3 marks] In a class of 30 students, 17 play football, 12 play tennis and 7 play both. Show that the probability that a student chosen at random plays neither sport is 4 over 15.

  1. Football and tennis both count the 7 who play both. Be careful not to count them twice.
  2. Find how many play at least one sport, then take that from 30.

Football only = 17 − 7 = 10 and tennis only = 12 − 7 = 5, so the number playing at least one sport is 10 + 7 + 5 = 22 (or 17 + 12 − 7 = 22).

Neither: 30 − 22 = 8, so P(neither) = 8 over 30 = 4 over 15, as required.

  • M1 for 17 + 12 − 7 = 22 (or 10, 7 and 5 in a Venn diagram)
  • M1 for 30 − 22 = 8
  • C1 for 8 over 30 simplified to 4 over 15 stated as the answer

Higher [4 marks] A bag has 6 red and 4 blue counters. Two counters are taken at random without replacement. Work out the probability that the two counters are the same colour. Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5.

  1. Same colour means both red, or both blue.
  2. Work out each one, remembering that the bag changes after the first counter.
  3. Add the two probabilities, then simplify.
  • Right value, but it can be simplified. Divide the top and bottom by 6.
  • That is with replacement. Without replacement the second pick is out of 9 counters.

Both red: 6 over 10 × 5 over 9 = 30 over 90. Both blue: 4 over 10 × 3 over 9 = 12 over 90. Add: 30 over 90 + 12 over 90 = 42 over 90 = 7 over 15.

  • M1 for 6 over 10 × 5 over 9
  • M1 for 4 over 10 × 3 over 9
  • M1 for adding the two products
  • A1 for 7 over 15

[3 marks] A factory tests 250 light switches and 10 of them fail. Use this to estimate how many of the next 5000 switches will fail.

  1. Estimate the probability of failure from the test.
  2. Relative frequency = failures ÷ switches tested.
  3. Multiply that probability by 5000.
  • That is the number you would expect in 500 switches. You have 5000.
  • You turned the fraction upside down. 10 over 250 is the probability, which is small.

Relative frequency = 10 over 250 = 0.04. Expected number = 0.04 × 5000 = 200.

  • M1 for 10 over 250 or 0.04
  • M1 for 0.04 × 5000
  • A1 for 200

Why it works

  • Probability is a fraction of the outcomes. If every outcome is equally likely, the chance of A is just the share of outcomes that are A. That is why it can never be more than 1.
  • P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Every outcome is either A or not A, and together they make up the whole, which is 1.
  • “Or” adds, “and” multiplies. For outcomes that cannot overlap, the shares just stack up. For independent events, you take a share of a share: half of a sixth is a twelfth.
  • Without replacement changes the bag. A tree diagram stays honest because each branch uses what is actually left.
  • More trials means a better estimate. Relative frequency settles down close to the true probability as the number of trials grows.

Common traps

  • Giving a probability bigger than 1 or smaller than 0.
  • Putting the wanted outcomes on the bottom instead of the total.
  • Counting different numbers instead of different sectors or outcomes.
  • Adding for “and”, or multiplying for “or”.
  • Adding probabilities for outcomes that can happen together, and counting the overlap twice.
  • Forgetting that HT and TH are different outcomes.
  • Forgetting to change the bottom (and sometimes the top) when picking without replacement.
  • Thinking a coin is “due” to change after a run of heads.

Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the first dice shows a bigger number than the second? Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form, like 3/5. (Hint: how many of the 36 pairs have the same number on both?)

  1. Count the pairs where both dice show the same number.
  2. In the other pairs, the first dice is bigger about as often as the second is. Split them fairly.
  3. How many pairs are left after the doubles are taken out? Halve it.
  • Some pairs have equal numbers, and then the first dice is not bigger. So it is less than a half.
  • That is the chance they are equal. Split the other 30 pairs evenly between the two cases.

There are 36 pairs. In 6 of them the numbers are equal. In the other 30 pairs, the first dice is bigger in exactly as many as the second dice is, so 15 each. P = 15 over 36 = 5 over 12.

A bag has red and blue counters. A counter is taken, its colour is noted, and it is put back. This is done twice. The probability of two reds is 9 over 25. What is the probability of a red on a single pick? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/5.

  1. Because the counter is put back, P(red) is the same each time. Call it p.
  2. Two reds means p × p = 9 over 25. What number multiplied by itself gives 9? And 25?
  3. Take the square root of the top and the bottom.
  • That is the chance of two reds. You need the chance of one red: the number that multiplies by itself to give 9 over 25.
  • That is bigger than 1. Take the square root of 9 and of 25 separately.

With replacement the picks are independent, so P(two reds) = p × p = 9 over 25. The square root of 9 is 3 and the square root of 25 is 5, so p = 3 over 5. Check: 3 over 5 × 3 over 5 = 9 over 25.

A fair dice is rolled three times. What is the probability of at least one six? Give your answer as a fraction, like 3/5. (A tempting wrong answer is 1 over 6 + 1 over 6 + 1 over 6 = 1 over 2. Why does it fail?)

  1. “At least one six” is everything except “no sixes at all”.
  2. P(not a six on one roll) = 5 over 6. Three rolls, independent: multiply.
  3. Take your answer away from 1.
  • Adding counts the cases with two or three sixes more than once. Use 1 − P(no sixes).
  • That is the chance of no sixes at all. Take it away from 1.

P(no sixes) = 5 over 6 × 5 over 6 × 5 over 6 = 125 over 216. So P(at least one six) = 1 − 125 over 216 = 91 over 216. Adding 1 over 6 three times fails because a roll sequence with two sixes is counted more than once: the events are not mutually exclusive.
