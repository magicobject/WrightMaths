    Averages and spread

    An average is one number that stands for a whole set of data, and the range tells you how spread out the data are. Learn the mean, median, mode and range, read charts and scatter graphs, spot a misleading graph, and then run it backwards: finding the mean, and finding a missing value from the mean.

    Where you will use this: comparing two players or two suppliers, working out a typical wage or house price, a builder's mean weekly spend, and checking whether a newspaper graph is fair.

    1. Mean, median, mode and range

    An Average is one number that stands for the whole set of data. There are three of them, and each one asks a different question. A fourth number, the range, tells you about the spread.

    • The Mean is the “fair share”. Add up all the values, then divide by how many there are.
    • The Median is the middle value once the data are in order from smallest to largest. With two middle values, go halfway between them.
    • The Mode is the value that appears most often. There can be more than one mode. If every value appears once, there is no mode.
    • The Range is the biggest value minus the smallest. It is a measure of spread, not an average.

    mean = total of the values ÷ number of values

    Key idea: to find the median, put the numbers in order from smallest to largest first. With n values, the middle one is in position (n + 1) ÷ 2. With 5 values, that is the 3rd. With 6 values, it is halfway between the 3rd and the 4th.

    Worked example: five values

    Find the mean, median, mode and range of 12, 7, 9, 7, 5.

    1. Mean: 12 + 7 + 9 + 7 + 5 = 40, and 40 ÷ 5 = 8.
    2. Median: put them in order: 5, 7, 7, 9, 12. The middle (3rd) value is 7.
    3. Mode: 7 appears twice and the others once, so the mode is 7.
    4. Range: 12 − 5 = 7.

    Worked example: six values

    Find the median and mean of 3, 8, 5, 10, 4, 6.

    1. In order: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10. There are 6 values, so the median is halfway between the 3rd and 4th.
    2. The 3rd is 5 and the 4th is 6. Halfway: (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5.
    3. Mean: 3 + 8 + 5 + 10 + 4 + 6 = 36, and 36 ÷ 6 = 6.
    4. There is no mode, because every value appears once.

    2. Frequency tables

    When the same value appears many times, a frequency table counts it instead of listing every one. The frequency (f) is how many times each value (x) happens.

    Goals scored in 20 matches
    Goals (x)01234
    Frequency (f)45632
    f × x051298
    • Total frequency: add the f row. This is how many values there are: 4 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 20.
    • Mean: add up each value times its frequency (the f × x row), then divide by the total frequency.
    • Mode: the value with the biggest frequency. Give the goals (2), not the frequency (6).
    • Median: find the middle position, then count along the frequencies to see which value is there.

    Key idea: mean = (total of f × x) ÷ (total frequency). The mode is the value with the biggest frequency, not the frequency itself.

    Worked example

    Use the table to find the mean, mode and median number of goals.

    1. f × x: 0, 5, 12, 9, 8. Their total is 0 + 5 + 12 + 9 + 8 = 34.
    2. Total frequency = 20. Mean = 34 ÷ 20 = 1.7 goals.
    3. The biggest frequency is 6, for 2 goals. The mode is 2 goals.
    4. Median: 20 values, so the middle is between the 10th and 11th. Counting along: 4 values are 0, then 5 more are 1 (that reaches 9), then 6 values are 2 (reaching 15). The 10th and 11th are both 2, so the median is 2 goals.

    Now you finish it

    Pets per household in a sample of 20 homes: 0 pets in 5 homes, 1 pet in 8 homes, 2 pets in 4 homes, 3 pets in 3 homes. Find the mean number of pets.

    1. f × x: 0 × 5 = 0, 1 × 8 = ___, 2 × 4 = ___, 3 × 3 = ___.
    2. Total of f × x = ___. Total frequency = 20.
    3. Mean = ___ ÷ 20 = ___.
    Check my answer

    f × x: 0, 8, 8, 9. The total is 25 and the total frequency is 20, so the mean is 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25 pets.

    3. Choosing the best average

    No average is always best. Pick the one that fits the data and the question.

    Which average to use
    AverageGood forWatch out
    MeanData that are fairly even, when you want every value to countOne very big or very small value (an outlier) pulls it up or down
    MedianData with an outlier, such as wages or house pricesIt ignores how big the highest and lowest values are
    ModeThings you can only count, such as colours or shoe sizesThere may be no mode or several
    RangeSaying how spread out or consistent the data areIt uses only two values, so one outlier changes it a lot

    Key idea: an outlier is a value far away from the rest. It can drag the mean a long way but barely moves the median.

    Worked example

    Five workers earn £24 000, £25 000, £26 000, £27 000 and £98 000 a year. Which average describes a typical worker better, the mean or the median?

    1. Mean: the total is 24 000 + 25 000 + 26 000 + 27 000 + 98 000 = 200 000, so the mean is 200 000 ÷ 5 = £40 000.
    2. Median: the middle of the ordered list is £26 000.
    3. Four of the five workers earn between 24 000 and 27 000. The £98 000 is an outlier that drags the mean up, so the median is the better description.

    Now you finish it

    A shoe shop wants to know which size to order the most of. Which average should it use, and why?

    1. The shop needs the size that sells most often.
    2. That is the ___ (mean, median or mode).
    Check my answer

    The mode, because it is the size bought most often. The mean (such as 6.3) might not even be a real shoe size.

    2. Add 50 to that total, then divide by 6.

    • That is the new total. Divide it by 6.

    4. Comparing two sets of data

    To compare two sets, use two things: an average (to see which set is higher) and the range (to see which is more consistent). A smaller range means more consistent.

    Key idea: write your comparison in words, in the context of the question. Say “on average, A is higher” and “B is more consistent because its range is smaller”. Numbers on their own do not answer a “compare” question.

    Worked example

    Two goalkeepers made these saves in five matches. Anna: 5, 6, 7, 6, 6. Ben: 2, 10, 4, 9, 5. Compare them.

    1. Anna: total 30, mean 6, range 7 − 5 = 2.
    2. Ben: total 30, mean 6, range 10 − 2 = 8.
    3. Both have the same mean, so on average they are equally good. Anna’s range is smaller, so Anna is more consistent.

    Now you finish it

    Bus route X has a median journey time of 8 minutes and a range of 4 minutes. Route Y has a median of 6 minutes and a range of 12 minutes. Compare them.

    1. Which route is quicker on average? Route ___ (smaller median).
    2. Which route is more consistent? Route ___ (smaller range).
    Check my answer

    Route Y is quicker on average (median 6 < 8), but route X is more reliable because its range (4) is smaller than Y’s range (12).

    5. Bar charts, pictograms and pie charts

    Charts turn a frequency table into a picture. Read them carefully: check the title, the labels and the scale before you read any numbers.

    • A bar chart uses the height of a bar for the frequency. Read across from the top of the bar to the scale.
    • A pictogram uses symbols. The key tells you what one symbol stands for. A half symbol is half of that.
    • A pie chart shows shares of a whole. The angles add up to 360°.

    Key idea: on a pie chart, work out how many degrees one item gets: 360° ÷ total number of items. Then angle = frequency × degrees per item. To read a pie chart backwards, divide the angle by the degrees per item.

    Worked example: drawing the angles

    A pet survey of 40 children: cat 15, dog 20, rabbit 5. Work out the pie chart angle for each pet.

    1. The whole pie is 360° and there are 40 children, so each child gets 360 ÷ 40 = 9°.
    2. Cat: 15 × 9 = 135°. Dog: 20 × 9 = 180°. Rabbit: 5 × 9 = 45°.
    3. Check: 135 + 180 + 45 = 360. ✓

    Now you finish it

    30 pupils chose a favourite subject: Maths 10, Art 5, PE 15. Work out the pie chart angle for each subject.

    1. Each pupil gets 360 ÷ 30 = ___°.
    2. Maths: 10 × ___ = ___°. Art: 5 × ___ = ___°. PE: 15 × ___ = ___°.
    3. Check that the angles add up to ___.
    Check my answer

    360 ÷ 30 = 12° per pupil. Maths 120°, Art 60°, PE 180°. 120 + 60 + 180 = 360.

    6. Scatter graphs

    A scatter graph plots two measurements for each person or thing, such as hours of revision and test score. It shows whether the two are linked. That link is called Correlation.

    • Positive correlation: as one goes up, the other goes up too. The points slope up to the right.
    • Negative correlation: as one goes up, the other goes down. The points slope down to the right.
    • No correlation: the points are scattered with no pattern.

    A Line of best fit is a straight line drawn through the middle of the points, with about as many points above it as below. It does not have to go through the first point or the origin. Use a ruler.

    Key idea: read an estimate from the line of best fit by going up from the x-value to the line, then across to the y-axis. Estimates inside the data (interpolation) are fairly reliable. Estimates far outside the data (extrapolation) are not, because the pattern may stop. Correlation does not prove one thing causes the other.

    Worked example

    Use the line of best fit to estimate the test score of a student who revised for 4 hours.

    1. Find 4 on the horizontal axis and go up to the line.
    2. Go across to the vertical axis and read the score. The line is at about 54.
    3. This is an estimate, because the line is only a best fit. A real student might score a bit higher or lower.

    7. Spotting a misleading graph

    Graphs in adverts and the news can be drawn to make a small difference look huge, or to hide one. Check these before you believe a graph.

    Text version of the picture, for people who cannot see it: A bar chart of sales at two shops. The vertical axis is sales in hundreds. It starts at 44, not 0, and goes up to 54 in steps of 2. Shop A reaches 48 and Shop B reaches 52.

    • The vertical axis does not start at 0. The bars are cut off, so a small difference looks big (as above).
    • The scale jumps unevenly, so the steps are not equal in size.
    • No title, labels or units, so you cannot tell what the graph shows.
    • Different symbol sizes in a pictogram, or bars of different widths, which change how big things look.
    • A pie chart with no total, so you cannot compare sizes of two pie charts.
    • A short time window chosen to make a trend look steeper than it is.

    Key idea: look at the numbers on the scale, not just the shape. If the axis does not start at 0, compare the actual values, not the heights of the bars.

    Worked example

    In the graph above, Shop A sold 4800 and Shop B sold 5200. Really, by what percentage did Shop B’s sales exceed Shop A’s?

    1. Difference: 52 − 48 = 4 (hundred).
    2. Compare it with the original, Shop A: 4 ÷ 48 = 0.0833… ≈ 8%.
    3. Shop B sold only about 8% more, but its bar looks about twice as tall as Shop A’s because the axis starts at 44.

    Flip it: run it backwards

    So far you knew the values and found the mean. Now go the other way: you know the mean, and you find a missing value.

    Forwards

    Values 5, 6, 9, 8 are known. Mean = ?

    Add them (28) and divide by 4. The mean is 7.

    Backwards

    The mean of 4 numbers is 7. Three are 5, 6, 9. What is the fourth?

    Total needed = 4 × 7 = 28. The known three add to 20, so the fourth is 8.

    Key idea: mean × number of values = total. So if you know the mean, the total is the mean times the number of values. Subtract the values you already know to get the missing one.

    Worked example

    Jo’s mean mark after 4 tests is 62. What must she score in the 5th test to bring her mean up to 65?

    2. Total so far, from 4 tests: 4 × 62 = 248.
    3. The 5th test must be worth 325 − 248 = 77 marks.
    4. Check: (248 + 77) ÷ 5 = 325 ÷ 5 = 65. ✓

    Why it works

    • The mean is a fair share. If every value were the same, what would it have to be to give the same total? Adding and dividing finds that. It is why mean × number of values = total.
    • The median ignores how big the values are. It only cares about order, so one huge value cannot drag it far.
    • The mode only counts. That makes it the only average that works for colours and other things that are not numbers.
    • The range shows spread. Two sets can share a mean but have very different ranges. You need both to compare them.
    • Midpoints are a fair guess. In a grouped table, high and low values in a class tend to balance, so the midpoint is a good stand-in. It is still only an estimate.

    Common traps

    • Finding the median without putting the numbers in order from smallest to largest first.
    • Dividing by the wrong number when finding a mean (the count of values, not the largest value).
    • Giving the frequency instead of the value when asked for the mode.
    • Forgetting to multiply by the frequency when finding the mean from a table.
    • Writing the range as “3 to 12” instead of one number.
    • Believing a bar chart without checking where the scale starts.
    • Saying a scatter graph proves one thing causes the other.
    • Using a line of best fit far outside the data.

    Going further

    10 students in class A have a mean score of 12. 15 students in class B have a mean score of 16. What is the mean score of all 25 students together? (The answer is not 14, the mean of 12 and 16. Why?)

    1. Find the total score for each class using mean × number of values.
    2. Add the two totals together.
    3. Divide by the total number of students, 25.
    • That is the mean of 12 and 16. Class B is bigger than class A, so it counts for more.
    • That is 12 + 16. Find the total scores first, then divide by 25 students.

    Class A total: 10 × 12 = 120. Class B total: 15 × 16 = 240. All together: 360 for 25 students. Mean = 360 ÷ 25 = 14.4. It is closer to 16 than to 12 because there are more students in class B.

    Five whole numbers, in order from smallest to largest, have mode 4, median 5, mean 6 and range 6. What is the largest number?

    1. The mode is 4, so 4 appears at least twice. With the median 5, where must the 4s be?
    2. The total is 5 × 6 = 30, and the range tells you how far the largest is from the smallest.
    • That is the fourth number. The largest is the smallest plus the range.
    • That is the total of the two biggest numbers. Use the range to find the biggest one.

    The median (3rd number) is 5, and the mode 4 needs two 4s, so the first two numbers are 4 and 4. The range is 6, so the largest is 4 + 6 = 10. The total is 30, so the 4th number is 30 − (4 + 4 + 5 + 10) = 7. The numbers are 4, 4, 5, 7, 10.

    Five numbers have a mean of 8. Every number is multiplied by 3 and then 2 is added to each. What is the new mean? (Does the range change in the same way?)

    1. The total of the five numbers is 40. See what happens to the total.
    2. Multiplying every number by 3 multiplies the total by 3. Adding 2 to five numbers adds 10 to the total.
    3. Divide the new total by 5.
    • That only multiplies by 3. Each number also has 2 added, which adds 2 to the mean.
    • That only adds 2. Each number is also multiplied by 3.

    Total = 5 × 8 = 40. After × 3 the total is 120. Adding 2 to each of 5 numbers adds 10, so the new total is 130 and the new mean is 130 ÷ 5 = 26. This is 3 × 8 + 2 = 26: the same operations as on each number. The range is not changed the same way: adding 2 to every value leaves the range alone, and multiplying by 3 triples it.
