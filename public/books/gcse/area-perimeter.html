    Area and perimeter

    Perimeter is the distance round the edge of a shape. Area is the flat space inside it. Learn the area formulas, pick the right units, move up to volume, and then run it all backwards to find a missing length.

    Where you will use this: tiles and flooring for a bathroom, paint for a wall, turf for a lawn, and concrete for a base.

    Perimeter
    The distance all the way round the edge of a shape.
    Area
    The amount of flat space inside a shape. It is measured in square units such as cm².
    Volume
    The amount of space inside a solid. It is measured in cubic units such as cm³ and m³.
    Perpendicular height
    The height of a shape measured at a right angle to its base. It is not the length of a slanting side.

    Units and money

    Length: cm, m. Area: cm², m². Volume: cm³, m³.

    1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m² = 10 000 cm². 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³, and 1000 cm³ = 1 litre.

    Round money to 2 decimal places. Round UP when you buy things by the box, tin or length.

    1. Perimeter: the distance round the edge

    The Perimeter of a shape is the distance all the way round its edge. Imagine walking round the edge of a field. The length of your walk is the perimeter.

    To find it, add up the lengths of all the sides. It is a length, so the units are cm, m and so on.

    rectangle: perimeter = 2 × (length + width)    square: perimeter = 4 × side

    Key idea: perimeter is the distance round the outside, so it is a LENGTH. Count every side, including the ones the picture does not label (opposite sides of a rectangle are equal).

    Try it: a square has sides of 6 cm. Its perimeter is 4 × 6.

    Worked example

    Find the perimeter of a rectangle 9 cm long and 4 cm wide.

    1. The opposite sides are equal, so the four sides are 9, 4, 9 and 4.
    2. 9 + 4 + 9 + 4 = 26.
    3. The perimeter is 26 cm. (Or: 2 × (9 + 4) = 2 × 13 = 26.)

    2. Area of rectangles and squares

    The Area of a shape is the amount of flat space inside it. We measure it by counting squares. A square that is 1 cm by 1 cm has an area of 1 square centimetre, written 1 cm².

    The grid has 3 rows of 5 squares, so you do not need to count one by one: 5 × 3 gives the number of squares.

    rectangle: area = length × width    square: area = side × side

    Key idea: area is measured in SQUARE units: cm², m². Perimeter is a length (cm, m). Two shapes can have the same perimeter and different areas.

    Try 2 cm by 6 cm and 4 cm by 4 cm. Both have a perimeter of 16 cm. The first has area 12 cm². The second has area 16 cm².

    Worked example

    Find the area of a rectangle 12 cm long and 7 cm wide.

    1. Area = length × width.
    2. 12 × 7 = 84.
    3. The area is 84 cm². (The units are square units because we multiplied two lengths.)

    Worked example: a square

    A square paving slab has a side of 0.6 m. Find its area.

    1. A square has area side × side.
    2. 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36.
    3. The area is 0.36 m².

    3. Triangles, parallelograms and trapezia

    The same idea works for other flat shapes, with one big warning. For these shapes you need the Perpendicular height: the height measured at right angles to the base. It is NOT the slanting side.

    Triangle

    A triangle is half of a rectangle (or half of a parallelogram) with the same base and height.

    triangle: area = ½ × base × height    A = 1 over 2bh

    Parallelogram

    A Parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides. Cut a triangle off one end and slide it to the other end and you get a rectangle. So the area is base × perpendicular height.

    parallelogram: area = base × height    A = bh

    Trapezium

    A Trapezium (plural: trapezia) has one pair of parallel sides. Add the two parallel sides, halve the total, then multiply by the height between them.

    trapezium: area = 1 over 2 × (a + b) × h    where a and b are the parallel sides

    Key idea: always use the PERPENDICULAR height (the dashed line in the pictures, at a right angle to the base). The slanting sides are there to catch you out.

    Use the formula: write it, substitute, work it out

    To Substitute means to put numbers in place of letters. Write the Formula first, then replace each letter with its value, then work it out. Keep the units for the end.

    Worked example: triangle

    A triangle has a base of 9 cm and a perpendicular height of 4 cm. Its slanting sides are 5 cm and 7.2 cm. Find the area.

    1. Formula: A = 1 over 2 × b × h.
    2. Substitute: A = 1 over 2 × 9 × 4. The slanting sides are not needed.
    3. 9 × 4 = 36 and half of 36 is 18.
    4. The area is 18 cm².

    Worked example: parallelogram

    A parallelogram has a base of 9 cm and a perpendicular height of 4 cm. The slanting side is 5 cm. Find the area.

    1. Formula: A = b × h.
    2. Substitute: A = 9 × 4 = 36.
    3. The area is 36 cm². The slanting side, 5 cm, is not used.

    Worked example: trapezium

    A trapezium has parallel sides of 6 cm and 10 cm. The height between them is 4 cm. Find the area.

    1. Formula: A = 1 over 2 × (a + b) × h.
    2. Substitute: A = 1 over 2 × (6 + 10) × 4.
    3. Brackets first: 6 + 10 = 16. Half of 16 is 8. Then 8 × 4 = 32.
    4. The area is 32 cm².

    4. Compound shapes

    A Compound shape is made from simpler shapes joined together. There are two ways to find its area.

    • Split it into rectangles, triangles and so on. Find each area, then add.
    • Subtract. Imagine a bigger rectangle round it. Take away the missing part.

    For the perimeter, add every edge. If some edges are not labelled, work them out from the sides that are. Opposite sides of a straight-sided shape with right angles must add up to the same total.

    Worked example: an L-shape

    Find the area and the perimeter of this shape. All the corners are right angles.

    1. Area, by splitting. The tall part is 6 cm by 8 cm: 6 × 8 = 48. The bottom right part is 4 cm by 5 cm: 4 × 5 = 20.
    2. 48 + 20 = 68, so the area is 68 cm².
    3. Missing sides: the bottom is 10 and the top is 6, so the step across is 10 − 6 = 4. The left is 8 and the right is 5, so the step down is 8 − 5 = 3.
    4. Perimeter: 10 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 8 = 36 cm. Check: the outer rectangle is 10 by 8, and 2 × (10 + 8) = 36. ✓

    5. Units: cm² and m²

    A big warning: 1 m² is NOT 100 cm². A square metre is a square that is 1 m by 1 m. In centimetres, that is 100 cm by 100 cm.

    1 m² = 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm²

    Key idea: to change m² to cm², multiply by 10 000. To change cm² to m², divide by 10 000. Or avoid the problem: change all the lengths to the same unit BEFORE you multiply.

    Worked example: change the lengths first

    A rectangle is 2 m long and 50 cm wide. Find its area in m².

    1. The units differ. Change 50 cm to 0.5 m.
    2. 2 × 0.5 = 1.
    3. The area is 1 m². (In cm: 200 × 50 = 10 000 cm², the same thing.)

    Worked example: change the answer

    Change 35 000 cm² to m².

    1. To go from cm² to m², divide by 10 000.
    2. 35 000 ÷ 10 000 = 3.5.
    3. 35 000 cm² = 3.5 m².

    6. Area and money

    In real jobs you find an area to work out how much to buy. Tiles, flooring, paint and turf are all sold by area. Follow the steps: find the area, then divide or multiply, then think about what you can actually buy.

    • Priced per m²: cost = area × price per m².
    • Sold in boxes or packs: number needed = area ÷ cover of one box. You cannot buy part of a box, so round UP.
    • Several coats: the area is covered once for each coat.

    Key idea: round UP when you buy things in whole boxes or tins, even if the next digit is small. Rounding down leaves you short.

    Worked example: tiles

    A bathroom floor is 4.5 m by 3.2 m. Tiles come in boxes that cover 1.5 m² each and cost £32.50 a box. How much will the tiles cost?

    1. Area = 4.5 × 3.2 = 14.4 m².
    2. Boxes: 14.4 ÷ 1.5 = 9.6. You cannot buy 0.6 of a box, so round up to 10 boxes.
    3. Cost: 10 × £32.50 = £325.00.

    7. Volume and surface area

    Volume is the amount of space inside a solid. It is measured in cubic units: cm³, m³. A cube 1 cm by 1 cm by 1 cm has a volume of 1 cm³.

    A Cuboid is a box shape. A Prism is a solid with the same Cross-section all the way along, such as a cuboid, a triangular tent or a pipe.

    cuboid: volume = length × width × height    prism: volume = area of cross-section × length

    The cuboid is a prism too. Its cross-section is a rectangle, and rectangle area × length gives the same answer.

    The Surface area is the total area of all the faces. A cuboid has 6 faces, in 3 matching pairs: top and bottom (l × w), front and back (l × h), and the two ends (w × h).

    cuboid: surface area = 2 × (lw + lh + wh)

    Key idea: volume = cross-section × length is the key to every prism. First the area of the flat end, then the length. And 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³. Also 1000 cm³ = 1 litre.

    Worked example: cuboid

    A cuboid is 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm. Find its volume and its surface area.

    1. Volume = 8 × 5 × 3 = 120 cm³.
    2. The three face pairs: lw = 8 × 5 = 40, lh = 8 × 3 = 24, wh = 5 × 3 = 15.
    3. Surface area = 2 × (40 + 24 + 15) = 2 × 79 = 158 cm².

    Flip it: run it backwards

    So far you know the lengths and find the area. Now go the other way: you know the area (or the perimeter or the volume) and one length, and you find the missing length.

    Forwards

    length 9, width 6, area = ?

    Multiply: 9 × 6 = 54.

    Backwards

    area 54, width 6, length = ?

    Divide: 54 ÷ 6 = 9. Use the Inverse operation.

    Key idea: write the formula with the numbers you know, then ask: what must I multiply by 6 to get 54? Undo the multiplication with a division. Then check by going forwards.

    Worked example: a missing side

    A rectangle has an area of 54 cm² and a width of 6 cm. Find its length.

    3. The length is 9 cm.
    4. Check forwards: 9 × 6 = 54. ✓

    Worked example: a missing height

    A triangle has an area of 24 cm² and a base of 8 cm. Find its perpendicular height.

    Why it works

    • Area counts squares. A rectangle 5 by 3 holds 3 rows of 5 squares, so 5 × 3 = 15. Multiplying is a quick way of counting equal rows.
    • A triangle is half of a rectangle. Draw a rectangle round a right-angled triangle and a diagonal cuts it into two equal triangles. So the triangle has half the area.
    • A parallelogram is a rectangle in disguise. Cut a triangle off one end, slide it to the other end, and you have a rectangle with the same base and height.
    • A trapezium is half a parallelogram. Two copies of a trapezium, one turned upside down, fit together into a parallelogram with base (a + b) and the same height h. Its area is (a + b) × h, so one trapezium is half of that.
    • Square units change by 10 000, not 100. A square metre is 100 rows of 100 square centimetres.
    • Prisms stack layers. Each layer of the cross-section has the same area, and the length tells you how many layers there are.

    Common traps

    • Using the slanting side instead of the perpendicular height.
    • Forgetting the half for a triangle or a trapezium.
    • Mixing up area and perimeter, or writing cm instead of cm².
    • Saying 1 m² = 100 cm². It is 10 000 cm².
    • Rounding down when buying boxes, tins or tiles.
    • Leaving out the unlabelled sides when you add up a perimeter.

    • That rounds down, and 5 lengths (15 m) would be less than the 16.2 m needed.

    All round: 2 × (5 + 3.6) = 17.2 m. Take off the door: 17.2 − 1 = 16.2 m. 16.2 ÷ 3 = 5.4, so round up to 6 lengths.

    A concrete base for a boiler store is 3 m by 2.5 m and 0.1 m thick. Ready-mixed concrete costs £130 per m³. How much does the concrete cost? Type it like 12.50.

    1. Volume = length × width × thickness.
    2. Then multiply the volume by the price per m³.
    • That is the volume in m³. Multiply by £130 to get the cost.
    • That looks like using 1 m for the thickness instead of 0.1 m.

    Volume = 3 × 2.5 × 0.1 = 0.75 m³. Cost = 0.75 × 130 = 97.50, so £97.50.

    [3 marks] A plasterer boards one side of a stud wall (a wall made from a wooden frame) 4.8 m long and 2.4 m high. Plasterboard sheets are 2.4 m by 1.2 m and cost £9.80 each. Work out the cost of the sheets. Type it like 12.50.

    2. Divide to find the number of sheets.
    3. Then multiply by the price of one sheet.

    A 5-a-side pitch (for teams of five players) is a rectangle 25 m long and 15 m wide. What is the perimeter of the pitch, in metres?

    1. Look at the picture. How many sides does a rectangle have, and which are equal?
    2. Add up all four sides, or add the length and width and double it.
    3. Check the sides: 25, 15, 25, 15.
    • That is the area (25 × 15). The perimeter is the distance round the edge.
    • That is only half way round (25 + 15). Double it for the whole perimeter.

    25 + 15 + 25 + 15 = 80 m. (Or 2 × (25 + 15) = 2 × 40 = 80 m.)

    The same pitch is 25 m by 15 m. What is its area, in square metres?

    1. Is the question asking about the edge or the space inside?
    2. The area of a rectangle is length × width.
    3. Work out 25 × 15. Try 25 × 10 and 25 × 5.
    • That is the perimeter. The area is the space inside, length × width.
    • That adds the sides. Multiply them for the area.

    25 × 15 = 250 + 125 = 375 m².

    Artificial turf costs £6.40 per square metre. The pitch is 25 m by 15 m. How many pounds does it cost to cover the whole pitch? Type it like 12.50.

    1. What do you need to find first, before you can use the price per square metre?
    2. Find the area of the pitch.
    3. Multiply the area by the cost of 1 m².
    • That is the cost of the perimeter, 80 × 6.40. Turf covers the area, not the edge.
    • That is the area. Multiply it by the cost per square metre.

    Area = 25 × 15 = 375 m². Cost = 375 × 6.40 = £2400.00.

    [3 marks] The pitch is 25 m by 15 m. Each end has a rectangular goal area 10 m wide and 4 m deep, painted a different colour. What is the area of the pitch outside the two goal areas, in square metres?

    2. Work out the area of the whole pitch, then the area of one goal area.
    3. Take away both goal areas.
