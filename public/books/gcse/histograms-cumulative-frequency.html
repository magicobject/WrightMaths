    Histograms, cumulative frequency and box plots

    Once data are grouped in classes you can still draw them, read the middle and the spread from a running-total curve, and compare two sets of data with a box plot. Learn to draw each graph from a table, and to read a table back from each graph.

    Where you will use this: checking that nine in ten cable cuts are the right length, comparing how quickly two plumbers arrive, and reading a wait-time report at a trade counter.

    Cumulative frequency
    A running total of the frequencies: how many values there are up to and including the end of each class.
    Interquartile range
    The upper quartile minus the lower quartile. It is the spread of the middle half of the data, so it ignores very high and very low values.
    Box plot
    A diagram of five numbers: the smallest value, lower quartile, median, upper quartile and largest value. The box shows the middle half of the data.
    Frequency density
    Frequency divided by class width. It is the height of a histogram bar.

    3. Reading the median, quartiles and percentiles

    With n values in total, the curve tells you where the key values are. Read across from the height on the vertical axis, then down to the horizontal axis.

    • The Median is at height n ÷ 2.
    • The lower Quartile is at height n ÷ 4 and the upper quartile is at height 3n ÷ 4.
    • The interquartile range (IQR) = upper quartile − lower quartile. It is the spread of the middle half of the data.
    • A percentile splits the data into hundredths. The 90th percentile is at height 90% of n.

    Key idea: the height tells you where to read, and the answer is the value along the bottom. To find how many values are below a given value, do it the other way round: go up from the value, then across.

    Worked example: median and quartiles

    There are 80 delivery times. Estimate the median, the quartiles and the interquartile range.

    1. Median: 80 ÷ 2 = 40. Across at 40 and down: about 29 minutes.
    2. Lower quartile: 80 ÷ 4 = 20. Across at 20 and down: about 22 minutes.
    3. Upper quartile: 3 × 80 ÷ 4 = 60. Across at 60 and down: about 37 minutes.
    4. Interquartile range = 37 − 22 = 15 minutes.

    Worked example: how many?

    Estimate how many of the 80 deliveries took more than 45 minutes.

    1. Go up from 45 minutes to the curve and across: about 72.
    2. So about 72 deliveries took 45 minutes or less.
    3. More than 45 minutes: 80 − 72 = 8.

    4. Box plots

    A box plot shows five numbers on one scale: the smallest value, the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and the largest value. The box runs from the lower quartile to the upper quartile, with a line at the median. The lines out of the box (the whiskers) go to the smallest and largest values. Each quarter of the data lies between two neighbouring numbers.

    From a list. Put the values in order. With 7 or 11 values, the median is the middle one, the lower quartile is the middle of the lower half, and the upper quartile is the middle of the upper half.

    Key idea: the box shows the middle half of the data, and its width is the interquartile range. The range is the distance between the ends of the whiskers.

    Worked example: from a list

    Find the five numbers for 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 19, 23.

    1. There are 11 values, already in order. The smallest is 4 and the largest is 23.
    2. The median is the 6th value: 12.
    3. The lower half is 4, 6, 7, 9, 10. Its middle (3rd) value is 7, so the lower quartile is 7.
    4. The upper half is 13, 15, 18, 19, 23. Its middle value is 18, so the upper quartile is 18.
    5. The five numbers are 4, 7, 12, 18, 23, as in the box plot above.

    5. Outliers

    An Outlier is a value far away from the rest. One common rule says a value is an outlier if it is more than 1.5 × the interquartile range above the upper quartile, or below the lower quartile. In an exam you will be told the rule to use. On a box plot an outlier is shown as a cross, and the whisker stops at the biggest or smallest value that is not an outlier.

    Key idea: the upper limit is upper quartile + 1.5 × IQR, and the lower limit is lower quartile − 1.5 × IQR. A value beyond a limit is an outlier.

    Worked example

    The quartiles are 30 and 46. Are 5 and 82 outliers (rule: 1.5 × IQR)?

    1. IQR = 46 − 30 = 16, and 1.5 × 16 = 24.
    2. Lower limit: 30 − 24 = 6. Upper limit: 46 + 24 = 70.
    3. 5 is less than 6, so it is an outlier. 82 is more than 70, so it is an outlier too.

    [4 marks] A data set has a lower quartile of 24 and an upper quartile of 40. A value is an outlier if it is more than 1.5 × the interquartile range above the upper quartile. Show that a value of 70 is an outlier.

    1. Find the interquartile range first.
    2. Then work out the upper limit and compare 70 with it.

    Interquartile range = 40 − 24 = 16. 1.5 × 16 = 24. Upper limit = 40 + 24 = 64. Since 70 is more than 64, it is an outlier.

    • M1 for interquartile range = 40 − 24 = 16
    • M1 for 1.5 × 16 = 24
    • M1 for the upper limit 40 + 24 = 64
    • C1 for a conclusion: 70 is more than 64, so it is an outlier

    Why it works

    • A running total only goes up. That is why the curve never dips, and why every point sits at the end of its class.
    • The quartiles split the data into four equal quarters. So the box holds the middle half, and the interquartile range ignores the extreme values at both ends.
    • Area is frequency. A wide class collects more values just by being wide, so we divide by the width to make bars comparable. Multiply by the width to get the frequency back.
    • You compare with an average and a spread. Two sets of data can have the same median but very different spreads.

    Common traps

    • Plotting cumulative frequency at the midpoint instead of the upper boundary.
    • Giving the height (such as 40) as the median instead of reading down to the value.
    • Adding the quartiles instead of subtracting them to get the interquartile range.
    • Using frequency as the bar height when the class widths are different.
    • Forgetting to take the reading from the total when a question asks “how many more than”.
    • Writing a comparison with numbers only and no words about the context.
