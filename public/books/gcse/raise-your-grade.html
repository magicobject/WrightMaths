Raise your grade

How to shift your results to the right

Imagine every GCSE result on a line from grade 1 on the left to grade 9 on the right. This page is about moving yours along it: what changes at each step, what to do about it, and which habits get you there fastest.

The scale

What the grades are

In England, GCSE maths is graded 9 to 1, with 9 the highest. Grade 4 is the "standard pass" and grade 5 the "strong pass". Your tier decides which grades are open to you:

  • Foundation tier covers grades 1 to 5.
  • Higher tier covers grades 4 to 9 (with a grade 3 allowed for a narrow miss of a 4).

The moves below are descriptions of what a typical student does differently at each step. They are our teaching guide, not an exam board's official wording, and mark boundaries change from year to year, so we never promise "X marks gets a grade Y". Ask your teacher which tier you are entered for before you choose where to aim.

The big idea: almost nobody jumps three grades by working three times harder at the same things. Each step is a change in what you do: a new habit, or a gap underneath filled in. That is why this book is built as a pyramid.

Step by step

Your next step, grade by grade

Find where you are now. Read what changes next. Do the "this week" action.

Grade 1–2 → 3

What changes: accuracy with the base. Place value, the four operations, fractions of an amount and simple algebra are secure and quick, and you can read a question and pick out the numbers that matter.

Common blocker: shaky times tables and negative numbers make everything above them wobble.

This week: five minutes a day on times tables and negatives, then the "Last time" questions at the top of Fractions.

Grade 3 → 4 (standard pass)

What changes: you can use a method in a new setting, not only in the exact shape you were taught. Fractions, percentages and ratio all work, and you show your working so method marks are earned.

Common blocker: stopping at a number when the question asked for something else (a total, a percentage, a unit).

This week: after every question, reread it and tick "did I answer what was asked?". Work through Fractions and Solving equations.

Grade 4 → 5 (strong pass)

What changes: multi-step problems. You split them into pieces, and you can move between fractions, decimals and percentages. Equations with brackets and unknowns on both sides are routine.

Common blocker: losing a minus sign or skipping the "check" step.

This week: put every answer back into the question to check it. Try the exam-style questions at the end of Solving equations.

Grade 5 → 6

What changes: choosing the method yourself. Pythagoras, ratio, percentage change and simple proportion appear in disguise, and you recognise which tool to use without being told.

Common blocker: practising one topic at a time, so you can do each in its own chapter but not when topics are mixed.

This week: use Mixed practice: five questions from different topics, chosen for you. Then try Pythagoras.

Grade 6 → 7

What changes: algebra becomes fluent. You factorise and expand without stopping to think, and you work with surds, fractions and powers, and show the full chain of reasoning.

Common blocker: being right but not showing why; "show that" questions need every line.

This week: pick any worked example and cover the solution; write it from memory and mark it against the page.

Grade 7 → 8

What changes: reasoning and proof. You set up algebra from words, solve problems with several unknowns, and explain why a result must be true.

Common blocker: trying a few numbers and stopping. Examples are evidence, not proof.

This week: for each topic, write a two-sentence explanation of why the method works.

Grade 8 → 9

What changes: unfamiliar problems. Nothing is a recognisable "type", so you try a diagram, a small case, or a letter for the unknown until a path appears, and you check it against the reverse.

Common blocker: giving up after the first idea fails.

This week: spend ten minutes on one hard problem before looking at any hint, then use the hints one at a time.

The habits

Eight habits that move results to the right

  1. Test yourself, don't re-read. Close the book and answer questions from memory. Getting an answer wrong and finding out why is worth more than reading it a third time. Every "Check your knowledge" box in this book is built for this.
  2. Space it out. Come back to a topic after a day, then a few days, then a couple of weeks. A little often beats a lot once. Put a reminder in your phone for each topic you finish.
  3. Mix it up. Once a topic is secure, practise it mixed with others. In the exam nobody tells you the topic, so choosing the method is part of the skill.
  4. Keep a mistake log. One line for each error: what happened, why, and what you will do next time. Look at it before each practice session. Most students make the same three or four mistakes over and over, so naming them is half of fixing them.
  5. Show your working. Examiners award marks for method, even when the final answer is wrong. A neat line of working can rescue a question; a bare wrong answer cannot.
  6. Flip it and check. Reverse the answer to see if it fits: put an equation's solution back in, undo a calculation, test a triangle with Pythagoras the other way. This one habit catches a huge share of slips.
  7. Estimate first. A rough Estimate tells you what size the answer should be, so a slipped decimal point or a missing zero is obvious before you move on.
  8. Explain it to the duck. When you are stuck, say the problem out loud to anybody, or to a rubber duck: what is it asking, what do I know, what did I try, where did it stop making sense? Programmers call this rubber duck debugging, and it works because explaining forces you to put a vague confusion into exact words, and the gap usually shows up as you speak. It also works the other way: if you can explain a method to someone else, you really own it.
Exam day

Exam technique in five minutes

  • Three papers. Most GCSE maths courses have two or three papers of equal weight, and at least one is without a calculator. Practise some questions both ways.
  • Read the question word (see Question words). Work out and Calculate want a number. Show that wants every step to a target you are given. Explain wants a reason in words. Write down wants no working.
  • Use the marks. A 1-mark question is quick. A 4-mark question wants a few steps of working. The number of marks tells you how much to write.
  • Never leave a blank. Any relevant start (an equation, a diagram, a correct first step) can earn a mark.
  • Round last. Keep the full calculator value until the final line, then round as asked.
  • Units and signs. Cm, cm², £, °. And check whether the answer should be negative.
  • Check in the last five minutes. Pick the questions where a quick reverse-check is easy.
Check your knowledge

Do you know how to raise your grade?

You get to the end of a 4-mark question and your final answer is wrong, but you wrote clear working. What can you still earn?

  • Nothing: only the final answer counts
  • Method marks for the correct steps you showed
  • Only the first mark
  1. Think about the word "method" in the mark scheme.
  2. Examiners are told to reward correct steps, not only the final number.

Mark schemes award marks for correct method as well as for accuracy. That is why showing your working is on the list of habits.

Which revision method is usually the most effective for making facts stick?

  • Reading your notes again and again
  • Highlighting every key line in yellow
  • Closing the book and answering questions from memory
  1. Which one makes your brain work hardest to pull the answer out?

Pulling an answer out of memory strengthens it much more than re-reading, even when you get it wrong first.

What is the best use of a mistake log?

  • To record your marks so you can compare with friends
  • To spot the few mistakes you keep repeating, and fix them on purpose
  • To show your teacher how many mistakes you made
  1. Think about what you do with the log afterwards.

Most people repeat the same small set of errors. Naming them lets you watch for them.

The question says Show that the answer is 12. What should you do?

  • Write 12 and nothing else
  • Write every step of working that leads to 12
  • Find some different answer, to be safe
  1. The target is given. What is left for you to provide?

The final answer is given to you. The marks are for the route.

You finish a topic. When is the best time to test yourself on it again?

  • Never; once is enough
  • Only the night before the exam
  • After a day, then a few days, then a couple of weeks
  1. Memory fades. Spacing your practice fights that fade.

Spaced practice beats cramming. Each return trip makes the memory last longer.

Need a person?

Sometimes a conversation is faster

If you are stuck on the same step over and over, a short chat with someone who can see how you are thinking usually unlocks it. Ask your teacher, or a parent or carer can get in touch about lessons.
