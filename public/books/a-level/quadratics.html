    Quadratics

    A quadratic has an x squared in it. This page gives you three ways to solve one, a quick test for how many roots it has, and a way to sketch its graph (draw its shape quickly).

    Where you will use this: finding the lowest or highest point of a curve. It also helps you find the centre of a circle (Circles) and factorise bigger polynomials (Polynomials). It helps with any problem where an area or a path length is squared.

    quadratic
    An expression or equation where the highest power of the unknown is 2, such as x squared plus 5x plus 4.
    root (of an equation or function)
    A value of x that makes a function equal zero. On the graph, it is where the curve meets the x-axis.
    completing the square
    Rewriting a quadratic in the form a(x minus p) squared plus q. The turning point is then (p, q).
    quadratic formula
    A formula that gives the roots of a quadratic equation from its coefficients a, b and c.
    discriminant
    For a quadratic with coefficients a, b and c, the number b squared minus 4ac. Its sign tells you how many real roots there are.
    vertex (of a parabola)
    The turning point of a parabola: its lowest point if it opens upwards, and its highest point if it opens downwards.

    1. Solving by factorising

    A quadratic is an expression where the highest power of x is 2. An expression is numbers and letters with no equals sign, such as 3x+1. An equation has an equals sign. The general form of a quadratic is ax2+bx+c, where a, b and c are numbers and a is not 0.

    A value of x that makes the quadratic equal to 0 is a root (of an equation or function). To solve ax2+bx+c=0 means to find all of its roots. On a graph, the roots are the x-values where the curve meets the x-axis.

    To factorise an expression is to write it as brackets multiplied together. Factorising gives the roots because of one fact about zero.

    Key idea: if two numbers multiply to give 0, then at least one of them is 0. So (x−p)(x−q)=0 means x=p or x=q.

    Follow these steps.

    1. Move every term to one side, so that the other side is 0.
    2. Factorise the quadratic.
    3. Set each bracket equal to 0 and solve it.
    4. Check each root in the original equation.

    Worked example

    Solve x2−x−6=0.

    1. One side is already 0.
    2. Find two numbers that multiply to −6 and add to −1. They are −3 and 2. So (x−3)(x+2)=0.
    3. Either x−3=0 or x+2=0. So x=3 or x=−2.
    4. Check: 32−3−6=0 and (−2)2−(−2)−6=0. Both are true.

    The curve in the figure meets the x-axis at exactly these two values.

    2. Completing the square

    Completing the square writes a quadratic in the form (x+p)2+q, where p and q are numbers. We read (x+p)2 as “x plus p, all squared”. The picture below shows why this works.

    Start with x2+6x. The x2 term is a square of side x. The 6x term is two strips, each x long and 3 wide.

    The big square has area (x+3)2. That area is x2+6x plus the corner, which has area 9. So x2+6x=(x+3)2−9. The −9 takes away the corner that we added.

    Key idea: halve the number in front of x. Write x plus that half in a bracket, and square the bracket. Then subtract the square of the half, so that the value does not change: x2+bx=(x+b2)2−(b2)2.

    Worked example

    Write x2+6x+5 in the form (x+p)2+q.

    1. Half of 6 is 3. So x2+6x=(x+3)2−9.
    2. Add the 5: x2+6x+5=(x+3)2−9+5=(x+3)2−4.
    3. Check by expanding: (x+3)2−4=x2+6x+9−4=x2+6x+5.

    Worked example: a negative number in front of x

    Write x2−8x+3 in the form (x+p)2+q.

    1. Half of −8 is −4. So x2−8x=(x−4)2−16.
    2. Add the 3: x2−8x+3=(x−4)2−16+3=(x−4)2−13.
    3. Check: (x−4)2−13=x2−8x+16−13=x2−8x+3.

    What completing the square tells you

    The graph of a quadratic is a parabola. Its vertex (of a parabola) is its turning point: the point where the curve stops going down and starts going up, or the other way round. The vertical line through the vertex is the axis of symmetry. This line splits the parabola into two mirror-image halves.

    The square of any real number is never negative. So (x+p)2 is 0 when x=−p, and bigger than 0 everywhere else. So the lowest point of y=(x+p)2+q is at x=−p, where y=q.

    Key idea: the graph of y=(x+p)2+q has its vertex at (−p,q), and its axis of symmetry is the line x=−p. The x-coordinate of the vertex is −p, the opposite sign to the number in the bracket, because (x+3) is 0 when x=−3.

    Worked example

    Find the least value of y=x2+6x+5, and the value of x where it happens.

    1. Complete the square: y=(x+3)2−4.
    2. (x+3)2 is never negative, and it is 0 when x=−3.
    3. So the least value of y is −4, and it happens at x=−3. The vertex is (−3,−4).

    5. The discriminant

    In the quadratic formula, the part under the square root is b2−4ac. This part is called the discriminant. The discriminant tells you how many roots the equation has, before you solve it.

    The formula needs the square root of the discriminant. A real number (ℝ) is any number on the number line. The square root of a negative number is not a real number, so the formula gives no real root. That is why the sign of the discriminant matters.

    Positive: two different roots

    If b2−4ac>0 (read “is greater than 0”), the square root of b2−4ac is a real number that is not 0. The sign ± then gives two different roots, and the curve crosses the x-axis twice.

    Zero: one repeated root

    If b2−4ac=0, then ±0 gives only one value. The equation has one repeated root. The vertex lies on the x-axis, so the curve touches the axis and turns round.

    Negative: no real roots

    If b2−4ac<0 (read “is less than 0”), there is no real square root. The equation has no real roots, and the curve does not meet the x-axis.

    Key idea: for ax2+bx+c=0, work out b2−4ac. If it is positive, there are two different real roots. If it is 0, there is one repeated root. If it is negative, there are no real roots.

    8. Problem solving with quadratics

    Many problems lead to a quadratic. Follow these steps.

    1. Draw a diagram, if there is a shape. Name the unknown with a letter.
    2. Write an equation from the facts you are given.
    3. Solve it.
    4. Rule out any root that does not fit the situation. For example, a length cannot be negative.
    5. Answer the question that was asked, with units, and check it.

    Worked example: an area

    A rectangle is 3 m longer than it is wide. Its area is 40 m². Find its width.

    1. Let the width be x metres. The length is x+3 metres.
    2. Area is length times width: x(x+3)=40, so x2+3x−40=0.
    3. (x+8)(x−5)=0, so x=−8 or x=5.
    4. A width cannot be negative. So x=5.
    5. The width is 5 m and the length is 8 m. Check: 5×8=40.

    Worked example: the greatest area

    A farmer has 40 m of fence. She uses it for three sides of a rectangular pen (a fenced area for animals). The fourth side is a wall. What is the greatest area the pen can have?

    1. Let each short side be x metres. The two short sides use 2x of the fence, so the long side is 40−2x.

    3. Complete the square: −2x2+40x=−2(x2−20x)=−2[(x−10)2−100]=200−2(x−10)2.

    Flip it: run it backwards

    Everything so far goes forwards: you are given a quadratic and you find its roots or its graph. Now reverse it. You are given the roots or the graph, and you build the quadratic.

    Forwards

    From the equation to its roots. Factorise, then set each bracket to 0.

    x2−5x+6=0 becomes (x−2)(x−3)=0, so x=2 or x=3.

    Backwards

    From the roots to the equation. Make one bracket from each root, then expand.

    The roots 2 and 3 give (x−2)(x−3)=x2−5x+6.

    Key idea: a root x=p comes from the bracket (x−p), so the root and the number in the bracket have opposite signs. For roots p and q, (x−p)(x−q)=x2−(p+q)x+pq. The number in front of x is minus the sum of the roots (add the roots, then change the sign). The number with no x is the product of the roots (multiply them). If the number in front of x2 is not 1, multiply the whole expression by that number: a(x−p)(x−q).

    2. a=1, b=2k, c=3k+4. Write (2k)2−4(3k+4)=0.
    3. Expand and simplify. You get a quadratic in k. Solve it.
    • That is one value. The equation in k is a quadratic, so it has two solutions.
    • You used the numbers in the brackets as the roots. (k−4) is 0 when k=4, so each root has the opposite sign to the number in its bracket.

    (2k)2−4(3k+4)=0 gives 4k2−12k−16=0, so k2−3k−4=0 and (k−4)(k+1)=0. So k=4 or k=−1. Check k=4: x2+8x+16=(x+4)2. Check k=−1: x2−2x+1=(x−1)2.

    • M1 for using b2−4ac=0 with b=2k and c=3k+4
    • A1 for 4k2−12k−16=0 (or k2−3k−4=0)
    • M1 for solving the quadratic in k
    • A1 for k=−1 and k=4

    [4 marks] Solve x−7x12+12=0. Type both roots with “or” between them, smallest first.

    1. This is a quadratic in disguise. Which power of x is the square of another power?
    2. Let u=x12. Then u2−7u+12=0.
    3. (u−3)(u−4)=0. Since x=u2, square each value of u.
    • Those are the values of u. Since u=x12, we have x=u2.

    Let u=x12, so u2−7u+12=0 and (u−3)(u−4)=0. u=3 gives x=9. u=4 gives x=16. Check x=9: 9−21+12=0. Check x=16: 16−28+12=0.

    • M1 for a substitution (a new letter) that gives a quadratic, such as u2−7u+12=0
    • M1 for solving it: u=3 and u=4
    • M1 for squaring to get x
    • A1 for x=9 and x=16

    A farmer has 80 m of fence. She uses it for three sides of a rectangular field. The fourth side is a wall. Each of the two sides next to the wall is x metres long. (a) [2 marks] Find the area A as an expression that contains x. Expand the brackets. Type the x term first, like 5x-3x^2.

    1. Two sides use 2x of the fence. What is left for the third side?
    2. The third side is 80−2x.
    3. Area is length times width. Expand your answer.
    • The third side is 80−2x, because two sides of length x use up 2x. Not 80−x.

    The third side is 80−2x. So A=x(80−2x)=80x−2x2.

    • B1 for the third side 80−2x
    • B1 for A=x(80−2x)=80x−2x2

    (b) [3 marks] Use completing the square to find the greatest possible area of the field, in m².

    1. Write 80x−2x2 with the x2 term first, and write −2 outside a bracket round the first two terms.
    2. −2(x2−40x). Complete the square inside the bracket.
    3. x2−40x=(x−20)2−400. Multiply the −400 by −2.
    • That is the value of x that gives the greatest area. The question asks for the area.
    • You did not multiply the 400 by 2. −2[(x−20)2−400]=−2(x−20)2+800.

    80x−2x2=−2(x2−40x)=−2[(x−20)2−400]=800−2(x−20)2. The greatest area is 800 m², when x=20. The field is 20 m by 40 m. Check: 20×40=800.

    • M1 for taking out −2 and completing the square: −2[(x−20)2−400]
    • A1 for 800−2(x−20)2
    • A1 for the greatest area, 800

    The equation x2+kx+(k−1)=0 has discriminant D. (a) [2 marks] Find D in terms of k. Expand and simplify it. Type it like k^2+3k-5.

    1. Read off a, b and c in terms of k.
    2. a=1, b=k, c=k−1. So D=k2−4(k−1).
    3. Multiply out the bracket. Remember that −4(k−1) changes the sign of both terms in the bracket.
    • You did not multiply the −1 by −4. −4(k−1)=−4k+4.

    D=k2−4×1×(k−1)=k2−4k+4.

    • M1 for k2−4(k−1)
    • A1 for k2−4k+4

    (b) [2 marks] Hence show that the equation x2+kx+(k−1)=0 has real roots for every value of k. Write your answer on paper, then open the mark scheme.

    1. “Hence” means use your answer to (a).
    2. Factorise k2−4k+4. It is a perfect square (a bracket multiplied by itself).
    3. What do you know about a square? What does that say about the discriminant?

    D=k2−4k+4=(k−2)2. A square is never negative, so D≥0 for every k. A discriminant of 0 or more means the equation has real roots, for every value of k.

    • M1 for D=(k−2)2
    • A1 for a reason ((k−2)2 is never negative, so D≥0) and the conclusion: real roots for every k

    Why it works

    • The zero idea. Zero times any number is 0. A product of two numbers is 0 only when one of them is 0. So we first rearrange so that one side is 0.
    • Completing the square. (x+p)2 is the area of a square of side x+p. Its pieces are x2, two strips of px and a corner of p2. Completing the square adds the missing corner, and then takes it away again.
    • The vertex. In (x+p)2+q the bracket is never negative. So the lowest value is q, when the bracket is 0. That is why the vertex is (−p,q).
    • The formula. The quadratic formula is what you get when you complete the square once for the general quadratic ax2+bx+c. The letters a, b and c stand for any numbers, so it works for all of them.
    • The discriminant. It is the number under the square root in the formula. The square root of a negative number is not real. The square root of 0 gives one value. The square root of a positive number gives two values, one positive and one negative.
    • Quadratics in disguise. The equation really is a quadratic in u, so every method for quadratics works on it. The only extra step is going back from u to x.

    Common traps

    • Using the number in the bracket as the root. The bracket (x−3) gives x=3.
    • Using the zero idea when the other side is not 0.
    • Dividing both sides by x, and losing the root x=0.
    • Giving one root when there are two. Write x=3 or x=−3.
    • Reading the vertex of (x+3)2−4 as (3,−4). It is (−3,−4).
    • In completing the square with a not 1, forgetting to multiply the number you subtract inside the bracket by a.
    • In the formula, forgetting brackets round a negative b, or forgetting to divide the whole top line by 2a.
    • Stopping at the values of u in a quadratic in disguise, or giving only the positive roots.
    • Keeping a root that does not fit: a negative length, or a value of u that would make x12 negative.
