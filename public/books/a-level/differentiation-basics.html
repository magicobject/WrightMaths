    Differentiation from first principles

    A curve is steeper in some places than in others. Differentiation finds exactly how steep it is at any point. You will build the idea from a straight line, then learn the shortcut that every later calculus topic uses.

    Where you will use this: finding a speed from a distance graph, how fast a tank fills or drains, the highest or lowest point of a curve (the next topic), and every Year 2 calculus topic.

    1. The gradient of a curve

    The Gradient of a straight line is the same everywhere: rise divided by run. A curve bends, so its steepness changes from point to point. We need a way to talk about the steepness at one point.

    Zoom in very close on a smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. That line is the tangent to the curve at that point. It touches the curve there and points the same way as the curve.

    Key idea: the gradient of a curve at a point is the gradient of the tangent at that point.

    To read a gradient, draw a triangle under the tangent and divide the distance up by the distance across. A positive gradient means the curve is rising at that point. A negative gradient means it is falling.

    At A, the gradient is 2: the curve rises 2 units for each 1 unit across.

    Worked example

    The tangent to a curve at P passes through (1, 4) and (3, 10). Find the gradient of the curve at P.

    1. The gradient of the curve at P is the gradient of the tangent. Use the two points on the tangent.
    2. Rise = 10 − 4 = 6. Run = 3 − 1 = 2.
    3. Gradient = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. At P, the curve rises 3 units for each 1 unit across.

    Now you finish it

    2. Chords closing in on a tangent

    A tangent needs only one point, but we can estimate its gradient from two points. A chord is a straight line segment that joins two points on a curve. Its gradient is rise over run, like any line.

    Take the point A (1, 1) on y=x2. Pick a second point B on the curve, further to the right. Now slide B along the curve towards A. The chord AB turns, and it gets closer and closer to the tangent at A.

    Here are more chords from A, with the x-coordinate of B and the gradient of the chord AB:

    • B at x=3: 9−13−1=4.
    • B at x=2: 4−12−1=3.
    • B at x=1.5: 2.25−11.5−1=2.5.
    • B at x=1.1: 1.21−10.1=2.1.
    • B at x=1.01: 1.0201−10.01=2.01.

    The gradients 4, 3, 2.5, 2.1 and 2.01 are getting close to 2. That is the gradient of the tangent at A.

    Key idea: as the second point of a chord moves towards the first, the gradient of the chord gets closer to the gradient of the tangent. Each chord gives an estimate. The gradient of the tangent is the value the estimates get close to. B can never be exactly A: a chord needs two different points.

    2. Rise = 9.61 − 9 = 0.61. Run = 3.1 − 3 = 0.1.
    3. Gradient of the chord = 0.61 ÷ 0.1 = 6.1. This is an estimate. A closer point, x=3.01, gives 9.0601−90.01=6.01. The estimates are getting close to 6.

    3. Limits, and differentiation from first principles

    The limit idea

    Look again at the chord from A (1, 1) to a point B on y=x2. Give B the x-coordinate 1+h. The number h is the run: how far B is to the right of A. (If h is negative, B is to the left. The algebra works for either sign.) So B is the point (1+h,(1+h)2).

    The rise is (1+h)2−1=2h+h2. The gradient of the chord is 2h+h2h. We may divide the top and the bottom by h, because h is not 0. If h were 0, B would be A and there would be no chord. So for every h that is not 0, the gradient of the chord is 2+h.

    As h gets closer to 0, 2+h gets closer to 2. We say that 2 is the limit of 2+h as h tends to 0 (h gets close to 0), and write

    limh→0(2+h)=2

    Read it as "the limit, as h tends to zero, of 2 plus h, is 2". "Tends to" means "gets as close as you like to".

    Key idea: a limit is the value an expression gets close to as h gets close to 0. The expression does not have to reach that value, and h is never actually 0. So simplify first, while h is not 0, and then see what value the result gets close to. Do not put h=0 into the fraction: it gives 0 over 0, which has no value.

    The derivative from the definition

    Now do the same at any point. Write the curve as y=f(x), read as "f of x". It means the height of the curve at x. Let A be the point (x,f(x)) and let B be the point (x+h,f(x+h)).

    The gradient of the chord is f(x+h)−f(x)h. The gradient of the curve at x is the limit of this as h tends to 0. This limit is called the derivative of f. It is written f′(x), read as "f dash of x".

    Key idea (the definition of the derivative):

    f′(x)=limh→0f(x+h)−f(x)h

    Using this to find a derivative is called differentiation from first principles.

    The method is the same every time:

    1. Write f(x+h) and expand it.
    2. Find f(x+h)−f(x), divide by h and simplify. Now h should have cancelled.
    3. Let h tend to 0.

    Worked example

    Differentiate f(x)=x2 from first principles.

    1. f(x+h)=(x+h)2=x2+2xh+h2.
    2. f(x+h)−f(x)=2xh+h2. Divide by h, which is allowed because h is not 0: 2xh+h2h=2x+h.
    3. As h tends to 0, 2x+h tends to 2x. So f′(x)=limh→0(2x+h)=2x.

    The gradient of y=x2 at any point is twice the x-coordinate. At x=1 it is 2, as the tangent triangle showed.

    Now you finish it

    4. Notation and the power rule

    Two ways to write a derivative

    If y=f(x), the derivative can be written f′(x) or dydx. Say "dy by dx". The symbol means "the derivative of y with respect to x".

    The letter d is not a number. It stands for "a small change in". So dydx is a single symbol: you cannot cancel the d's. We also write ddx(x2)=2x, read as "d by dx of x squared is 2x". The symbol d by dx means: differentiate what follows with respect to x.

    A pattern

    We found two results from first principles: x2 has derivative 2x, and x3 has derivative 3x2. The same method shows that x4 has derivative 4x3. Two more facts. The derivative of x is 1, because the graph of y=x has gradient 1 everywhere. The derivative of a constant is 0, because the graph of y=7 is horizontal.

    Key idea (the power rule):

    ddx(xn)=nxn−1

    Bring the power down to the front as a multiplier. Then subtract 1 from the power (this is "taking 1 off"). This works for every rational number n, wherever xn is defined: whole numbers, negative numbers and fractions.

    We have proved the rule for n=2 and n=3. The proof for every positive whole number n is in "Why it works" at the end of this page. For negative and fractional n we use the rule without proof here.

    Constants, multiples and sums

    Three more facts let you differentiate any sum of powers of x.

    • A constant on its own has derivative 0.
    • A constant multiple stays in front: ddx(kf(x))=kf′(x). For example, 5x4 has derivative 5×4x3=20x3.
    • A sum is differentiated term by term: the derivative of f(x)+g(x) is f′(x)+g′(x).

    Why this works: multiplying a function by 5 multiplies every chord gradient by 5. Adding two functions adds their chord gradients. Both facts still hold after you take the limit.

    5. Negative and fractional powers

    The power rule works for every rational number n, wherever xn is defined. But a question rarely writes the powers for you. Before you differentiate, rewrite each term as a number times a power of x.

    • A negative power means one over the positive power: 1x=x−1, 1x2=x−2 and 3x2=3x−2.
    • A fractional power is a root: x=x1/2, x3=x1/3 and 1x=x−1/2.
    • A number on the bottom of a fraction is a constant multiplier: x23=13x2.

    Then use the same two moves: bring the power down, then take 1 off. Take care with the arithmetic. −2−1=−3, and 12−1=−12.

    Worked example

    Find dydx when y=4x−3x2.

    1. Rewrite as powers: y=4x1/2−3x−2.
    2. Differentiate the first term: 4×12x−1/2=2x−1/2. Differentiate the second: −3×(−2)x−3=6x−3.
    3. dydx=2x−1/2+6x−3. You may write this as 2x+6x3.

    Now you finish it

    1. Rewrite each term as a number times a power of x. The first term, 2x3, has the power ___. The second term, x, has the power ___.
    2. So y=2x−3−x1/2. The first term gives 2×(−3)x−4=___.

    8. Sketching the graph of the derivative

    9. The derivative as a rate of change

    Flip it: from the gradient back to the function

    Differentiating goes from a function to its derivative. Now run it backwards: given a derivative, find a function that has it.

    Forwards

    Start with y=x2. Differentiate: dydx=2x.

    Backwards

    Start with dydx=2x. Which function has this derivative? y=x2 does. So does y=x2+3, and so does y=x2−3. A constant differentiates to 0, so the derivative cannot tell these functions apart.

    Key idea: undoing a derivative gives a whole family of functions that differ only by a constant. One point on the curve fixes the constant. To undo the power rule, do its two moves backwards: add 1 to the power, then divide by the new power. Check every answer by differentiating it.

    This undoing has a name, integration. It is a later topic. Here you only need to guess a function and check it.

    Why it works

    • The tangent is the limit of chords. A chord needs two different points, so h is never 0. We simplify while h is not 0, and then ask what value the chord gradient gets close to. That is how a gradient at one point comes from a gradient between two points.
    • The power rule, for positive whole n. Use the binomial expansion of (x+h)n. The first two terms are xn and nxn−1h. Every other term has at least h2 in it. Subtract xn and divide by h: you get nxn−1 plus terms that each still contain h. As h tends to 0, those terms tend to 0. What is left is nxn−1.
    • Negative and fractional powers. The same rule holds, and the pictures agree: y=x flattens as x grows, so its gradient 12x−1/2 falls. y=1/x falls, so its gradient −x−2 is negative. A full proof for these needs ideas from Year 2.
    • Constant multiples and sums. If you multiply a function by 5, every chord gradient is multiplied by 5, and so is the limit. If you add two functions, the chord gradients add, and so do the limits.
    • A constant has derivative 0. Its graph is horizontal, so every chord gradient is 0.
    • The second derivative. It is the rate of change of the gradient. If the gradient is increasing, the tangent turns anticlockwise as you move right, and the curve bends upwards.
    • Working backwards loses the constant. Constants differentiate to 0, so many functions share one derivative. A point on the curve fixes the constant.

    Common traps

    • Putting h=0 into the chord gradient before cancelling h.
    • Stopping at 2x+h, or writing it as the derivative. The limit has not been taken yet.
    • Bringing the power down but not taking 1 off it, or taking 1 off but not bringing it down.
    • Leaving a constant term in the derivative. It differentiates to 0.
    • Differentiating 1x2 or x without rewriting them as powers first.
    • Differentiating the top and the bottom of a fraction separately, or multiplying the derivatives of two factors.
    • Losing a minus sign when the power is negative: −2×(−3)=+6.
    • Putting the x-value into the original function instead of into the derivative.
    • Reading d2ydx2 as (dydx)2.
    • Forgetting the constant when working backwards from a gradient.
