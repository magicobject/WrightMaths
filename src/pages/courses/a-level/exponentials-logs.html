Exponentials and logarithms

Some numbers grow by multiplying, not by adding. In {m:2^x} the unknown x is in the power. This page shows the graphs of these functions, including the special number e. Then it introduces the logarithm, which finds the power for you. You will learn three laws for logarithms. You will use them to solve equations and inequalities where the unknown is in a power.

Where you will use this: in Exponential modelling, to describe growth and decay. Also in Differentiation, to differentiate {m:e^x} and {m:ln x}. You use it too in Geometric series, to find how many terms you need.

2. Logarithms: the power that gives a number

You can solve {m:2^x=8} by eye, because {m:2^3=8}. Now try {m:2^x=10}. There is no whole number. You need a new tool that finds the power.

A {term:logarithm|logarithm} is that tool. It is the power you must raise the base to, to get a given number.

Key idea: {m:log_a x=y} means exactly the same as {m:a^y=x}. Read {m:log_2 8} as “log to the base 2 of 8”. The base a is positive and not 1, and the number x is positive. Two facts are always true: {m:log_a 1=0}, because {m:a^0=1}, and {m:log_a a=1}, because {m:a^1=a}.

{m:2^3=8}{m:log_2 8=3}

{m:7^1=7}{m:log_7 7=1}

Two bases have their own names. The {term:common-logarithm|common logarithm} has base 10. It is the log key on your calculator. The {term:natural-logarithm|natural logarithm} has base e. It is written {m:ln x}, and it is the ln key. So {m:ln x=y} means {m:e^y=x}.

Worked example: evaluate a logarithm

Find {m:log_3 81}, {m:log_2 (frac(1,8))} and {m:log_4 2}.

  2. {m:log_2 (frac(1,8))} asks: 2 to what power is {m:frac(1,8)}? {m:2^(-3)=frac(1,8)}, so the answer is {m:-3}.
  3. {m:log_4 2} asks: 4 to what power is 2? {m:4^(frac(1,2))=sqrt(4)=2}, so the answer is {m:frac(1,2)}.

A logarithm is a power, so it can be negative or a fraction.

Logarithms undo powers

A logarithm and a power with the same base undo each other. This is what “{term:log-exp-inverse|inverse}” means here.

Key idea: {m:log_a (a^x)=x} and {m:a^(log_a x)=x}. In particular {m:ln(e^x)=x} and {m:e^(ln x)=x}. The base of the power and the base of the logarithm must be the same. The second form needs {m:x>0}.

So {m:ln(e^4)=4} and {m:e^(ln 7)=7}. But {m:10^(ln 7)} is not 7, because the bases differ.

The {term:log-graph|graph of a logarithm} follows from this picture. The graph of {m:y=ln x} passes through {m:(1, 0)}. It exists only for {m:x>0}. The y-axis is its asymptote. The graph rises, but slowly.

3. The laws of logarithms

The laws of indices have matching laws for logarithms. Each one turns a hard operation into an easier one.

Key idea: for any base a, and for {m:x>0} and {m:y>0}, there are three laws.

The {term:log-product-law|product law}: {m:log_a x+log_a y=log_a (x y)}.

The {term:log-quotient-law|quotient law}: {m:log_a x-log_a y=log_a frac(x, y)}.

The {term:log-power-law|power law}: {m:log_a (x^k)=k log_a x}.

Every logarithm in a law has the same base.

Two special cases follow. {m:log_a frac(1, x)=-log_a x}, from the quotient law with {m:log_a 1=0}. And {m:log_a sqrt(x)=frac(1,2) log_a x}, from the power law with {m:k=frac(1,2)}.

There is no law for {m:log_a (x+y)}. It is not {m:log_a x+log_a y}. There is no law for a product of logarithms either.

Worked example: write as a single logarithm

Write {m:3 log_2 5-log_2 25} as a single logarithm.

  1. Use the power law on the first term: {m:3 log_2 5=log_2 (5^3)=log_2 125}.
  2. Now both terms have the form {m:log_2}: {m:log_2 125-log_2 25}.
  3. Use the quotient law: {m:log_2 frac(125, 25)=log_2 5}.

The answer is {m:log_2 5}.

Worked example: find a number

Find the value of {m:log_6 4+log_6 9}.

4. Solving equations like ax = b

Now you can solve {m:2^x=10}. The unknown is in the power. The tool is to take logarithms of both sides, then use the power law to bring the power down.

Key idea: to solve {m:a^x=b}, follow these steps. First, get the power on its own on one side. Second, {term:taking-logs|take logarithms of both sides}. You can use ln or log. Third, use the power law to bring the unknown down: {m:x ln a=ln b}. Last, divide by {m:ln a}. Keep the full calculator value until the last step, and round once at the end.

Worked example: the basic equation

Solve {m:2^x=10}. Give x to 3 significant figures.

  1. Take natural logarithms of both sides: {m:ln(2^x)=ln 10}.
  2. Use the power law: {m:x ln 2=ln 10}.
  3. Divide by {m:ln 2}: {m:x=frac(ln 10, ln 2)=3.3219}.

So {m:x=3.32} to 3 significant figures. Check: {m:2^3=8} and {m:2^4=16}, and 10 is between them.

The same answer can be written exactly. From the meaning of a logarithm, {m:2^x=10} gives {m:x=log_2 10}. Taking logs of both sides shows that this equals {m:frac(ln 10, ln 2)}.

Worked example: the power is an expression

Flip it: power ↔ logarithm

A power and a logarithm are the same fact written two ways. You can go either way.

Forwards

From a base and a power to a number. Raise the base to the power.

Why it works

  • A logarithm is a power. The statement {m:log_a x=y} is the same statement as {m:a^y=x}, written another way. Every rule about logarithms is a rule about powers in disguise.
  • The product law. Write {m:x=a^m} and {m:y=a^n}. Then {m:log_a x=m} and {m:log_a y=n}. And {m:x y=a^m*a^n=a^(m+n)}, so {m:log_a (x y)=m+n}. Adding the powers is the law of indices for multiplying.
