1. Tangents to a curve

You have already met the {term:tangent-to-curve|tangent} to a curve at a point. It is the straight line that touches the curve there and has the same gradient as the curve there. This lesson writes down the equation of that line.

A straight line needs two things: a point on it and its gradient. The point is on the curve. The gradient is the value of the derivative at that point.

Key idea: suppose {m:y=f(x)} has a derivative at {m:x=a}. The tangent at the point {m:(a, f(a))} is the straight line through that point with gradient {m:f'(a)}. Its equation is

{md:y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)}

The point comes from the equation of the curve. The gradient comes from the derivative.

The method has three steps. The picture below shows them for the curve above.

  1. Differentiate to find {m:frac(dy, dx)}. If the equation has roots or fractions, rewrite them as powers of x first.
  2. Put the x-value into {m:frac(dy, dx)}. The result is the gradient m of the tangent.
  3. Put the same x-value into the equation of the curve to find y. Then write {m:y-y_1=m(x-x_1)} with this point as {m:(x_1, y_1)}, and tidy it.

Do not mix the two equations up. The derivative gives the gradient. The original equation gives the y-coordinate.

Worked example

  2. At {m:x=3}, the gradient is {m:2(3)-4=2}.
  3. At {m:x=3}, {m:y=9-12+5=2}. The point is (3, 2). The tangent is {m:y-2=2(x-3)}, which is {m:y=2x-4}.

2. Normals to a curve

The {term:normal-to-curve|normal} to a curve at a point is the straight line through that point at right angles to the tangent there.

You know from Straight lines that two lines at right angles have gradients that multiply to {m:-1}. So the gradient of the normal is the {term:negative-reciprocal} of the gradient of the tangent: turn the fraction upside down, and change the sign.

Key idea: suppose the tangent to a curve at a point has gradient m, and m is not 0. The normal at the same point has gradient {m:-frac(1, m)}, so the two gradients multiply to {m:-1}. The normal passes through the same point as the tangent. If {m:m=0}, the tangent is horizontal, and the normal is the vertical line {m:x=a} through the point, where a is the x-coordinate of the point. (You cannot use {m:-frac(1, m)} when {m:m=0}: it would need a division by 0.)

To find the equation of a normal, do the same three steps as for a tangent, then use the new gradient in the last step.

Worked example

Find the equation of the normal to {m:y=x^2-4x+5} at the point where {m:x=3}. Give your answer in the form {m:x+by=c}, with whole numbers b and c.

  2. The normal has gradient {m:-frac(1, 2)}, because {m:2*(-frac(1, 2))=-1}.
  3. {m:y-2=-frac(1, 2)(x-3)}. Multiply both sides by 2: {m:2y-4=-(x-3)=-x+3}. So {m:x+2y=7}. Check: at (3, 2), {m:3+4=7}. ✓

3. Increasing and decreasing

A curve rises where its gradient is positive, and falls where its gradient is negative. A function is {term:increasing-function|increasing} where its graph rises as x increases. It is {term:decreasing-function|decreasing} where its graph falls as x increases.

Key idea: on an interval where {m:frac(dy, dx) > 0} at every point, y is increasing as x increases. On an interval where {m:frac(dy, dx) < 0} at every point, y is decreasing as x increases. At a point where {m:frac(dy, dx)=0}, the tangent is horizontal.

To find where a function is increasing or decreasing, differentiate, then solve an inequality. Solve {m:frac(dy, dx) > 0} for the values of x where it is increasing. Solve {m:frac(dy, dx) < 0} for the values where it is decreasing. The answer is a set of x-values, not y-values. (Quadratic inequalities are in the Inequalities topic: find where the quadratic is 0, then decide which parts are above and below the x-axis.)

Worked example

Find the values of x for which {m:f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x} is increasing, and the values for which it is decreasing.

  1. Differentiate: {m:f'(x)=3x^2-12x+9=3(x^2-4x+3)=3(x-1)(x-3)}.
  2. {m:f'(x)=0} at {m:x=1} and {m:x=3}. The graph of {m:y=f'(x)} is U-shaped, because the coefficient of {m:x^2} is positive. It is below the x-axis between the roots, and above it outside them.
  3. So {m:f'(x) > 0} for {m:x < 1} and for {m:x > 3}: f is increasing there. And {m:f'(x) < 0} for {m:1 < x < 3}: f is decreasing there.

This page writes {m:x < 1} and {m:x > 3}, where the gradient is strictly positive. Including the end points, {m:x <= 1} and {m:x >= 3}, is also correct, and is the form the glossary uses: the curve never goes down on those ranges.

4. Stationary points

A {term:stationary-point|stationary point} is a point on a curve where the gradient is zero: {m:frac(dy, dx)=0}. The tangent there is horizontal.

Key idea: a stationary point of {m:y=f(x)} is a point where {m:frac(dy, dx)=0}. To find the stationary points: differentiate; solve {m:frac(dy, dx)=0} to get every x-value; then put each x-value into {m:y=f(x)} to find its y-coordinate. Solving {m:frac(dy, dx)=0} gives only x-values. The y-coordinates come from the curve. At a stationary point the tangent is the horizontal line {m:y=k}, where k is the y-coordinate, and the normal is the vertical line {m:x=a}, where a is the x-coordinate of the point.

Worked example

  2. Solve {m:3x^2-12=0}: {m:x^2=4}, so {m:x=2} or {m:x=-2}. (Both values count: {m:x^2=4} has two solutions.)
  3. Put each x-value into the curve. At {m:x=2}: {m:y=8-24=-16}. At {m:x=-2}: {m:y=-8+24=16}. The stationary points are (2, −16) and (−2, 16).

5. Maximum, minimum or neither

A stationary point can be a {term:maximum-point|maximum}, a {term:minimum-point|minimum}, or neither. A maximum or a minimum is also called a {term:turning-point}. Deciding which kind a stationary point is means finding its {term:nature-of-stationary-point|nature}. There are two tests, and you should be able to use both.

Test 1: the second derivative

The {term:second-derivative|second derivative}, {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)}, tells you how fast the gradient is changing. At a stationary point the gradient is 0. If the gradient is increasing there, it goes from negative to positive, so the curve goes down and then up: a minimum. If the gradient is decreasing there, it goes from positive to negative: a maximum.

Key idea: at a stationary point of {m:y=f(x)}, where {m:frac(dy, dx)=0}, find {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)} there. The second derivative must exist at the point. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2) > 0}, the point is a minimum. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2) < 0}, the point is a maximum. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)=0}, the test does not decide: the point could be a minimum, a maximum or neither.

6. Optimisation in context

{term:optimisation|Optimisation} means finding the greatest or the least value of a quantity. Many problems fix a total, such as a length of fence or a perimeter, and ask for the best shape. The idea is the same as before: write the quantity as a function of one variable, then find where its derivative is 0.

  1. Name the quantity and the variable. Use any fixed total to write the quantity in terms of one variable.
  2. Differentiate, and solve {m:frac(dQ, dx)=0}.
  3. Use the second derivative, or the sign of the gradient, to check that the point is a maximum (or a minimum, whichever the question asks for).
  4. Put the x-value into the quantity. Give the value the question asks for, with units. Check that the x-value is possible in the problem, such as a length that is positive. If it is the only stationary point in the allowed values, a maximum there is the greatest value.

Key idea: to find the greatest or least value of a quantity Q that depends on one variable x, write Q as a function of x, solve {m:frac(dQ, dx)=0}, and check the nature of each solution. Then put the x-value into Q. This finds a maximum or a minimum where the gradient is 0, so the x-value must be allowed in the problem. If the allowed values of x stop at an end point, the quantity at the end point must be compared too.

Why it works

  • The tangent equation. The gradient of a curve at a point is the gradient of the tangent there. A straight line is fixed by one point and its gradient, so the tangent is the line through the point on the curve with that gradient.
  • The normal. A line with direction {m:(1, m)} turned through a right angle has direction {m:(-m, 1)}, and its gradient is {m:frac(1, -m)=-frac(1, m)}. So two lines at right angles have gradients that multiply to {m:-1}. If {m:m=0}, the direction {m:(-m, 1)} is {m:(0, 1)}: straight up, a vertical line.
  • Increasing and decreasing. If the gradient is positive at every point of an interval, the curve rises as you move along it. A full proof needs ideas beyond A-level, but the picture is clear: a tangent that slopes up everywhere cannot belong to a curve that goes down.
  • Stationary points. A smooth curve, with no break and no corner, can only turn from rising to falling, or from falling to rising, by passing through a point where its gradient is 0. That is why the stationary points are where to look for a maximum or a minimum. The reverse is not true: {m:y=x^3} has a stationary point at the origin and does not turn.
  • The second derivative test. At a stationary point the gradient is 0. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2) > 0}, the gradient is increasing, so it is negative a little before and positive a little after: the curve goes down, then up. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2) < 0}, it is the other way round. If {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)=0}, you cannot tell whether the gradient changes sign: {m:y=x^4} and {m:y=x^3} are two examples with different results.
  • Optimisation. If a quantity has a greatest or a least value at a point inside the allowed values of x, and it can be differentiated there, the gradient at that point is 0, because the quantity has stopped rising and not yet started to fall (or the reverse). So you look at the stationary points, and check the end points of the allowed values of x.

Common traps

  • Putting the x-value into the original equation to get the gradient, instead of into {m:frac(dy, dx)}.
  • Using the gradient as the y-intercept of the tangent, or forgetting to add the y-coordinate back when writing {m:y=m(x-x_1)+y_1}.
  • Using the gradient of the tangent as the gradient of the normal, or changing the sign without turning the fraction upside down.
  • Forgetting that a horizontal tangent has a vertical normal, {m:x=a} (a is the x-coordinate of the point), with no gradient to find.
  • Solving a derivative inequality and giving y-values instead of x-values.
  • Dividing both sides of {m:frac(dy, dx)=0} by x, and losing the solution {m:x=0}.
  • Giving only the x-coordinate of a stationary point, when the question asks for the point.
  • Saying a stationary point is a minimum because {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)=0}, or an inflection because it is 0, without checking the sign of the gradient.
  • In optimisation, giving x when the question asks for the greatest value, and forgetting units.
  • Keeping a solution that is not allowed in the problem, such as a negative length.
