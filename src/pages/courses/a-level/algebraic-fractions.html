Algebraic fractions and partial fractions

An {term:algebraic-fraction|algebraic fraction} has algebra in its top, its bottom or both, such as {m:frac(x+1, x^2-4)}. This page shows how to simplify one, how to multiply and divide them, and how to add and subtract them. Then it goes the other way. It splits one fraction into simpler fractions, which is called {term:partial-fractions|partial fractions}. The bottoms on this page are made of straight-line factors such as {m:(x-1)}, and one of them may be repeated.

Where you will use this: in Binomial expansion with fractional and negative powers, where you split a fraction before you expand it. Also in Integrating with partial fractions, where each piece is integrated on its own. It helps whenever an answer must be one neat fraction, or a fraction must be taken apart.

1. Simplifying an algebraic fraction

An {term:algebraic-fraction|algebraic fraction} is a fraction with algebra on the top, the bottom or both. You simplify one in the same way as a number fraction. For {m:frac(6, 8)}, the top and the bottom share the factor 2, so {m:frac(6, 8)=frac(2*3, 2*4)=frac(3, 4)}.

You can {term:cancel} only a factor. A factor is a part that multiplies the whole top and the whole bottom. A term is a part that is added or subtracted. Compare {m:frac(2*3, 2*4)}, where cancelling the 2 works, with {m:frac(2+3, 2+4)}. Here {m:frac(5, 6)} is not {m:frac(3, 4)}, so cancelling the 2 is wrong.

So the first step is to write the top and the bottom as products. Take out common factors. Then factorise quadratics and, where you can, cubics. The factor theorem from Polynomials and the factor theorem helps with a cubic.

Key idea: to simplify an algebraic fraction, factorise the top and the bottom completely. Then cancel every factor that appears on both. Cancel factors, not terms. The simplified fraction equals the original for every x except the values that make the bottom of the original 0. At those values the original has no value. Those values are the {term:excluded-value|excluded values}.

Look at {m:frac(x^2-4, x-2)}. Its top factorises: {m:x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)}. Cancel {m:(x-2)} and you get {m:x+2}. This is true for every x except {m:x=2}. When {m:x=2}, the original is {m:frac(0, 0)}, which has no value. The graph shows this as a gap.

2. Multiplying and dividing

To multiply number fractions, you multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms. You can cancel first, which keeps the numbers small: {m:frac(2, 3)*frac(9, 10)=frac(1*3, 1*5)=frac(3, 5)}. Algebraic fractions work in the same way. You factorise first, so that you can see which factors are there to cancel.

To divide by a fraction, you turn it upside down and multiply. Turning a fraction upside down gives its reciprocal. This works for algebraic fractions too.

Key idea: to multiply algebraic fractions, factorise every top and every bottom. Cancel a factor on any top with the same factor on any bottom, in either fraction. Then multiply what is left. To divide by a fraction, turn the second fraction upside down and then multiply. The result has no value where any bottom is 0. When you divide, it also has no value where the fraction you divide by is 0.

Worked example: multiplying

Simplify {m:frac(x^2-4, x^2+x)*frac(x+1, x+2)}.

  2. Cancel {m:(x+1)}: it is on the bottom of the first fraction and the top of the second.
  3. Cancel {m:(x+2)}: it is on the top of the first fraction and the bottom of the second.
  4. What is left is {m:frac(x-2, x)}.

The answer is {m:frac(x-2, x)}. The original has no value when a bottom is 0, which is for {m:x=0}, {m:x=-1} and {m:x=-2}.

Worked example: dividing

  3. The product is {m:frac((x-3)(x+2), (x-1)(x+1))*frac(x+1, x-3)}. Cancel {m:(x-3)} and {m:(x+1)}.
  4. What is left is {m:frac(x+2, x-1)}.

The answer is {m:frac(x+2, x-1)}. The original has no value for {m:x=1} and {m:x=-1}, where a bottom is 0. It also has no value for {m:x=3}, because {m:frac(x-3, x+1)} is 0 there and you cannot divide by 0.

3. Adding and subtracting

To add {m:frac(1, 4)} and {m:frac(1, 6)}, you write both fractions with the bottom 12. This bottom is the {term:lowest-common-denominator|lowest common denominator}: the smallest number that both bottoms divide into. Then you add the tops: {m:frac(3, 12)+frac(2, 12)=frac(5, 12)}.

Algebraic fractions work in the same way. The lowest common denominator is made from the bottoms. You write each bottom as factors, and use each different factor once, to the highest power it has in either bottom. Then you multiply the top of each fraction by the factors its bottom is missing.

Key idea: to add or subtract algebraic fractions, write each over the lowest common denominator. Factorise the bottoms first, and use each factor once, to its highest power. Multiply each top by the factors that its bottom is missing. Then add or subtract the tops over the one bottom. When you subtract, put brackets round the whole top of the second fraction, so that its signs change.

Worked example: two different bottoms

Write {m:frac(3, x+1)+frac(2, x-2)} as a single fraction.

  1. The lowest common denominator is {m:(x+1)(x-2)}.
  2. The first fraction is missing {m:(x-2)}: {m:frac(3, x+1)=frac(3(x-2), (x+1)(x-2))}.
  3. The second fraction is missing {m:(x+1)}: {m:frac(2, x-2)=frac(2(x+1), (x+1)(x-2))}.
  4. Add the tops: {m:3(x-2)+2(x+1)=3x-6+2x+2=5x-4}.

The answer is {m:frac(5x-4, (x+1)(x-2))}. Check with {m:x=3}: {m:frac(3, 4)+frac(2, 1)=frac(11, 4)} and {m:frac(11, 4*1)=frac(11, 4)}.

Worked example: subtracting

Write {m:frac(x+3, x-1)-frac(x-2, x+2)} as a single fraction.

  1. The lowest common denominator is {m:(x-1)(x+2)}.
  2. The top is {m:(x+3)(x+2)-(x-2)(x-1)}. The brackets round the second part change its signs.
  3. Expand each part: {m:(x+3)(x+2)=x^2+5x+6} and {m:(x-2)(x-1)=x^2-3x+2}.
  4. Subtract: {m:x^2+5x+6-x^2+3x-2=8x+4}.

The answer is {m:frac(8x+4, (x-1)(x+2))}. The {m:x^2} terms cancel, so the top has a lower degree than the bottom.

Worked example: a bottom that is a factor of another

Write {m:frac(3, x-2)-frac(4, x^2-4)} as a single fraction.

  1. Factorise the second bottom: {m:x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)}. It already contains {m:(x-2)}.
  2. The lowest common denominator is {m:(x-2)(x+2)}. Use {m:(x-2)} once, not twice.
  3. Only the first fraction needs changing: {m:frac(3, x-2)=frac(3(x+2), (x-2)(x+2))}.
  4. Subtract the tops: {m:3(x+2)-4=3x+6-4=3x+2}.

The answer is {m:frac(3x+2, (x-2)(x+2))}, which is {m:frac(3x+2, x^2-4)}.

  1. The highest power of {m:(x+1)} in either bottom is 2. So the lowest common denominator is {m:(x+1)^2}.

4. Splitting a fraction: two different factors

In lesson 3 you combined two fractions into one. Now you go the other way. Writing one fraction as a sum of simpler fractions is called {term:partial-fractions|partial fractions}. Each simpler fraction has one factor of the original bottom as its own bottom.

For example, {m:frac(5x+1, (x-1)(x+2))} can be written as {m:frac(2, x-1)+frac(3, x+2)}. Add those two with the method of lesson 3 and you get the original fraction back. The task is to find the numbers 2 and 3.

Why split a fraction? A simple fraction such as {m:frac(2, x-1)} is easier to expand as a series and easier to integrate than the original. You will do both in later topics.

Key idea: suppose the bottom is two different factors, {m:(x-a)(x-b)}, and the top is a number or a multiple of x plus a number. Then {m:frac(px+q, (x-a)(x-b))=frac(A, x-a)+frac(B, x-b)} for two numbers A and B. To find them, multiply both sides by {m:(x-a)(x-b)}. This gives {m:px+q=A(x-b)+B(x-a)}, and it is true for every x. Put {m:x=a} to find A, because it makes the B part 0. Put {m:x=b} to find B. You can also compare coefficients.

Worked example: a linear top

Write {m:frac(5x+1, (x-1)(x+2))} in partial fractions.

  2. Multiply both sides by {m:(x-1)(x+2)}: {m:5x+1=A(x+2)+B(x-1)}.
  3. Put {m:x=1}. The B part is 0: {m:6=A*3}, so {m:A=2}.
  4. Put {m:x=-2}. The A part is 0: {m:-9=B*(-3)}, so {m:B=3}.

Worked example: a number on the top

7. Choosing the form, and checking

Every question on partial fractions has the same three steps. Factorise the bottom into straight-line factors. Write the form that matches those factors. Find the letters, and check.

The bottom isWrite
two different factors, {m:(x-a)(x-b)}{m:frac(A, x-a)+frac(B, x-b)}
three different factors, {m:(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)}{m:frac(A, x-a)+frac(B, x-b)+frac(C, x-c)}
a repeated factor, {m:(x-a)(x-b)^2}{m:frac(A, x-a)+frac(B, x-b)+frac(C, (x-b)^2)}

The bottom may be given as a quadratic or a cubic that is multiplied out. Then you factorise it first. For a quadratic, use the methods of Quadratics. For a cubic, use the factor theorem.

Key idea: after you find the letters, check the answer. Put a value of x into the original fraction and into your answer. Do not use a value that makes a bottom 0. The value 0 is often the easiest. If the two results differ, there is a mistake. If they agree, that is a good sign, but one value cannot prove the answer is right.

Extra: when the top is as big as the bottom

Flip it: one fraction ↔ several fractions

Adding fractions and splitting a fraction are opposites. You can go either way, and each way checks the other.

Forwards

From separate fractions to one fraction. Write each over a common bottom, then add the tops.

Backwards

From one fraction to separate fractions. Write the form, multiply through, and find the letters.

Forwards

With {m:A=2}, {m:B=3}, {m:a=1} and {m:b=-2}, the top is {m:2(x+2)+3(x-1)=5x+1}.

Why it works

  • Why you cancel factors and not terms. The fraction {m:frac(a*b, a*c)} equals {m:frac(b, c)} when a is not 0, because dividing the top and the bottom by the same non-zero number does not change the value. A term that is added, as in {m:frac(a+b, a+c)}, is not a multiplier of the whole top, so it cannot be divided out.
  • Why the excluded values stay. A fraction with the bottom 0 has no value. After cancelling, the new fraction may have a value where the old one did not. The two fractions agree only where the old one has a value.
  • Why a common bottom is needed. You can add parts only when they are measured in the same unit. Thirds and quarters are different units. Twelfths measure both. The same is true of {m:frac(1, x-1)} and {m:frac(1, x+2)}.
  • Why dividing is multiplying. Dividing by a number d means multiplying by {m:frac(1, d)}, because {m:frac(1, d)*d=1}. The reciprocal of {m:frac(r, s)} is {m:frac(s, r)}, because {m:frac(r, s)*frac(s, r)=1}, when r and s are not 0.
  • Why you may put in the value that makes a bracket 0. After you multiply through, no fractions are left. Both sides are polynomials in x. They are equal for every x that does not make a bottom 0, which is infinitely many values. Two polynomials that agree at infinitely many values are the same polynomial. So they agree at every x, including the values that make a bottom 0. That is why the substitution is allowed, even though the original fraction has no value there.
  • Why a repeated factor needs two fractions. The bottoms {m:(x+1)} and {m:(x+1)^2} are different. A fraction such as {m:frac(2x+5, (x+1)^2)} needs both {m:frac(A, x+1)} and {m:frac(B, (x+1)^2)}. Multiplying {m:frac(A, x+1)} by {m:(x+1)^2} gives {m:A(x+1)}, which has an x in it. Without that fraction, the top could not have an x term.
  • Why A + B + C = 0. The factors are {m:(x-a)}, {m:(x-b)} and {m:(x-c)}. The top of the identity is {m:A(x-b)(x-c)+B(x-a)(x-c)+C(x-a)(x-b)}. Its {m:x^2} terms add to {m:(A+B+C)x^2}. The left side is a number or a multiple of x plus a number, so it has no {m:x^2} term, and {m:A+B+C=0}.
  • Why each factor gives an asymptote. As x gets close to a value that makes one factor of the bottom 0, the bottom gets close to 0. When the top is not 0 at that value, the fraction becomes very large. That is why each factor has its own dashed line in the figures, and its own partial fraction.

Common traps

  • Cancelling a term instead of a factor. In {m:frac(x+3, x+5)}, the x cannot be cancelled.
  • Forgetting the excluded values after you cancel. The original fraction still has no value where its bottom was 0.
  • Adding the tops and adding the bottoms. Make the bottoms the same first.
  • Not changing every sign when you subtract. Put brackets round the whole top of the second fraction.
  • Using {m:(x+1)^3} as the common bottom of {m:frac(2, x+1)} and {m:frac(3, (x+1)^2)}. The lowest common denominator uses the highest power once.
  • Writing {m:frac(1, (x-1)(x+2))} as {m:frac(1, x-1)+frac(1, x+2)}. The tops A and B must be found, and here they are {m:frac(1, 3)} and {m:-frac(1, 3)}.
  • Putting in {m:x=-a} for a factor {m:(x-a)}. The value that makes {m:(x-a)} zero is {m:x=a}.
  • For a factor such as {m:(2x-1)}, putting in {m:x=1}. The value that makes it 0 is {m:x=frac(1, 2)}.
  • Leaving out a fraction for a repeated factor. {m:(x+2)^2} needs both {m:frac(B, x+2)} and {m:frac(C, (x+2)^2)}.
  • Not checking. Put {m:x=0}, or another value that is allowed, into both sides. A difference shows a mistake.
  • Trying to split a bottom that does not factorise into straight lines, such as {m:x^2+2x+5}. This page does not cover it.
