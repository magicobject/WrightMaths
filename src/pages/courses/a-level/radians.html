Radians, arcs and sectors

Degrees cut a full turn into 360 parts. That number was chosen long ago, and the circle has no say in it. A radian is different: it is measured with the circle itself. One radian is the angle at the centre that cuts off an arc as long as the radius. In radians, the length of an arc and the area of a sector have short formulae. Sine and cosine of a small angle become simple. The graphs keep their shapes, with a new scale on the x-axis. Exact values are written in terms of {m:pi}.

Where you will use this: in Differentiating standard functions (Year 2), where the derivative of sine is cosine only when the angle is in radians. From there, radians are the unit for the calculus of sine and cosine in the rest of Year 2.

1. Measuring an angle with the circle

You have measured angles in degrees so far. A full turn is 360°, a number chosen because it divides neatly. A {term:radian|radian} is a different unit, and it comes from the circle itself.

Draw a circle with radius r and centre O. Mark two points A and B on it so that the {term:arc|arc} AB, the curved part of the circle between them, has length r. The angle between the radii OA and OB is called the {term:central-angle|angle at the centre}. Here it is 1 radian.

How many radians make a full turn? The circumference of a circle is {m:2*pi*r}. Each arc of length r cuts off 1 radian. So the whole circle holds {m:frac(2*pi*r, r)=2*pi} of them. A full turn is {m:2*pi} radians, which is about 6.28. Half a turn is {m:pi} radians.

Key idea: radians.

  • One radian is the angle at the centre of a circle that cuts off an arc as long as the radius.
  • A full turn is {m:2*pi} radians, so {m:180deg=pi} radians.
  • To change degrees to radians, multiply by {m:frac(pi, 180)}. To change radians to degrees, multiply by {m:frac(180, pi)}.
  • At A-level, an angle written without a degree sign, such as {m:theta=2}, is in radians. The unit is sometimes written rad.

3. Arc length

An arc is a part of the circumference. The angle {m:theta} at the centre is the fraction {m:frac(theta, 2*pi)} of a full turn. So the {term:arc-length|arc length} is the same fraction of the circumference {m:2*pi*r}:

{md:s=frac(theta, 2*pi)*2*pi*r=r theta}

Key idea: arc length. [Learn]

  • {m:s=r theta}, where s is the arc length, r is the radius and {m:theta} is the angle at the centre in radians. The radius r is positive, and {m:theta} is between 0 and {m:2*pi}.
  • The perimeter of a {term:sector|sector} is {m:2r+r theta}: the arc and the two straight radii.
  • If the angle is in degrees, change it to radians first.

4. Area of a sector

The {term:area-of-sector|area of a sector} is the same fraction of the area of the whole circle. The fraction is {m:frac(theta, 2*pi)} and the circle has area {m:pi r^2}:

{md:A=frac(theta, 2*pi)*pi r^2=frac(1, 2) r^2 theta}

Key idea: area of a sector. [Learn]

  • {m:A=frac(1, 2) r^2 theta}, where A is the area, r is the radius and {m:theta} is the angle at the centre in radians. The radius r is positive, and {m:theta} is between 0 and {m:2*pi}.
  • Since {m:s=r theta}, you can also write {m:A=frac(1, 2) r s}, where s is the arc length.
  • Check the whole circle: {m:theta=2*pi} gives {m:A=frac(1, 2) r^2*2*pi=pi r^2}.

Why it works

  • Why a full turn is {m:2*pi} radians. One radian cuts off an arc of length r. The whole circumference is {m:2*pi*r}, which is {m:2*pi} radius-lengths. So there are {m:2*pi} radians in a full turn, and {m:pi} in a half turn.
  • Why the formulae need radians. The angle {m:theta} is the fraction {m:frac(theta, 2*pi)} of a full turn only when {m:theta} is in radians. With that fraction, the arc is {m:frac(theta, 2*pi)*2*pi*r=r theta} and the sector is {m:frac(theta, 2*pi)*pi r^2=frac(1, 2) r^2 theta}. In degrees the fraction is {m:frac(theta, 360)}, and the same working leaves a {m:frac(pi, 180)} in each formula.

It cuts the triangle into two right-angled triangles, each with hypotenuse r and angle {m:frac(theta, 2)} at O. The half-chord is opposite that angle, so it is {m:r sin(frac(theta, 2))}.

  • Why the second solution of a tangent equation is {m:alpha+pi}. The tangent repeats every {m:pi}, because {m:tan(x+pi)=frac(sin(x+pi), cos(x+pi))=frac(-sin x, -cos x)=tan x}. Sine and cosine both change sign, and the signs cancel.

Common traps

  • Using {m:s=r theta} or {m:A=frac(1, 2) r^2 theta} with the angle in degrees. Change it to radians first.
  • Leaving out the half in the area of a sector, or squaring r in the arc length.
  • Leaving the two radii out of the perimeter of a sector. A segment has no radii on its boundary: its boundary is the arc and the chord.
  • Writing {m:180deg} as {m:2*pi}, or {m:360deg} as {m:pi}. A half turn is {m:pi}, and a full turn is {m:2*pi}.
  • Rounding {m:pi} to 3.14 early in a question. Keep {m:pi}, or the full value on your calculator, until the last step.
  • Using the wrong pair of solutions. Sine uses {m:alpha} and {m:pi-alpha}. Cosine uses {m:alpha} and {m:2*pi-alpha} in an interval from 0 to {m:2*pi}. Tangent uses {m:alpha} and {m:alpha+pi}.
  • Giving answers in degrees when the interval is in radians, such as {m:0<=x<=2*pi}.
  • Using a small-angle approximation with an angle in degrees, or for an angle that is not small.
  • Forgetting that an angle written with no degree sign is in radians.
  • Having the calculator in degree mode. Check that it shows RAD. The calculator on this page works in degrees only.
