Graphs of polynomials and reciprocals

A sketch is a quick drawing that shows the shape of a graph and the points that matter. You do not need a table of values. This page shows you how to sketch a cubic or a quartic from its factors. You find where it crosses or touches the x-axis, where it meets the y-axis, and what its two ends do. Then you meet the graphs of {m:frac(a, x)} and {m:frac(a, x^2)}, which have asymptotes: lines they get closer and closer to but never touch. Last, you find where two graphs meet by solving their equations together.

Where you will use this: in Transformations of graphs, which moves and stretches the graphs you sketch here. It also helps in Differentiation, where you find the exact turning points that a sketch only shows roughly. Any question that says “sketch the curve” uses the four facts on this page.

1. The ends of a graph, and the y-intercept

A {term:polynomial} is a sum of powers of x, such as {m:x^3-5x^2+2}. When x is very large, the highest power takes over. Try {m:x=100} in {m:y=x^3-5x^2+2}. The term {m:x^3} is 1 000 000 and the term {m:5x^2} is only 50 000. The first is 20 times bigger. So far from the origin, the graph looks like the graph of {m:x^3}.

The {term:leading-term|leading term} is the term with the highest power of x. It decides the {term:end-behaviour|end behaviour}: whether the left end and the right end of the graph go up or down.

Key idea: look at the leading term. Two things decide the ends: the power (odd or even) and the sign of its number.

  • Odd power, positive number: the graph starts at the bottom left and ends at the top right.
  • Odd power, negative number: the graph starts at the top left and ends at the bottom right.
  • Even power, positive number: both ends go up.
  • Even power, negative number: both ends go down.

Worked example: the ends of a graph

Describe the ends of {m:y=-2x^4+x^2-5}.

  1. The highest power is 4, so the leading term is {m:-2x^4}.
  2. The power 4 is even, so both ends point the same way.
  3. The number {m:-2} is negative, so both ends go down.

Worked example: the highest power is not written first

Describe the ends of {m:y=5-x^3}.

  1. Put the terms in order of power: {m:y=-x^3+5}. The leading term is {m:-x^3}.
  2. The power 3 is odd, so the two ends point in opposite directions.
  3. The number {m:-1} is negative, so the graph starts at the top left and ends at the bottom right.

The {term:y-intercept} is where the graph meets the y-axis. At that point {m:x=0}. For an expanded polynomial, the y-intercept is the constant term. For a factorised polynomial, put {m:x=0} into every bracket and multiply the numbers you get.

Worked example: the y-intercept of a factorised cubic

  2. Multiply: {m:2*(-1)*(-3)=6}.
  3. The y-intercept is {m:(0, 6)}.

Worked example: the leading term of a factorised cubic

Describe the ends of {m:y=(2-x)(x+1)^2}.

  2. The leading term is {m:(-x)*x*x=-x^3}.
  3. The power 3 is odd and the number is negative, so the graph starts at the top left and ends at the bottom right.

2. Sketching a cubic from its factors

A {term:sketch} shows the shape of a graph and the points that matter. It is not drawn to scale, and you do not plot a table of values. For a {term:cubic} written in factors you need four facts.

Key idea: the four facts for a sketch.

  • The roots. Set each bracket to 0. Mark each point on the x-axis.
  • Cross or touch. Look at the power of each bracket. Power 1: the graph crosses the axis. Power 2: it touches the axis and turns back. Power 3: it crosses, and the gradient is 0 at the root. This number is the {term:multiplicity}.
  • The y-intercept. Put {m:x=0} into every bracket and multiply.
  • The ends. Multiply the x terms of the brackets to find the leading term.

Then draw one smooth curve through the points. Label the coordinates you found. The turning points between the roots are drawn roughly. A sketch does not need their exact position. You find that with Differentiation.

Worked example: a cubic with three different roots

Sketch {m:y=(x+2)(x-1)(x-3)}.

  1. Roots: {m:x+2=0}, {m:x-1=0} and {m:x-3=0} give {m:x=-2}, {m:x=1} and {m:x=3}.
  2. Each bracket appears once, so the graph crosses the x-axis at all three points.
  3. y-intercept: {m:2*(-1)*(-3)=6}, so {m:(0, 6)}.
  4. Ends: the leading term is {m:x*x*x=x^3}, which is positive. The graph starts at the bottom left and ends at the top right.
  5. Draw it: start low on the left, cross at {m:-2}, rise to a high point a little left of the y-axis, come down through {m:(0, 6)}, cross at 1, dip, cross at 3 and keep rising.

A bracket that appears twice is a {term:repeated-root|repeated root}. The graph touches the x-axis there. It does not cross.

Worked example: a cubic with a repeated root

Sketch {m:y=(x+3)(x-1)^2}.

  1. Roots: {m:x=-3} and {m:x=1}.
  2. The bracket {m:(x+3)} appears once, so the graph crosses at {m:-3}. The bracket {m:(x-1)} is squared, so the graph touches the axis at 1.
  3. y-intercept: {m:3*(-1)^2=3}, so {m:(0, 3)}.
  4. Ends: the leading term is {m:x^3}, which is positive. The graph starts at the bottom left and ends at the top right.

Worked example: a cubic that ends at the bottom right

Sketch {m:y=(2-x)(x+1)^2}.

  1. Roots: {m:2-x=0} gives {m:x=2}. {m:x+1=0} gives {m:x=-1}.
  2. The bracket {m:(x+1)} is squared, so the graph touches the axis at {m:-1}. The bracket {m:(2-x)} appears once, so it crosses at 2.
  3. y-intercept: {m:2*1^2=2}, so {m:(0, 2)}.
  4. Ends: the leading term is {m:-x^3}. The graph starts at the top left and ends at the bottom right.

Worked example: a bracket that appears three times

Sketch {m:y=(x-1)^3}.

  1. The only root is {m:x=1}. The bracket appears three times, which is an odd number, so the graph crosses the axis.
  2. With a power of 3 the gradient is 0 at the root. The graph levels out for a moment and keeps going the same way. This is a {term:point-of-inflection}.
  3. y-intercept: {m:(-1)^3=-1}, so {m:(0, -1)}.
  4. Ends: the leading term is {m:x^3}, which is positive. The graph starts at the bottom left and ends at the top right.

Key idea: the power of a bracket tells you what the graph does at its root.

  • An odd power (1 or 3): the graph crosses the x-axis.
  • An even power (2 or 4): the graph touches the x-axis and turns back.
  • A power of 2 or more makes the graph level with the axis at the root. With 2 it touches and turns back. With 3 the gradient is 0 and it crosses.

If the polynomial is not factorised, factorise it first. Take out any common factor, then factorise what is left.

Worked example: from an expanded cubic

  2. Factorise the quadratic: {m:x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)}. So {m:y=x(x-3)(x+2)}.
  3. The roots are {m:x=0}, {m:x=3} and {m:x=-2}. Each bracket appears once, so the graph crosses at all three.
  4. The constant term is 0, so the y-intercept is {m:(0, 0)}. This is the root at {m:x=0}.

Worked example: brackets with a number in front of x

Sketch {m:y=x(2x-1)(x+2)}.

  1. Roots: {m:x=0}. Then {m:2x-1=0} gives {m:x=0.5}, and {m:x+2=0} gives {m:x=-2}. Each bracket appears once, so the graph crosses at all three.
  2. y-intercept: put {m:x=0} into the first bracket and the product is 0, so {m:(0, 0)}. This is the root {m:x=0}.
  3. Ends: multiply the x terms: {m:x*2x*x=2x^3}. The number 2 is positive, so the graph starts at the bottom left and ends at the top right.
  4. The 2 in {m:(2x-1)} does not make the root 2. It makes the root {m:x=0.5}.

3. Sketching a quartic from its factors

A {term:quartic} has degree 4. It has up to four roots, and its two ends point the same way. You use the same four facts as for a cubic: the roots, cross or touch, the y-intercept and the ends. The ends come from the leading term, which is an even power.

Worked example: a quartic with four different roots

Sketch {m:y=(x+3)(x+1)(x-2)(x-4)}.

  1. Roots: {m:x=-3}, {m:x=-1}, {m:x=2} and {m:x=4}. Each bracket appears once, so the graph crosses at all four.
  2. y-intercept: {m:3*1*(-2)*(-4)=24}, so {m:(0, 24)}.
  3. Ends: the leading term is {m:x^4}, which is positive. Both ends go up.
  4. Draw it: come down from the top left, cross at {m:-3}, dip, cross at {m:-1}, rise past {m:(0, 24)}, cross at 2, dip, cross at 4 and rise.

4. Reciprocal graphs

The reciprocal of a number is 1 divided by it. A reciprocal graph divides a number by x. The equation is {m:y=frac(a, x)}, where a is a number that is not 0. Try {m:y=frac(6, x)}.

  • When {m:x=1}, {m:y=6}. When {m:x=2}, {m:y=3}. When {m:x=6}, {m:y=1}.
  • As x gets bigger, y gets smaller and closer to 0. It never reaches 0.
  • As x gets closer to 0 from the positive side, y gets bigger and bigger.
  • When {m:x=0} you cannot divide by 0. So the graph has no point on the y-axis.

The graph gets closer and closer to the two axes but never touches them. A line like this is an {term:asymptote}. The x-axis, {m:y=0}, is one. The y-axis, {m:x=0}, is the other. 5. Where two graphs meet

At a {term:point-of-intersection|point where two graphs meet}, both graphs have the same x and the same y. So set the two expressions for y equal to each other. Solve for x. Then put each x back into one equation to find y.

Key idea: the steps for where two graphs meet.

  • Write the two y-expressions equal to each other.
  • Rearrange so that one side is 0, then factorise and solve.
  • Put each x into the simpler equation to find y. Check in the other equation.
  • The number of solutions is the number of points. A sketch of both graphs shows how many to expect.

Worked example: a reciprocal graph and a line

Find where {m:y=frac(6, x)} meets {m:y=x+1}.

  3. Rearrange: {m:x^2+x-6=0}. Factorise: {m:(x+3)(x-2)=0}. So {m:x=-3} or {m:x=2}.
  4. Put the values into {m:y=x+1}: {m:x=-3} gives {m:y=-2}, and {m:x=2} gives {m:y=3}.
  5. Check in {m:y=frac(6, x)}: {m:frac(6, -3)=-2} and {m:frac(6, 2)=3}.
  6. The graphs meet at {m:(-3, -2)} and {m:(2, 3)}.

Worked example: a cubic and a line

6. Proportion

Two quantities are in {term:proportional|proportion} when one is a fixed number times the other. The sign ∝ reads “is proportional to”. The fixed number is k.

  • {m:y=kx^n} means y is proportional to {m:x^n}. For example, {m:y=kx^2} means y is proportional to {m:x^2}.
  • {m:y=frac(k, x)} means y is {term:inversely-proportional|inversely proportional} to x. Its graph is a reciprocal graph.
  • {m:y=k*sqrt(x)} means y is proportional to the square root of x. Its graph is half of a parabola on its side, starting at the origin.

Key idea: to use a proportion, write the equation with k, find k from one known point, then use the equation. The graph shows which kind of proportion it is.

  • {m:y=kx} is a straight line through the origin.
  • {m:y=kx^2} is a parabola through the origin. It has the same shape as {m:y=x^2}, but it is steeper.
  • {m:y=frac(k, x)} is a reciprocal graph, with the asymptotes {m:x=0} and {m:y=0}.
  • {m:y=k*sqrt(x)} starts at the origin and exists only for {m:x >= 0}.

Why it works

  • The leading term wins far away. When x is very large, the highest power is far bigger than all the others. So the ends of the graph follow the leading term.
  • A factor gives a root. If {m:(x-a)} is a factor, then putting {m:x=a} makes one bracket 0, and the whole product is 0. That is why each bracket gives a point on the x-axis.
  • An odd power keeps the sign change. Shortly before a root a bracket {m:(x-a)} is negative, and soon after it is positive. Raise it to an odd power and the sign still changes, so the graph crosses. Raise it to an even power and the sign does not change, so the graph touches and turns back.
  • The y-intercept is x = 0. Every point on the y-axis has {m:x=0}. So you put 0 into each bracket and multiply.
  • A reciprocal graph has no point at x = 0. You cannot divide by 0. As x gets close to 0, the size of {m:frac(a, x)} grows without limit: it is very large and positive on one side of 0 and very large and negative on the other. As x grows, it shrinks towards 0. That is why the axes are asymptotes.
  • The product xy stays constant. If {m:y=frac(a, x)}, then {m:x*y=a}. Doubling x halves y, which is inverse proportion.
  • Where graphs meet, the y-values are equal. Both equations hold at the same point. So setting the two expressions for y equal gives every x where the graphs meet.

Common traps

  • Copying the signs from the brackets. The bracket {m:(x+3)} gives the root {m:x=-3}.
  • Adding the numbers to find the y-intercept. Multiply them.
  • Forgetting the number in front of the brackets when you find the y-intercept.
  • Drawing a crossing where a bracket is squared. A squared bracket touches the axis and turns back.
  • Counting a repeated root as two points on the axis. It is one point.
  • Reading the ends from the first term written, instead of from the highest power. {m:5-x^3} has the leading term {m:-x^3}.
  • Missing a minus sign hidden in a bracket such as {m:(2-x)}, which turns the leading term negative.
  • Giving {m:y=frac(a, x^2)} one branch in each of two corners. Both branches are on the same side of the x-axis.
  • Dividing both sides by x when solving, which loses the solution {m:x=0}.
  • Giving a point as (y, x). A point is written with x first.
  • Leaving out the coordinates of the points on a sketch, or drawing the curve through an asymptote.
