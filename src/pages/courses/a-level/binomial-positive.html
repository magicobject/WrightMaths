Binomial expansion (positive whole-number powers)

A bracket such as {m:(2+3x)^5} means five brackets multiplied together. Multiplying them out one by one is slow, and a slip is likely. This page gives you a fast, reliable method. You will use Pascal’s triangle and the “n choose r” button on your calculator. You will expand brackets, pick out one term, find a missing number from a coefficient, and use the expansion to estimate a number such as {m:1.02^8}.

Where you will use this: in Binomial expansion for fractional and negative powers in Year 2, which carries on from this page. The same coefficients also give the probabilities of a binomial distribution in statistics.

1. Expanding a bracket, and Pascal’s triangle

A {term:binomial} is a bracket with two terms in it, such as {m:(a+b)} or {m:(1+2x)}. To expand a bracket is to multiply it out. You already know {m:(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2}. Multiply that by one more {m:(a+b)} and you get the cube:

{m:(a+b)^3=(a+b)(a^2+2ab+b^2)=a^3+3a^2 b+3a b^2+b^3}.

Look at the pattern. It is the same for every power n.

Key idea: the shape of the {term:binomial-expansion|binomial expansion} of {m:(a+b)^n}.

  • There are {m:n+1} terms.
  • The power of a starts at n and goes down by 1 each term, to 0.
  • The power of b starts at 0 and goes up by 1 each term, to n.
  • In every term the two powers add up to n.
  • The numbers in front of the terms are the {term:coefficient|coefficients}. They come from {term:pascals-triangle|Pascal’s triangle}.

Pascal’s triangle is a triangle of numbers. Each row starts and ends with 1. Every other number is the sum of the two numbers above it. The top row, a single 1, is row 0. Row n gives the coefficients for the power n.

2. Factorials and nCr

Pascal’s triangle is quick for a small power. For a big power, such as 15, it would take too many rows. You need a way to find any number in the triangle directly.

A {term:factorial|factorial} is a product of whole numbers counting down to 1. The symbol {m:n!} is read “n factorial”. For example {m:5! =5*4*3*2*1=120}. By agreement {m:0! =1}.

The {term:ncr|nCr} number is the number in row n, position r of Pascal’s triangle. Position counting starts at 0. It is read “n choose r”. It is written Crn. The stacked form (nr) means the same thing.

Key idea: the formula for Crn.

{md:frac(n!, r!(n-r)!)}
  • Your calculator has an nCr button. Use it to check your working.
  • Crn and Cn−rn are equal, because the triangle is the same read from the left or from the right.
  • C0n and Cnn are both 1. These are the ends of each row.

Why does this count the right thing? To expand {m:(a+b)^n}, you pick a or b from each of the n brackets. A term with {m:b^r} comes from picking b in exactly r of the brackets. The number of ways to choose which r brackets is Crn. That is why Crn is the coefficient.

4. Finding one term or one coefficient

Sometimes you do not need the whole expansion. A question may ask for “the coefficient of {m:x^3}” or “the fourth term”. Use the general term and work out one value of r.

Key idea: find r first.

  • For “the term in {m:x^3}”, the power of x is r, so {m:r=3}.
  • For “the fourth term”, count from the first term, which has {m:r=0}. The fourth term has {m:r=3}.
  • The coefficient is the number in front of the power of x. Include its sign.

Why it works

  • Pascal’s triangle. Each term of {m:(a+b)^n} comes from {m:(a+b)^(n-1)} multiplied by one more bracket. A term in the expansion of {m:(a+b)^n} is made two ways: from the term of {m:(a+b)^(n-1)} with the same power of b, times a; or from the term with one power of b fewer, times b. So each new coefficient is the sum of two coefficients in the row above.
  • nCr as a coefficient. Each term of {m:(a+b)^n} picks a or b from every one of the n brackets. A term with {m:b^r} needs b from exactly r brackets. The number of ways to choose those r brackets is Crn.
  • The formula. There are {m:n!} ways to put n different things in a line. Choosing r of them counts each group of r in {m:r!} orders, and each leftover group of {m:n-r} in {m:(n-r)!} orders. Dividing by both gives {m:frac(n!, r!(n-r)!)}.
  • The symmetry. Choosing which r brackets give b is the same as choosing which {m:n-r} give a. So Crn = Cn−rn.
  • Estimating. When x is small, {m:x^2}, {m:x^3} and the later powers are tiny. The first few terms hold almost all of the value.
  • The check at x = 1. Put {m:x=1} in {m:(a+bx)^n} and you get {m:(a+b)^n}. The coefficients add up to that. It is a quick test of any expansion.

Common traps

  • Writing {m:(a+b)^n=a^n+b^n}. The middle terms are missing.
  • Not putting brackets round {m:bx}. The power applies to the number b as well: {m:(2x)^3=8x^3}, not {m:2x^3}.
  • Using the wrong row of the triangle. The row number is the power. The top row is row 0.
  • Forgetting that there are {m:n+1} terms, not n.
  • Losing the signs in {m:(a-bx)^n}. The odd powers of the negative term are negative, so the signs alternate.
  • Using r = 3 for the third term. The first term has {m:r=0}.
  • Mixing up the power of x with the power of a. In the term in {m:x^r}, the power of a is {m:n-r}.
  • Forgetting the second factorial in Crn. The bottom is {m:r!(n-r)!}.
  • Keeping {m:n=-5} when n must be a positive whole number.
  • Giving only k = 3 when the equation is {m:k^2=9}, and k = −3 also works (unless the question rules it out).
  • Using too few terms to estimate, or using this method when x is not small.
