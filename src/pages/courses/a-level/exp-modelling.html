Exponential modelling

Some quantities grow or shrink by the same factor in every equal step. A formula such as {m:N=100*1.5^t} describes them. This page shows what each number in the formula means, and what happens when t is large. Then it shows how to turn a curve into a straight line using logarithms, so that you can read the numbers off a graph. You will meet the gradient of {m:e^(k x)}. Last, you will ask when a model can be trusted, and how to improve it.

Where you will use this: in Differentiating standard functions, where you prove the gradient of {m:e^(k x)}. In First-order differential equations, to solve problems about a rate of change. Also in other subjects, wherever a graph of real data has to be turned into a formula.

2. The model with e, and finding the constants

The same family of curves can be written with the number e. The usual form is {m:y=A e^(k t)}. The number k is called the {term:rate-constant|rate constant}. Its sign decides the shape.

Key idea: in the model {m:y=A e^(k t)}, take {m:A>0}. The number A is the initial value, because {m:e^0=1}. If {m:k>0}, y grows and becomes larger than any number you choose. If {m:k<0}, y decays towards 0 and never reaches it. The model is the same as {m:y=A*b^t} when {m:b=e^k}, which means {m:k=ln b}. Each unit of t multiplies y by {m:e^k}. It does not multiply y by k.

Worked example: change the form

Write {m:y=5*1.5^t} in the form {m:y=5 e^(k t)}.

  1. Use {m:e^(ln b)=b}. So {m:1.5=e^(ln 1.5)}.
  2. Then {m:1.5^t=(e^(ln 1.5))^t=e^((ln 1.5) t)}.
  3. So {m:k=ln 1.5=0.4055}, and {m:y=5 e^(0.4055 t)}.

Check: {m:e^(0.4055)=1.5}, so each unit of t multiplies y by 1.5.

Worked example: what k means

A population is {m:P=50 e^(0.3t)}. By what percentage does it rise in each unit of time? Give your answer to 2 significant figures.

  2. {m:e^(0.3)=1.3499}, which is 1.35 to 3 significant figures.

A model with a limiting value

Some quantities do not decay to 0. A hot drink cools towards the temperature of the room. A model for that adds a constant: {m:y=c+A e^(-k t)}.

Key idea: take {m:y=c+A e^(-k t)} with A and k positive. The term {m:A e^(-k t)} is positive and gets close to 0 as t gets large. So y gets close to c from above. The number c is the {term:limiting-value|limiting value}, and the line {m:y=c} is an asymptote. The initial value is {m:c+A}, not A.

3. Straight lines from y = a xn

A curve is hard to read. A straight line can be read quickly: its {term:gradient} and its intercept show the numbers. The method of {term:linearising|reducing to a straight line} uses logarithms to turn a curve into a straight line.

Take the power model {m:y=a x^n}. Here a and x and y are positive. Take logarithms of both sides, and use the product law and then the power law:

{md:log y=log(a x^n)=log a+n log x}

Compare with the straight line {m:Y=m X+c}. Here {m:Y=log y} and {m:X=log x}. The gradient is {m:m=n} and the intercept is {m:c=log a}.

Key idea: if {m:y=a x^n} with {m:a>0} and {m:x>0}, then {m:log y=n log x+log a}. So the graph of {m:log y} (vertical) against {m:log x} (horizontal) is a straight line. Its gradient is n. Its vertical intercept is {m:log a}, not a. Use the same base for both logarithms. To get a back, undo the logarithm: {m:a=10^c} for base 10, or {m:a=e^c} for {m:ln}.

This graph is a {term:log-log-graph|log-log graph}, because both axes show logarithms. The gradient between two points on it is {m:n=frac(log y_2-log y_1, log x_2-log x_1)}.

6. Assumptions and refining a model

A model is a simple description of something real. It rests on assumptions. If an assumption is false, the model can give nonsense, even when the arithmetic is right.

Key idea: the model {m:y=A*b^t} assumes that y is multiplied by the same factor in every equal step, and that nothing limits it. To judge a model, compare what it predicts with real data, and ask whether its assumptions are reasonable. To refine a model, change the assumption that failed, and then test the new model against the data again.

Worked example: a model for fish

  1. Set {m:500 e^(0.2t)=20000}. Divide by 500: {m:e^(0.2t)=40}.
  2. Take ln of both sides: {m:0.2t=ln 40=3.6889}.
  3. So {m:t=frac(3.6889, 0.2)=18.44}.

After 18.4 years the model predicts more than the lake can hold. At {m:t=30}, it predicts {m:500 e^6=201714}, about 202000 fish. The model assumes that the rate of growth is proportional to the number of fish for ever. That ignores limits on food and space.

Why it works

  • Why A is the initial value. Put {m:t=0} into {m:A*b^t}. Since {m:b^0=1}, you get A. In {m:A e^(k t)}, {m:e^0=1} gives A in the same way.
  • Why b is a factor. {m:A*b^(t+1)=A*b^t*b}. Each step of 1 in t multiplies the value by b. That is why a fall of r% has {m:b=1-frac(r, 100)}.
  • Why {m:b=e^k}. Any positive b can be written as {m:b=e^(ln b)}. So {m:A*b^t=A*(e^(ln b))^t=A e^((ln b) t)}, and {m:k=ln b}.
  • Why the large-t table shows what it does. If {m:b>1}, write {m:b=1+d} with {m:d>0}. Expanding the bracket gives {m:(1+d)^t>=1+t d} for whole t, and {m:1+t d} passes any number you choose. If {m:0
  • Why logarithms give a straight line. The product law turns a product into a sum, and the power law brings a power down in front. For {m:y=a x^n}, that gives {m:log y=log a+n log x}. For {m:y=k*b^x}, it gives {m:log y=log k+x log b}. Each is of the form {m:Y=m X+c}.
  • Why the gradient of {m:e^(k x)} is {m:k e^(k x)}. For {m:k>0}, the curve {m:y=e^(k x)} is the curve {m:y=e^x} stretched along the x-axis by a factor of {m:frac(1, k)}. That makes every slope k times as steep. The curve {m:y=e^x} has gradient equal to its height, so the gradient of {m:e^(k x)} is k times its height. For {m:k<0} the same result holds, and {m:k=0} gives a constant. Both are proved in Differentiating standard functions. The table of chords showed this for {m:k=2} at {m:x=0}.
  • Why a model can fail. A model is a rule chosen to fit some data. It says nothing outside the range of the data unless its assumptions stay true. Checking a prediction against a measurement tests the assumptions as well as the arithmetic.

Common traps

  • Using the percentage as the factor. A fall of 15% has {m:b=0.85}, not 0.15. A rise of 15% has {m:b=1.15}.
  • Treating k as a percentage. In {m:A e^(k t)}, the factor is {m:e^k}. A rate constant {m:k=0.3} is a rise of about 35%.
  • Forgetting that a decaying quantity does not reach 0. It gets closer and closer, but it is never 0.
  • Giving A as the initial value of {m:c+A e^(-k t)}. The initial value is {m:c+A}.
  • Taking ln of a sum without isolating the exponential first. In {m:20+60 e^(-0.1t)=30}, subtract 20 and divide by 60 before you take ln.
  • Plotting the wrong graph. A power model {m:y=a x^n} gives a line of {m:log y} against {m:log x}. An exponential model {m:y=k*b^x} gives a line of {m:log y} against x.
  • Reading the intercept as a, or the gradient as b. The intercept is {m:log a} (or {m:log k}) and the gradient is {m:log b}. Undo the logarithm.
  • Mixing the bases: using ln for the gradient and then raising 10 to the power. Raise the same base that the logarithm used.
  • Rounding in the middle. Keep the full calculator value for k until the last line.
  • Forgetting the factor A in the gradient. The gradient of {m:A e^(k x)} is {m:A k e^(k x)}, not {m:k e^(k x)}.
  • Trusting a model far outside the data it was built from. Ask what it assumes, and whether that is still true.
