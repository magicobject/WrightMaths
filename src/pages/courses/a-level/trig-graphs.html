Trigonometric graphs and exact values

In the last topic every angle was inside a triangle. Here an angle can be any size: 150°, 480° or −60°. You will meet the unit circle, which gives sine, cosine and tangent for every angle. You will learn the exact values at 30°, 45° and 60°, and draw the graphs of sine, cosine and tangent. Then you will use a graph to find every angle that has a given value. All angles on this page are in degrees.

Where you will use this: in Trigonometric identities and equations, which solves equations like these by algebra. It is also needed in Radians, arcs and sectors (Year 2), where angles are measured in a new unit. The graphs here are the starting point for Transformations of graphs.

1. Angles of any size

In a right-angled triangle an angle is less than 90°. To use sine, cosine and tangent for any angle, we need a new picture. It is the {term:unit-circle|unit circle}: a circle of radius 1 with its centre at the origin.

Measure an angle from the positive x-axis. Turn anticlockwise for a positive angle and clockwise for a negative angle. The line at that angle meets the circle at a point P.

Key idea: the point P on the unit circle at angle {m:theta} has coordinates {m:(cos theta, sin theta)}.

  • {m:cos theta} is the x-coordinate of P.
  • {m:sin theta} is the y-coordinate of P.
  • {m:tan theta=frac(y, x)} is the y-coordinate divided by the x-coordinate. It is the gradient of the line from the origin to P.

For an angle in a right-angled triangle this gives the same answers as before. It also works for angles of 90° or more.

Worked example: reading sine, cosine and tangent from the circle

Use the figure to find {m:sin(150deg)}, {m:cos(150deg)} and {m:tan(150deg)}.

  1. The y-coordinate of P is 0.5, so {m:sin(150deg)=0.5}.
  2. The x-coordinate of P is {m:-0.866}, so {m:cos(150deg)=-0.866}.
  3. {m:tan(150deg)=frac(0.5, -0.866)=-0.577}.

The cosine and the tangent are negative because P is to the left of the y-axis.

The axes split the plane into four {term:quadrant|quadrants}. Quadrant 1 is the top right. The numbers go anticlockwise. The signs of the coordinates tell you which of sine, cosine and tangent are positive.

The {term:cast-diagram|CAST diagram} is a way to remember this. Start in the bottom right and go anticlockwise: Cos, All, Sin, Tan. Reading the figure from the top right instead, the order is All, Sin, Tan, Cos.

Worked example: the signs

  3. Tangent is a negative divided by a negative, so tangent is positive.

2. Exact values

An {term:exact-value|exact value} is written with fractions and surds, not as a rounded decimal. Three special angles have exact values: 30°, 45° and 60°. You can rebuild them from two triangles.

The first is half of an equilateral triangle with side 2. Cutting it down the middle gives a right-angled triangle. Its hypotenuse is 2 and its short side is 1. Pythagoras gives the third side: {m:sqrt(2^2-1^2)=sqrt(3)}. The angles are 30°, 60° and 90°.

The second is half of a square with side 1, cut along its diagonal. It has two sides of 1 and a hypotenuse of {m:sqrt(2)}. Its angles are 45°, 45° and 90°.

Now read off the ratios. Sine is opposite over hypotenuse, cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse, and tangent is opposite over adjacent.

Key idea: learn this table, or rebuild it from the two triangles.

{m:sin theta}0{m:frac(1,2)}{m:frac(sqrt(2),2)}{m:frac(sqrt(3),2)}1
{m:cos theta}1{m:frac(sqrt(3),2)}{m:frac(sqrt(2),2)}{m:frac(1,2)}0
{m:tan theta}0{m:frac(sqrt(3),3)}1{m:sqrt(3)}does not exist

The tangent of 30° can also be written {m:frac(1, sqrt(3))}. It is the same number. The tangent of 90° does not exist, because the x-coordinate is 0 and you cannot divide by 0.

There is a pattern in the sine row. It reads {m:frac(sqrt(0),2)}, {m:frac(sqrt(1),2)}, {m:frac(sqrt(2),2)}, {m:frac(sqrt(3),2)} and {m:frac(sqrt(4),2)}. The cosine row is the same list backwards.

3. The graphs of sine and cosine

Draw the angle on the x-axis and the value of the sine on the y-axis. Each angle gives one point. Joining the points gives the graph of {m:y=sin x}.

The graph does not stop at 360°. It repeats the same shape again and again, in both directions. The length of one full repeat is the {term:period}. For sine the period is 360°. The {term:amplitude} is the distance from the middle line to the top. For {m:y=sin x} the amplitude is 1.

The graph of {m:y=cos x} has the same shape. It starts at its highest point instead of at zero. It is the sine graph moved 90° to the left.

Key idea: sine and cosine both have period 360° and amplitude 1.

  • Their values always lie between {m:-1} and 1: {m:-1<=sin x<=1} and {m:-1<=cos x<=1}.
  • They repeat: {m:sin(x+360deg)=sin x} and {m:cos(x+360deg)=cos x}.
  • Sine is 0 at 0°, 180° and 360°. Cosine is 0 at 90° and 270°.

Worked example: reading values from the graphs

Write down {m:sin(270deg)}, {m:cos(180deg)} and {m:cos(450deg)}.

  1. On the sine graph the lowest point is at 270°, so {m:sin(270deg)=-1}.
  2. On the cosine graph the lowest point is at 180°, so {m:cos(180deg)=-1}.
  3. {m:450-360=90}, so {m:cos(450deg)=cos(90deg)=0}.

Symmetry

The graphs have symmetry. It lets you find a value from another value you already know.

The sine graph has a mirror line at {m:x=90deg}. Two angles the same distance either side of 90° have the same sine. Those angles add up to 180°.

The sine graph also looks the same after a half turn about the origin. A positive angle and the matching negative angle give values of opposite sign.

The cosine graph is symmetrical about the y-axis. A positive angle and the matching negative angle give the same value.

Key idea: three symmetries to use.

  • {m:sin(180deg-x)=sin x}. The sine graph has a mirror line at 90°.
  • {m:sin(-x)=-sin x}. A half turn about the origin.
  • {m:cos(-x)=cos x}. The cosine graph has a mirror line at the y-axis. Because cosine repeats every 360°, {m:cos(360deg-x)=cos x} too.

Worked example: using symmetry

  1. {m:325=360-35}, so {m:cos(325deg)=cos(35deg)=0.819}.
  2. {m:160=180-20}, so {m:sin(160deg)=sin(20deg)=0.342}.
  3. {m:sin(-20deg)=-sin(20deg)=-0.342}.

4. The graph of tangent

The {term:tangent-function|tangent} of an angle is the y-coordinate divided by the x-coordinate on the unit circle. This is a problem at 90° and at 270°. There the x-coordinate is 0 and you cannot divide by 0.

Near those angles the tangent gets very large. A little below 90° it is a large positive number. A little above 90° it is a large negative number. The graph has a vertical {term:asymptote}: a line that the curve gets closer to but never touches.

Key idea: the graph of {m:y=tan x}.

  • The period is 180°: {m:tan(x+180deg)=tan x}. This is half the period of sine and cosine.
  • There are asymptotes at {m:x=90deg}, {m:x=270deg} and every 180° after that, and also at {m:x=-90deg}.
  • The graph goes through the origin and rises between each pair of asymptotes. It takes every value, so it has no highest or lowest value and no amplitude.
  • {m:tan(-x)=-tan x}. The graph looks the same after a half turn about the origin.

Worked example: using the period

Find {m:tan(225deg)} and {m:tan(300deg)}, giving exact values.

  1. The period is 180°, so {m:tan(225deg)=tan(225deg-180deg)=tan(45deg)=1}.
  2. In the same way {m:tan(300deg)=tan(120deg)}.
  3. {m:120deg} is in quadrant 2. The matching acute angle is 60° and tangent is negative there. {m:tan(120deg)=-tan(60deg)=-sqrt(3)}.

Worked example: why there is an asymptote at 90°

Explain why {m:tan(90deg)} does not exist.

  2. {m:tan(90deg)=frac(1, 0)}. You cannot divide by 0.
  3. So the graph has no point at 90°. It has an asymptote there instead.

5. Finding every angle with a given value

Suppose you are told {m:sin x=0.5} and asked for every x between 0° and 360°. Draw the graph of sine and add the horizontal line {m:y=0.5}. Every point where the line cuts the curve is a solution.

Key idea: to find every angle in a range with a given sine, cosine or tangent:

  1. Sketch the graph for the range and draw the horizontal line at the given value. Each point where they meet is a solution, so you know how many to find.
  2. Use your calculator, or an exact value, to find the matching acute angle. For a negative value, use the size of the value.
  3. Use the symmetry of the graph and the quadrants to find the other angles.
  4. Check that every answer lies inside the range.

For sine, the pairs are {m:x} and {m:180deg-x}. For cosine, they are {m:x} and {m:360deg-x}. For tangent, they are {m:x} and {m:x+180deg}.

Worked example: sine

Solve {m:sin x=0.5} for {m:0deg<=x<=360deg}.

  1. The calculator gives {m:sin^(-1)(0.5)=30deg}. This is the first solution.
  2. Use the mirror line at 90°: the other solution is {m:180-30=150}.
  3. The solutions are {m:x=30deg} and {m:x=150deg}.

When the value is negative, find the matching acute angle from the size of the value. Then use the quadrants to place it.

Worked example: cosine with a negative value

  3. In quadrant 3 the angle is {m:180+60=240}. (This is also {m:360-120}, as the symmetry rule says.)
  4. The solutions are {m:x=120deg} and {m:x=240deg}.

Worked example: tangent

  3. The period is 180°, so add 180°: {m:135+180=315}.

6. Period and amplitude

A wave can be taller or more squashed than {m:y=sin x}. You can read two things from the graph: how tall it is and how long one cycle takes.

A number in front of the sine changes the amplitude. The graph of {m:y=2 sin x} has amplitude 2. It is twice as tall, but it crosses the x-axis in the same places.

A number multiplying the angle changes the period. The graph of {m:y=sin 2x} goes through a full cycle in 180°, not 360°. It is squashed from the sides.

Key idea: for positive numbers a and b:

  • {m:y=a sin x} and {m:y=a cos x} have amplitude a. The values lie between {m:-a} and a.
  • {m:y=sin(bx)} and {m:y=cos(bx)} have period {m:frac(360deg, b)}. The graph is squashed from the sides if b is more than 1, and stretched if b is less than 1.
  • {m:y=tan(bx)} has period {m:frac(180deg, b)}.

The full rules for stretching and moving any graph are in Transformations of graphs. Here you only read the amplitude and the period.

Worked example: amplitude and period

State the amplitude of {m:y=3 cos x}, and the period of {m:y=cos(frac(x,2))}.

  1. The number in front of {m:cos x} is 3, so the amplitude is 3. The values lie between {m:-3} and 3.
  2. In {m:cos(frac(x,2))} the multiplier of x is {m:frac(1,2)}, so {m:b=frac(1,2)}.
  3. The period is {m:frac(360deg, frac(1,2))=720deg}. The graph is stretched, so one cycle takes longer.

Worked example: the asymptotes of a stretched tangent

Find the period of {m:y=tan(3x)} and the first asymptote after the y-axis.

  1. The period is {m:frac(180deg, 3)=60deg}.
  2. The graph of {m:y=tan x} has its first asymptote at 90°. For {m:tan(3x)} that happens when {m:3x=90deg}.
  3. So the first asymptote is at {m:x=30deg}. The next ones are 60° apart, at 90°, 150° and so on.

Worked example: counting solutions on a squashed graph

  2. The line {m:y=0.5} cuts each cycle twice.
  3. So there are {m:2*2=4} solutions.

Finding those four angles by algebra is covered in Trigonometric identities and equations.

Flip it: from the angle to the value, and back

You have read a value from an angle, and found angles from a value. Each is the reverse of the other.

Forwards

From an angle to its value. Pick x on the x-axis, go up to the curve and read the height. There is exactly one answer.

{m:sin(210deg)=-0.5}.

From a value to the angles. Draw a horizontal line at that height and read every x where it meets the curve. There can be many answers, or none.

{m:sin x=-0.5} gives {m:x=210deg} and {m:x=330deg} in one turn.

{m:cos^(-1)(-0.2)=101.5deg}. The other solution in the first turn is {m:360-101.5=258.5}. Adding 360° gives {m:101.5+360=461.5} and {m:258.5+360=618.5}.

Why it works

  • The unit circle. In a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 1, the side next to the angle is {m:cos theta} and the side opposite is {m:sin theta}. Putting that triangle in the circle makes the next-to side the x-coordinate and the opposite side the y-coordinate. Using the coordinates for any angle extends the idea, and it agrees with the old definition when the angle is acute.
  • The exact values. An equilateral triangle of side 2 has three angles of 60°. Its height splits it into two triangles with angles 30°, 60° and 90°. The base of each is 1 and the hypotenuse is 2. Pythagoras gives the height {m:sqrt(3)}. A square of side 1 has a diagonal of {m:sqrt(2)}, which gives the 45° triangle.
  • Period. A full turn of 360° returns to the same point on the circle, so the coordinates repeat. That is why sine and cosine repeat every 360°. Tangent is the gradient of the line to the point. Half a turn, 180°, gives the same line, so tangent repeats every 180°.
  • The symmetries. The angle {m:180deg-x} gives the point reflected in the y-axis. Its y-coordinate is the same, so {m:sin(180deg-x)=sin x}. The angle {m:-x} gives the point reflected in the x-axis. Its y-coordinate changes sign, so {m:sin(-x)=-sin x}, and its x-coordinate stays the same, so {m:cos(-x)=cos x}.
  • The asymptotes. {m:tan theta=frac(y, x)}. When the x-coordinate is 0, at 90° and 270°, the division is impossible. Close to those angles the x-coordinate is tiny and the quotient is very large.
  • Period of {m:sin(bx)}. The graph of {m:sin(bx)} is at the same height when {m:bx} has gone up by 360°. That takes {m:x} up by {m:frac(360deg, b)}.

Common traps

  • Using a calculator set to radians. Check that it shows DEG.
  • Giving only the calculator’s answer. An equation like {m:sin x=0.5} usually has more than one solution in a range.
  • Using the wrong pair. Sine uses {m:x} and {m:180deg-x}. Cosine uses {m:x} and {m:360deg-x}. Tangent uses {m:x} and {m:x+180deg}.
  • Forgetting the sign. Work out which quadrant the angle is in, and use it to decide whether the answer is positive or negative.
  • Giving an answer outside the range. Always check the end points.
  • Saying tangent has period 360°. Its period is 180°.
  • Multiplying instead of dividing for the period. {m:sin(3x)} has period {m:frac(360deg, 3)}, not {m:360deg*3}.
  • Using the wrong matching acute angle. For 150° it is {m:180-150=30}, not 150 or {m:150-90}.
