Definite integrals and area

An integral with limits is a number. This page shows how to find that number, how it gives the area between a curve and the x-axis, and when it does not. You also find the area between a line and a curve.

Where you will use this: finding areas of curved regions, working out a quantity from its rate of change, and the area between two curves and the trapezium rule in Year 2.

1. The definite integral and area

A curve has no formula for the area under it, the way a rectangle has. Look at the shaded region. It is bounded by the curve, the x-axis, and two vertical lines.

An integral with a lower value and an upper value written on the integral sign is a {term:definite-integral}. The two values are the {term:limits-of-integration|limits}. The lower limit is where the region starts and the upper limit is where it stops. Both are values of x.

For the region in the figure, the lower limit is 1 and the upper limit is 3. We write it {m:int(1, 3) x^2 dx}. Read it as "the integral from 1 to 3 of x squared, dx". Compare this with an {term:indefinite-integral}, which has no limits and ends in {m:+c}.

Key idea: let {m:f(x)} be a function whose graph is unbroken from {m:x=a} to {m:x=b}, with {m:a < b}. Suppose {m:f(x) >= 0} for every x from a to b: the curve is on or above the x-axis. Then the area of the region between the curve, the x-axis and the lines {m:x=a} and {m:x=b} is the definite integral {m:int(a, b) f(x) dx}. A definite integral is a number, so it has no {m:+c}.

The condition {m:f(x) >= 0} matters. Section 4 shows what happens when the curve is below the axis. The area under a curve is measured in square units.

Worked example

Write the area of the shaded region in the figure as a definite integral.

  1. The region starts at {m:x=1} and stops at {m:x=3}. These are the limits. The lower limit is 1.
  2. The curve {m:y=x^2} is above the x-axis from {m:x=1} to {m:x=3}, so the area equals the integral.
  3. The area is {m:int(1, 3) x^2 dx}. Section 2 shows how to find its value.

2. Finding the value: upper minus lower

Integrating without limits gives a function plus c. To use limits, take any {term:antiderivative} {m:F(x)} of the function, put the upper limit into it, put the lower limit into it, and subtract.

Key idea: the {term:fundamental-theorem-of-calculus|fundamental theorem of calculus}. If {m:F(x)} has derivative {m:f(x)}, and {m:f} is unbroken from a to b, then {m:int(a, b) f(x) dx=F(b)-F(a)}. Upper limit first, then lower limit, and subtract. Leave out {m:+c}: it cancels, as the next paragraph shows.

Some books write the working with square brackets, like {m:[frac(x^3, 3)]_1^3}. The limits go to the right of the bracket. This page writes {m:F(b)-F(a)} out in full.

Worked example

Find {m:int(1, 3) x^2 dx}. This is the area under the curve in section 1.

  1. Integrate without the constant: {m:F(x)=frac(x^3, 3)}.
  2. Put in the upper limit: {m:F(3)=frac(27, 3)=9}.
  3. Put in the lower limit: {m:F(1)=frac(1, 3)}.
  4. Subtract: {m:F(3)-F(1)=9-frac(1, 3)=frac(26, 3)}.
  5. So {m:int(1, 3) x^2 dx=frac(26, 3)}. The curve is above the axis, so the area of the region is {m:frac(26, 3)} square units.

Why the constant cancels

Two antiderivatives of {m:2x} are {m:x^2} and {m:x^2+2}. They have the same shape, one higher than the other. Between {m:x=1} and {m:x=3}, each rises by the same amount.

In symbols, with a constant c: {m:(F(b)+c)-(F(a)+c)=F(b)-F(a)}. The c is added once and taken away once. Any antiderivative gives the same answer.

Why the rule works

Let {m:A(x)} be the area under the curve from the fixed value {m:x=a} to a variable x. Add a thin strip of width h to the right. The strip is almost a rectangle of height {m:f(x)}, so its area is about {m:f(x)*h}.

So {m:A(x+h)-A(x)} is about {m:f(x)*h}. Divide by h: the rate at which the area grows is {m:f(x)}. In other words, the derivative of {m:A(x)} is {m:f(x)}. Then {m:A(x)} is an antiderivative of {m:f(x)}, so {m:A(x)=F(x)+c}. At {m:x=a} no area has been collected yet, so {m:A(a)=0} and {m:c=-F(a)}. The area up to {m:x=b} is {m:F(b)-F(a)}.

The smaller h is, the closer {m:frac(A(x+h)-A(x), h)} gets to {m:f(x)}. Its limit as h tends to 0 is {m:f(x)}. A full proof needs f to be continuous, and more care with the limit.

3. Area under a curve above the axis

Sometimes a question names the region by the curve and the x-axis alone, as in "the area enclosed between {m:y=4x-x^2} and the x-axis". Then the limits are the x-values where the curve meets the x-axis. Find them first by putting {m:y=0}.

Key idea: to find the {term:area-under-curve|area under a curve}, first check that the curve is on or above the x-axis between the limits. If it is, the area is the definite integral. When a question asks for the area enclosed between a curve and the x-axis, the limits are where the curve crosses the axis. If the curve is below the axis anywhere between the limits, this method needs the changes in sections 4 and 5.

Check which side of the axis the curve is on by testing one x-value between the limits. For {m:y=4x-x^2}, put {m:x=2}: the height is {m:8-4=4}, which is positive. So the curve is above the axis between 0 and 4.

Worked example

4. Below the axis: negative integrals

What if the curve is below the x-axis? The region has a real area, but the integral behaves differently.

Work out {m:int(0, 2) (x^2-4) dx}. The antiderivative is {m:F(x)=frac(x^3, 3)-4x}. Then {m:F(2)=frac(8, 3)-8=-frac(16, 3)} and {m:F(0)=0}. The integral is {m:-frac(16, 3)}: a negative number.

An area cannot be negative. The integral is negative because the curve is below the axis, where the heights {m:f(x)} are negative.

Key idea: if the curve is on or below the x-axis from a to b, so {m:f(x) <= 0}, then {m:int(a, b) f(x) dx} is zero or negative, and the area of the region between the curve and the x-axis is {m:-int(a, b) f(x) dx}. Swapping the limits changes the sign: {m:int(b, a) f(x) dx=-int(a, b) f(x) dx}. The integral is the signed area. The {term:area-below-the-axis|area below the axis} counts as negative.

The area is the integral with its minus sign removed: {m:frac(16, 3)} square units. The definite integral is a number that can be negative. The area is the size of the region.

Worked example

Find the area between the curve {m:y=x^2-4} and the x-axis, from {m:x=0} to {m:x=2}.

  1. Test {m:x=1}: {m:y=1-4=-3}. The curve is below the axis between 0 and 2.
  2. Integrate: {m:F(x)=frac(x^3, 3)-4x}. {m:F(2)=-frac(16, 3)} and {m:F(0)=0}.
  3. The integral is {m:-frac(16, 3)}. The area is its size, {m:frac(16, 3)}, without the minus sign.

6. Between a line and a curve

A line and a curve can enclose a region. Here the line {m:y=x+2} cuts the curve {m:y=x^2}. The region between them is shaded.

First find where they meet. At a meeting point both equations give the same y, so put the two expressions equal: {m:x^2=x+2}, which gives {m:x^2-x-2=0}, so {m:(x-2)(x+1)=0}. The meeting points are at {m:x=-1} and {m:x=2}. Those are the limits.

Then decide which is above. Test an x-value between the limits. At {m:x=0}: the line has height 2 and the curve has height 0, so the line is above.

Key idea: suppose {m:y=g(x)} is above {m:y=f(x)} for every x from a to b, with both unbroken. Then the area of the region between them is {m:int(a, b) (g(x)-f(x)) dx}: the upper function minus the lower function. This holds wherever the region lies, above or below the x-axis. If the line and curve are both above the axis, it is also the area under the line minus the area under the curve. If you subtract in the wrong order you get the negative of the area.

Why it works

  • The area function grows at the height of the curve. A thin strip added to the right of the region has area about height times width. So the rate of growth of the area is the height {m:f(x)}.
  • That makes the area function an antiderivative. Integrating undoes differentiating, so {m:F(b)-F(a)} gives the area collected between a and b.
  • The constant cancels. The same c is in both values, so it drops out when you subtract.
  • A curve below the axis has negative height. The integral adds up heights, so the integral over a part below the axis is negative. The area is its size.
  • A line and a curve work by subtraction. The height of the region at each x is the upper function minus the lower function. Integrating that difference from a to b gives the area.

Common traps

  • Putting in only the upper limit and forgetting to subtract {m:F(a)}.
  • Adding {m:F(b)} and {m:F(a)} instead of subtracting.
  • Writing {m:+c} in a definite integral.
  • Using y-values as limits. Limits are x-values.
  • Calling a negative integral an area, or writing a negative area.
  • Integrating across a point where the curve crosses the axis, and calling the result the area.
  • Splitting the integral at a root outside the limits.
  • Swapping the limits without changing the sign.
  • Subtracting a line and a curve in the wrong order, or using only the area under the line.
  • Forgetting to divide by the new power, or squaring each term of a bracket instead of expanding it.
