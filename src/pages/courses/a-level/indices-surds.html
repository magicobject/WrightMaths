Indices and surds

A power tells you how many times to multiply. A root undoes a power. This page shows that every root is also a power, and teaches you to keep answers exact, with square root signs left in.

Where you will use this: solving quadratics with exact answers (Quadratics), expanding brackets with powers (Binomial expansion), working with exponentials and logarithms, and differentiating roots such as {m:sqrt(x)} (Differentiation).

1. Laws of indices

An {term:index} (also called a power) is the small raised number in {m:x^3}. For a whole number, it says how many copies of the base x to multiply together. So {m:x^3=x*x*x}. The plural of index is indices.

Three rules, the {term:laws-of-indices|laws of indices}, work on powers that have the same base.

Key idea:

  • To multiply, add the indices: {m:a^m*a^n=a^(m+n)}.
  • To divide, subtract the indices: {m:frac(a^m, a^n)=a^(m-n)}.
  • To raise a power to a power, multiply the indices: {m:(a^m)^n=a^(m*n)}.

These rules only work when the base is the same. You cannot combine {m:2^3*3^2} by adding indices.

When a power has a number in front, deal with the numbers and the powers separately. Multiply or divide the numbers. Then add or subtract the indices.

Worked example

Simplify {m:3x^2*4x^5}.

  1. Multiply the numbers: {m:3*4=12}.
  2. Add the indices of x: {m:2+5=7}.
  3. So {m:3x^2*4x^5=12x^7}.

Worked example: dividing

Simplify {m:frac(10x^6, 4x^2)}.

  1. Divide the numbers: {m:frac(10, 4)=frac(5, 2)}.
  2. Subtract the indices of x: {m:6-2=4}.
  3. So {m:frac(10x^6, 4x^2)=frac(5x^4, 2)}.

Worked example: a power of a bracket

Simplify {m:(2x^3)^4}.

  1. The index 4 applies to everything inside the bracket, the 2 as well as the {m:x^3}.
  2. {m:2^4=16}, and {m:(x^3)^4=x^12}, because {m:3*4=12}.
  3. So {m:(2x^3)^4=16x^12}.

2. Fractional indices

What could {m:x^(frac(1, 2))} mean? Use the first law. If you multiply it by itself, you add the indices: {m:x^(frac(1, 2))*x^(frac(1, 2))=x^1=x}. A number that multiplies by itself to give x is the square root of x. So {m:x^(frac(1, 2))=sqrt(x)}.

The same idea works for other roots. A {term:fractional-index|fractional index} has a root on the bottom of the fraction and a power on the top. We write {m:root(3, x)} for the cube root of x, and {m:root(n, x)} for the nth root.

Key idea: {m:a^(frac(1, n))=root(n, a)} and {m:a^(frac(m, n))=(root(n, a))^m}. The bottom of the fraction is the root. The top is the power. Take the root first, then the power. The numbers stay small.

A negative fractional index combines both ideas. The negative sign means “one over”. The fraction means “root and power”.

Even roots of negative numbers are not real numbers. So {m:x^(frac(1, 2))} only has a value when {m:x >= 0}. Odd roots are fine: the cube root of {m:-8} is {m:-2}.

Worked example

Work out {m:49^(frac(1, 2))} and {m:8^(frac(1, 3))}.

  1. {m:49^(frac(1, 2))=sqrt(49)=7}.
  2. {m:8^(frac(1, 3))=root(3, 8)=2}, because {m:2*2*2=8}.

Worked example: a power on top

Work out {m:27^(frac(2, 3))}.

  1. The bottom is 3, so take the cube root: {m:root(3, 27)=3}.
  2. The top is 2, so square it: {m:3^2=9}.
  3. So {m:27^(frac(2, 3))=9}. Taking the root first kept the numbers small. The other order gives {m:27^2=729} and then a cube root, which is much harder.

Worked example: a negative index

Work out {m:4^(-frac(3, 2))}, and then {m:(frac(8, 27))^(-frac(2, 3))}.

  1. The negative sign means one over: {m:4^(-frac(3, 2))=frac(1, 4^(frac(3, 2)))}.
  2. {m:4^(frac(3, 2))=(sqrt(4))^3=2^3=8}. So {m:4^(-frac(3, 2))=frac(1, 8)}.
  3. For the fraction, flip it first: {m:(frac(8, 27))^(-frac(2, 3))=(frac(27, 8))^(frac(2, 3))}.
  4. The cube root of {m:frac(27, 8)} is {m:frac(3, 2)}. Square it: {m:(frac(3, 2))^2=frac(9, 4)}.

3. Surds

A {term:rational-number|rational number} can be written as one whole number divided by another, such as {m:frac(3, 4)}. An {term:irrational-number|irrational number} cannot. Its decimal never stops and never repeats. Many square roots are irrational. By Pythagoras, the diagonal of a square with side 1 is {m:sqrt(2)}.

A {term:surd} is a root that is an irrational number, such as {m:sqrt(2)} or {m:root(3, 5)}. We keep it as a root so that the answer is exact. A calculator decimal such as 1.414 is only a rounded copy. The number {m:sqrt(9)=3} is rational, so it is not a surd.

Two rules help you work with square roots. Both need a and b to be positive.

Key idea: {m:sqrt(a)*sqrt(b)=sqrt(a*b)} and {m:frac(sqrt(a), sqrt(b))=sqrt(frac(a, b))}. Also {m:sqrt(a)*sqrt(a)=a}.

Simplifying a surd

To simplify a square root, find the largest square number that divides the number under the root sign. Split the root into two roots, one of which is a whole number.

Worked example

Write {m:sqrt(50)} and {m:sqrt(72)} in the form {m:a*sqrt(b)}, where b is as small as possible.

  1. {m:50=25*2}, and 25 is the largest square number that divides 50. So {m:sqrt(50)=sqrt(25)*sqrt(2)=5sqrt(2)}.
  2. {m:72=36*2}. So {m:sqrt(72)=sqrt(36)*sqrt(2)=6sqrt(2)}.
  3. If you chose {m:72=4*18}, you get {m:2sqrt(18)}. That is not finished, because 18 still contains a square number: {m:2sqrt(18)=2*3sqrt(2)=6sqrt(2)}.

Adding and subtracting surds

You can add surds only when they are {term:like-surds|like surds}: the same root part, after each has been simplified. It works like adding like terms in algebra, {m:2x+5x=7x}. So {m:2sqrt(3)+5sqrt(3)=7sqrt(3)}. The surds {m:sqrt(2)} and {m:sqrt(3)} are not alike, so {m:sqrt(2)+sqrt(3)} cannot be written as one surd. It is not {m:sqrt(5)}.

Worked example

Simplify {m:sqrt(50)+sqrt(8)-sqrt(18)}.

  1. Simplify each surd: {m:sqrt(50)=5sqrt(2)}, {m:sqrt(8)=2sqrt(2)} and {m:sqrt(18)=3sqrt(2)}.
  2. They are now like surds. Add and subtract the numbers in front: {m:5+2-3=4}.
  3. So {m:sqrt(50)+sqrt(8)-sqrt(18)=4sqrt(2)}.

Now you finish it

  2. {m:12=4*3}, so {m:sqrt(12)=}___{m:sqrt(3)}.

Multiplying surds and expanding brackets

Expand brackets with surds in the same way as in algebra: multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second. Remember that {m:sqrt(a)*sqrt(a)=a}. Then collect like surds.

Worked example

Expand and simplify {m:(2+sqrt(3))(4-sqrt(3))}.

  1. Multiply out: {m:2*4=8}, {m:2*(-sqrt(3))=-2sqrt(3)}, {m:sqrt(3)*4=4sqrt(3)} and {m:sqrt(3)*(-sqrt(3))=-3}.
  2. Collect: the numbers give {m:8-3=5}. The surds give {m:-2sqrt(3)+4sqrt(3)=2sqrt(3)}.
  3. So {m:(2+sqrt(3))(4-sqrt(3))=5+2sqrt(3)}.

Worked example: squaring a bracket

Expand {m:(3+sqrt(5))^2}.

  1. {m:(3+sqrt(5))^2=(3+sqrt(5))(3+sqrt(5))}.
  2. The pieces are {m:9}, {m:3sqrt(5)}, {m:3sqrt(5)} and {m:sqrt(5)*sqrt(5)=5}.
  3. So {m:(3+sqrt(5))^2=9+6sqrt(5)+5}.
  4. Collect the numbers: {m:9+5=14}. So the answer is {m:14+6sqrt(5)}.

A bracket squared always has a middle term. Here it is {m:2*3*sqrt(5)=6sqrt(5)}.

Worked example: a product with no surd

Expand {m:(5+sqrt(3))(5-sqrt(3))}.

  1. The pieces are {m:25}, {m:-5sqrt(3)}, {m:5sqrt(3)} and {m:-3}.
  2. The two surd pieces cancel. So the result is {m:25-3=22}.

This is a difference of two squares (one square minus another square): {m:(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2}. Here the product has no surd. You will use this in the next section.

Algebraic surds

A surd can contain a letter. In this book, a letter under a square root stands for a number that is positive or zero, so that the square root is defined. The same rules hold. Notice that {m:sqrt(x)*sqrt(x)=x}, and {m:sqrt(a^2)=a} when a is positive or zero.

Worked example

Take a to be positive. Simplify {m:sqrt(18a^2)}. Then expand {m:(sqrt(x)+2)(sqrt(x)-2)}, where {m:x >= 0}.

  1. {m:18a^2=9a^2*2}, and {m:9a^2} is a square: its square root is {m:3a}. So {m:sqrt(18a^2)=3a sqrt(2)}.
  2. For the brackets, the pieces are {m:x}, {m:-2sqrt(x)}, {m:2sqrt(x)} and {m:-4}.
  3. The middle pieces cancel. So {m:(sqrt(x)+2)(sqrt(x)-2)=x-4}.

4. Rationalising denominators

A fraction such as {m:frac(1, sqrt(3))} has a surd on the bottom. The convention is to remove it. To {term:rationalise-denominator|rationalise the denominator} means to rewrite the fraction so that the bottom is a whole number. This does not change the value. It gives every answer one standard form, so you can compare answers and add them without extra work.

The method is to multiply the top and the bottom by the same expression. That is multiplying by 1, so the value stays the same.

One surd on the bottom

Multiply the top and the bottom by that surd. Then {m:sqrt(a)*sqrt(a)=a} makes the bottom a whole number.

Worked example

  3. Divide the top and the bottom by 2: {m:frac(6sqrt(2), 2)=3sqrt(2)}.

A number plus or minus a surd on the bottom

Why does this work? The product of an expression and its conjugate is a difference of two squares: {m:(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2}. The square of a surd is a whole number, so the surd disappears.

6. Exact answers in problems

In many problems a length or an area is a surd. Keep it exact unless the question asks for a decimal. Say what you are finding, show the working, and simplify at the end. Pythagoras and area formulas often turn a surd times itself into a whole number.

Worked example: a rectangle

Why it works

  • Adding indices. {m:a^m} is m copies of a multiplied together. Multiplying by {m:a^n} adds n more copies. So the indices add.
  • Zero and negative indices. Keep the first law true. Then {m:a^0*a^n=a^n}, so {m:a^0=1}. And {m:a^n*a^(-n)=a^0=1}, so {m:a^(-n)} must be {m:frac(1, a^n)}.
  • Fractional indices. Keep the first law true again. Then {m:a^(frac(1, 2))*a^(frac(1, 2))=a^1}, so {m:a^(frac(1, 2))} is a number that multiplies by itself to give a. That is the square root.
  • Surds are exact. A calculator gives a rounded copy of {m:sqrt(2)}. Rounding errors grow through a long calculation. A surd has none, so it is the exact answer.
  • Conjugates. {m:(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2}: the two middle terms are {m:-ab} and {m:+ab}, which cancel. If b is a square root, {m:b^2} is a whole number.
  • Rationalising. Multiplying the top and the bottom by the same number is multiplying by 1. So the value never changes, only its form.
  • Rejecting solutions. A square root is never negative. So a value of u that would need {m:sqrt(x)} to be negative gives no solution.

Common traps

  • Adding indices when the bases are different, such as {m:2^3*3^2}.
  • Treating {m:(2x)^3} as {m:2x^3}. The index applies to everything in the bracket: {m:(2x)^3=8x^3}.
  • Thinking a negative index gives a negative number. {m:2^(-3)=frac(1, 8)}.
  • Reading {m:frac(2, 3)} as a multiplier, not as a cube root followed by a square.
  • Adding or subtracting numbers under roots: {m:sqrt(2)+sqrt(3)} is not {m:sqrt(5)}.
  • Leaving a surd half-simplified, such as {m:2sqrt(18)} instead of {m:6sqrt(2)}.
  • Missing the middle term of a squared bracket: {m:(2+sqrt(5))^2} is not {m:4+5}.
  • Using the same bracket, not the conjugate, to rationalise {m:frac(1, 2+sqrt(3))}.
  • Forgetting a negative solution, such as {m:x=-8} in {m:x^(frac(2, 3))=4}.
  • Keeping a value of u that would make a square root negative. Always check in the original equation.
