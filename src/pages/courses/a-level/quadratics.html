Quadratics

A quadratic has an x squared in it. This page gives you three ways to solve one, a quick test for how many roots it has, and a way to sketch its graph (draw its shape quickly).

Where you will use this: finding the lowest or highest point of a curve. It also helps you find the centre of a circle (Circles) and factorise bigger polynomials (Polynomials). It helps with any problem where an area or a path length is squared.

1. Solving by factorising

A {term:quadratic} is an expression where the highest power of x is 2. An expression is numbers and letters with no equals sign, such as {m:3x+1}. An equation has an equals sign. The general form of a quadratic is {m:ax^2+bx+c}, where a, b and c are numbers and a is not 0.

A value of x that makes the quadratic equal to 0 is a {term:root}. To solve {m:ax^2+bx+c=0} means to find all of its roots. On a graph, the roots are the x-values where the curve meets the x-axis.

To factorise an expression is to write it as brackets multiplied together. Factorising gives the roots because of one fact about zero.

Key idea: if two numbers multiply to give 0, then at least one of them is 0. So {m:(x-p)(x-q)=0} means {m:x=p} or {m:x=q}.

Follow these steps.

  1. Move every term to one side, so that the other side is 0.
  2. Factorise the quadratic.
  3. Set each bracket equal to 0 and solve it.
  4. Check each root in the original equation.

Worked example

Solve {m:x^2-x-6=0}.

  1. One side is already 0.
  2. Find two numbers that multiply to {m:-6} and add to {m:-1}. They are {m:-3} and {m:2}. So {m:(x-3)(x+2)=0}.
  3. Either {m:x-3=0} or {m:x+2=0}. So {m:x=3} or {m:x=-2}.
  4. Check: {m:3^2-3-6=0} and {m:(-2)^2-(-2)-6=0}. Both are true.

The curve in the figure meets the x-axis at exactly these two values.

2. Completing the square

{term:completing-the-square|Completing the square} writes a quadratic in the form {m:(x+p)^2+q}, where p and q are numbers. We read {m:(x+p)^2} as “x plus p, all squared”. The picture below shows why this works.

Start with {m:x^2+6x}. The {m:x^2} term is a square of side x. The {m:6x} term is two strips, each x long and 3 wide.

The big square has area {m:(x+3)^2}. That area is {m:x^2+6x} plus the corner, which has area 9. So {m:x^2+6x=(x+3)^2-9}. The {m:-9} takes away the corner that we added.

Key idea: halve the number in front of x. Write x plus that half in a bracket, and square the bracket. Then subtract the square of the half, so that the value does not change: {m:x^2+bx=(x+frac(b,2))^2-(frac(b,2))^2}.

Worked example

Write {m:x^2+6x+5} in the form {m:(x+p)^2+q}.

  1. Half of 6 is 3. So {m:x^2+6x=(x+3)^2-9}.
  2. Add the 5: {m:x^2+6x+5=(x+3)^2-9+5=(x+3)^2-4}.
  3. Check by expanding: {m:(x+3)^2-4=x^2+6x+9-4=x^2+6x+5}.

Worked example: a negative number in front of x

Write {m:x^2-8x+3} in the form {m:(x+p)^2+q}.

  1. Half of {m:-8} is {m:-4}. So {m:x^2-8x=(x-4)^2-16}.
  2. Add the 3: {m:x^2-8x+3=(x-4)^2-16+3=(x-4)^2-13}.
  3. Check: {m:(x-4)^2-13=x^2-8x+16-13=x^2-8x+3}.

What completing the square tells you

The graph of a quadratic is a {term:parabola}. Its {term:vertex} is its turning point: the point where the curve stops going down and starts going up, or the other way round. The vertical line through the vertex is the {term:axis-of-symmetry}. This line splits the parabola into two mirror-image halves.

The square of any real number is never negative. So {m:(x+p)^2} is 0 when {m:x=-p}, and bigger than 0 everywhere else. So the lowest point of {m:y=(x+p)^2+q} is at {m:x=-p}, where {m:y=q}.

Key idea: the graph of {m:y=(x+p)^2+q} has its vertex at {m:(-p, q)}, and its axis of symmetry is the line {m:x=-p}. The x-coordinate of the vertex is {m:-p}, the opposite sign to the number in the bracket, because {m:(x+3)} is 0 when {m:x=-3}.

Worked example

Find the least value of {m:y=x^2+6x+5}, and the value of x where it happens.

  1. Complete the square: {m:y=(x+3)^2-4}.
  2. {m:(x+3)^2} is never negative, and it is 0 when {m:x=-3}.
  3. So the least value of y is {m:-4}, and it happens at {m:x=-3}. The vertex is {m:(-3, -4)}.

3. Completing the square when the number in front of x squared is not 1

When a is not 1, write a outside a bracket that holds the {m:x^2} term and the x term. Then complete the square inside that bracket.

Key idea: to complete the square when the number a in front of {m:x^2} is not 1, take a out of the first two terms: {m:ax^2+bx+c=a(x^2+frac(b, a)x)+c}. Complete the square inside the bracket, then multiply the number you subtracted by a. The result is {m:a(x+p)^2+q}, where {m:p=frac(b, 2a)} and {m:q=c-frac(b^2, 4a)}. The vertex is {m:(-p, q)}. This needs {m:a != 0}.

Worked example

Write {m:2x^2+8x+3} in the form {m:a(x+p)^2+q}.

  1. Write 2 outside a bracket round the first two terms: {m:2(x^2+4x)+3}.
  2. Complete the square inside the bracket: {m:x^2+4x=(x+2)^2-4}. So the expression is {m:2[(x+2)^2-4]+3}.
  3. Multiply the {m:-4} by 2: {m:2(x+2)^2-8+3=2(x+2)^2-5}.
  4. Check: {m:2(x^2+4x+4)-5=2x^2+8x+8-5=2x^2+8x+3}. The vertex is {m:(-2, -5)}.

In the quadratic formula, the part under the square root is {m:b^2-4ac}. This part is called the {term:discriminant}. The discriminant tells you how many roots the equation has, before you solve it.

7. Quadratics in disguise

Some equations are not quadratics in x, but they are quadratics in something else. They are {term:quadratic-in-disguise|quadratics in disguise}. For example, {m:x^4} is the square of {m:x^2}, and {m:x} is the square of {m:x^(frac(1,2))}. A {term:fractional-index} such as {m:frac(1,2)} means taking a root of a number: {m:x^(frac(1,2))=sqrt(x)}, the square root of x.

Follow these steps.

  1. Choose a new letter, u, for the smaller power. Choosing a new letter like this is called a substitution.
  2. Rewrite the equation as a quadratic in u.
  3. Solve for u.
  4. Go back to x. For each value of u, solve for x.
  5. Check each x in the original equation.

Key idea: if an equation has a power of x and also the square of that power, and no other power of x, let u stand for the smaller power. The equation is then a quadratic in u. Why it works

  • The zero idea. Zero times any number is 0. A product of two numbers is 0 only when one of them is 0. So we first rearrange so that one side is 0.
  • Completing the square. {m:(x+p)^2} is the area of a square of side {m:x+p}. Its pieces are {m:x^2}, two strips of {m:px} and a corner of {m:p^2}. Completing the square adds the missing corner, and then takes it away again.
  • The vertex. In {m:(x+p)^2+q} the bracket is never negative. So the lowest value is q, when the bracket is 0. That is why the vertex is {m:(-p, q)}.
  • The formula. The quadratic formula is what you get when you complete the square once for the general quadratic {m:ax^2+bx+c}. The letters a, b and c stand for any numbers, so it works for all of them.
  • The discriminant. It is the number under the square root in the formula. The square root of a negative number is not real. The square root of 0 gives one value. The square root of a positive number gives two values, one positive and one negative.
  • Quadratics in disguise. The equation really is a quadratic in u, so every method for quadratics works on it. The only extra step is going back from u to x.

Common traps

  • Using the number in the bracket as the root. The bracket {m:(x-3)} gives {m:x=3}.
  • Using the zero idea when the other side is not 0.
  • Dividing both sides by x, and losing the root {m:x=0}.
  • Giving one root when there are two. Write {m:x=3} or {m:x=-3}.
  • Reading the vertex of {m:(x+3)^2-4} as {m:(3, -4)}. It is {m:(-3, -4)}.
  • In completing the square with a not 1, forgetting to multiply the number you subtract inside the bracket by a.
  • In the formula, forgetting brackets round a negative b, or forgetting to divide the whole top line by {m:2a}.
  • Stopping at the values of u in a quadratic in disguise, or giving only the positive roots.
