Integration as reverse differentiation

Differentiating gives the gradient of a curve. Integrating goes the other way: from the gradient back to the curve. This page shows how to do it, and why every integral without limits ends in + c.

Where you will use this: finding a curve when you know its gradient, finding an area under a curve (the next topic), and working back from a rate of change to the quantity.

1. Going backwards: what was differentiated?

To {term:differentiate} a function is to find its {term:derivative}. The derivative gives the gradient of the curve at every value of x.

Now go the other way. You are given the derivative, and you want the function it came from. To {term:integrate} is to do exactly that. Integrating reverses differentiating.

Read the two graphs together. The height of the lower graph is the gradient of the upper graph. Integrating starts from the lower graph and asks: which curve has these gradients?

Key idea: to integrate a function {m:f(x)} is to find a function whose derivative is {m:f(x)}. Any such function is an {term:antiderivative} of {m:f(x)}. The result of integrating is called an {term:integral}.

So there is one question to ask each time: what did I differentiate to get this function? Use what you know about differentiating powers. Differentiating brings the power down in front and takes 1 off the power. Integrating must undo that.

Worked example

Find a function whose derivative is {m:4x^3}.

  1. Differentiating takes 1 off the power. So the power in the answer is one more than 3. Try {m:x^4}.
  2. Differentiate {m:x^4}: the 4 comes down in front and the power drops to 3. This gives {m:4x^3}.
  3. That is the derivative we were given. So {m:x^4} is a function whose derivative is {m:4x^3}.

2. Why every integral ends in + c

Differentiate {m:x^2}. You get {m:2x}. Now differentiate {m:x^2+3} and {m:x^2-5}. You get {m:2x} again each time.

Adding a number moves a curve up or down. It does not change how steep the curve is at any value of x. So many functions have the same derivative. The picture shows three of them.

Together, these curves are a family: one curve for each value of c. Every function with derivative {m:2x} has the form {m:x^2+c}, where c is a number. We write all of them at once as {m:x^2+c}.

Key idea: the letter {m:c} is the {term:constant-of-integration|constant of integration}. It stands for an unknown number: any real number. Every integral on this page has no limits, so always write {m:+c}.

Why can you not find c from the derivative alone? Differentiating turns every constant into 0, so the derivative shows nothing about it. Integrating cannot tell which constant was there. Section 7 shows how a point on the curve gives c.

  4. So the functions with derivative {m:3x^2} are {m:x^3+c}, for any number c.

3. The integral sign, and the rule for powers of x

Integrating has its own notation. {m:int() 2x dx} is read "the integral of 2x with respect to x". The sign {m:int()} is a stretched letter S. The integrals on this page have no numbers on the sign. Numbers there are called limits. They come in the next topic.

The function between the sign and the dx is the {term:integrand}. The dx says which letter is the variable: this is what {term:with-respect-to|with respect to x} means. Always write the dx.

{md:int() 2x dx=x^2+c}

Read this as: the integral of 2x with respect to x equals x squared plus c.

Key idea: to integrate a power of x, add 1 to the power, then divide by the new power. Then add c.

{md:int() x^n dx=frac(x^(n+1), n+1)+c}

Here n is the power: {m:x^n} is read "x to the power n". This works for every number n except {m:n=-1}.

Why the rule works

Differentiate {m:frac(x^(n+1), n+1)}. The power {m:n+1} comes down in front, and the power drops to n. The result is {m:frac((n+1)x^n, n+1)=x^n}. So the rule undoes the {term:power-rule|power rule}.

Why n = −1 is excluded

If {m:n=-1}, then {m:n+1=0}. The rule would divide by 0, which is not possible. The integrand is then {m:x^(-1)=frac(1, x)}. No power of x has {m:frac(1, x)} as its derivative. You meet the answer in Year 2.

Every other power works, including negative powers and fractional powers. Write roots and reciprocals as powers first.

Worked example

Find {m:int() x^5 dx}.

  1. The power is 5. Add 1: the new power is 6.
  2. Divide by the new power: {m:frac(x^6, 6)}.
  3. Add c: {m:int() x^5 dx=frac(x^6, 6)+c}.
  4. Check by differentiating: {m:frac(x^6, 6)} gives {m:frac(6x^5, 6)=x^5}. ✓

4. Numbers, multiples and sums

Three facts let you integrate longer expressions.

  1. A number in front stays in front. {m:int() 6x^2 dx}: integrate {m:x^2} to get {m:frac(x^3, 3)}, then multiply by 6. The answer is {m:2x^3+c}.
  2. A sum is integrated one term at a time. Integrate each term, then add the results.
  3. A number on its own gets an x. {m:int() 5 dx=5x+c}, because the derivative of {m:5x} is 5. This is the rule with {m:5=5x^0}.

Write one c at the end of the whole answer, not one c for each term. Each term could have its own constant, but a sum of unknown constants is one unknown constant.

Key idea: for {m:n != -1}, {m:int() k x^n dx=frac(k x^(n+1), n+1)+c}. A number in front stays in front. A sum is integrated one term at a time. A number on its own gets an x: {m:int() k dx=k x+c}. Write one c at the end of the whole answer.

8. When the inside is linear Extra

This is an extra method. In the syllabus plan it belongs to Year 2, where it is called the reverse chain rule, and some courses introduce it earlier. Check your specification. If your course does not need it, skip to "Flip it".

A linear expression has the form {m:ax+b}, where a and b are numbers, such as {m:2x+1}. To integrate a power of {m:(ax+b)}, treat the bracket like x. Then also divide by a, the number in front of x. The expression in the bracket is called the inside.

Key idea:

{md:int() (ax+b)^n dx=frac((ax+b)^(n+1), a(n+1))+c}

This works for every n except {m:n=-1}, and for {m:a != 0}. It works only when the expression in the bracket is linear.

Why you divide by a

Now use the rule with {m:a=2} and {m:n=2}. It gives {m:frac((2x+1)^3, 6)+c}. Expand the top: {m:frac(8x^3+12x^2+6x+1, 6)=frac(4x^3, 3)+2x^2+x+frac(1, 6)}. This matches the expanded method. The two answers differ only by a constant, and c can be any number, so they agree. The 6 on the bottom is {m:a(n+1)=2*3}.

Differentiating {m:frac((2x+1)^3, 6)} needs the chain rule, which comes in Year 2. For now, check by expanding, as above.

For brackets like {m:(x+2)^2}, where {m:a=1}, the rule gives {m:frac((x+2)^3, 3)+c}. The two answers differ only by a constant, so they agree.

Why it works

  • Integrating undoes differentiating. The derivative of an integral is the integrand. That is the reason you can check any answer by differentiating it.
  • The rule is the power rule backwards. Differentiating brings the power down and takes 1 off. Integrating adds 1 to the power and divides by the new power, which cancels the number that came down.
  • The constant appears because differentiating loses it. A number added to a function moves its graph up or down, and does not change the gradient. So a whole family of curves has the same derivative.
  • One point fixes the constant. Only one curve in the family passes through a given point.
  • The power {m:-1} is different. The rule would divide by 0. A different function has {m:frac(1, x)} as its derivative. You meet it in Year 2.

Common traps

  • Forgetting {m:+c} in an integral with no limits.
  • Adding 1 to the power but not dividing by the new power.
  • Dividing by the old power instead of the new power.
  • Differentiating when you should integrate, by bringing the power down.
  • Leaving a number on its own without an x: {m:int() 5 dx} is {m:5x+c}.
  • Writing a root or a reciprocal without rewriting it as a power first.
  • Losing a sign with negative powers: {m:int() x^(-2) dx} is {m:-x^(-1)+c}.
  • Dividing by {m:frac(3, 2)} as if it were {m:frac(2, 3)}. Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal.
  • Squaring each term of a bracket: {m:(x+1)^2} is not {m:x^2+1}.
  • Using the y-value of a point as c. Put the point into the equation and solve for c.
