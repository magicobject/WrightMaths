Trigonometric identities and equations

An identity is a fact about sine, cosine and tangent that is true for every angle. You meet two of them: {m:tan theta=frac(sin theta, cos theta)} and {m:sin^2 theta+cos^2 theta=1}. Then you use them to solve equations. An equation such as {m:sin x=0.5} is true for only some angles. You find every such angle in a stated interval, even when the angle is {m:2x} or {m:x+30deg}, and even when the equation is a quadratic in {m:sin x}. All angles on this page are in degrees.

Where you will use this: in Reciprocal and inverse trigonometric functions, and in Addition formulae, double angles and the R-form (both Year 2), where you prove identities and solve harder equations by the same steps. It is also needed in Parametric equations (Year 2).

1. The two identities

An {term:trigonometric-identity|identity} is true for every angle where both sides have a value. An {term:trigonometric-equation|equation} is true only for some angles. For example, {m:sin x=0.5} is an equation: it is true at 30° and 150° and false at 40°. You solve an equation. You do not solve an identity, because every angle already works.

Look again at the {term:unit-circle|unit circle}, the circle of radius 1 with its centre at the origin. The point P at angle {m:theta} is {m:(cos theta, sin theta)}. Drop a line from P straight down to the x-axis. This makes a right-angled triangle. Its longest side is the radius, which is 1. Its other sides are {m:cos theta} along the x-axis and {m:sin theta} upright.

Pythagoras says that the two short sides squared add up to the longest side squared. Here that is {m:cos^2 theta+sin^2 theta=1^2}. The notation {m:sin^2 theta} means {m:(sin theta)^2}: find the sine, then square it.

The tangent is the gradient of the line from the origin to P. The rise is {m:sin theta} and the run is {m:cos theta}. So the gradient is {m:frac(sin theta, cos theta)}.

Key idea: two identities.

  • {m:sin^2 theta+cos^2 theta=1}. It is true for every angle {m:theta}.
  • {m:tan theta=frac(sin theta, cos theta)}. It is true for every angle where {m:cos theta} is not 0. At 90° and 270°, and every 180° from them, {m:cos theta=0}, so the tangent has no value.
  • The first comes from Pythagoras on the unit circle. The second comes from the gradient of the line OP.

The first identity is true for angles of any size: acute, obtuse, negative or above 360°. Every point of the unit circle satisfies {m:x^2+y^2=1}, and {m:cos theta} and {m:sin theta} are its coordinates.

2. Finding the other values

Sometimes you know one of {m:sin theta}, {m:cos theta} and {m:tan theta}, and the {term:quadrant|quadrant} of the angle. You can then find the other two exactly. The first identity gives the square of the missing value. The quadrant gives its sign.

Key idea: to find the other values:

  1. Use {m:sin^2 theta+cos^2 theta=1} to find the square of the missing value. For example, {m:cos^2 theta=1-sin^2 theta}.
  2. Take the square root. There are two roots, one positive and one negative.
  3. Use the quadrant, with the {term:cast-diagram|CAST diagram}, to choose the sign.
  4. Find the tangent from {m:tan theta=frac(sin theta, cos theta)}.

3. Every solution in an interval

A {term:trigonometric-equation|trigonometric equation} asks for the angles that make it true. The question names an {term:interval|interval}, such as {m:0deg<=x<=360deg}, and you must give every solution inside it. The calculator gives only one angle, the {term:principal-value|principal value}. You find the others from the symmetry and the period of the graph.

The principal value is always in a fixed range. {m:sin^(-1)} gives angles from {m:-90deg} to 90°. {m:cos^(-1)} gives 0° to 180°. {m:tan^(-1)} gives angles between {m:-90deg} and 90°.

Key idea: to find every solution of {m:sin x=k}, {m:cos x=k} or {m:tan x=k} in an interval:

  1. Find the calculator value {m:alpha}. For sine and cosine, k must be between {m:-1} and 1 (including both). If it is not, there are no solutions.
  2. Find the second angle by symmetry. For {m:sin x=k} it is {m:180deg-alpha}. For {m:cos x=k} it is {m:-alpha}. For {m:tan x=k} there is no second angle in the same period: the next solution is {m:alpha+180deg}.
  3. Add or subtract 360° to each angle, as many times as you need (180° for tangent). Stop when the angles leave the interval.
  4. Keep only the angles inside the interval. Include an end point if the interval does. Check how many you expect from the graph.

In full: the solutions of {m:sin x=k} are {m:x=alpha+360deg*n} and {m:x=180deg-alpha+360deg*n}. The solutions of {m:cos x=k} are {m:x=alpha+360deg*n} and {m:x=-alpha+360deg*n}. The solutions of {m:tan x=k} are {m:x=alpha+180deg*n}. In each, n is any whole number: positive, negative or zero.

  4. The solutions are {m:x=-330deg}, {m:-210deg}, {m:30deg} and {m:150deg}. This matches the four points in the figure.

4. A multiple of the angle

Sometimes the angle inside the sine is not {m:x} but {m:2x}, {m:3x} or {m:frac(x,2)}. The graph of {m:y=sin 2x} goes through two cycles while {m:y=sin x} goes through one, so the line cuts it more often. You must work out the longer interval first.

Key idea: to solve an equation in {m:kx}, where k is a positive number, for {m:a<=x<=b}:

  1. Write the interval for {m:kx}. Multiply every part by k: {m:ka<=kx<=kb}.
  2. Treat {m:kx} as one unknown. Find every solution for {m:kx} in the new interval, as in lesson 3.
  3. Divide each solution by k to get x.

The new interval is k times as long, so there are about k times as many solutions.

Why it works

  • The first identity. The point P at angle {m:theta} on the unit circle has coordinates {m:(cos theta, sin theta)}. Every point of the circle has {m:x^2+y^2=1}. Putting in the coordinates of P gives {m:cos^2 theta+sin^2 theta=1}. This works at every angle, because P is always on the circle.
  • The second identity. The tangent is the gradient of the line from the origin to P. The line goes {m:sin theta} up for {m:cos theta} across, so the gradient is {m:frac(sin theta, cos theta)}. If {m:cos theta=0}, the line is upright and has no gradient, so the tangent has no value.
  • Finding the second solution. The sine wave has a mirror line at 90°, so {m:sin(180deg-x)=sin x}. The cosine wave has a mirror line at the y-axis, so {m:cos(-x)=cos x}. The tangent wave repeats every 180°. These three facts, with the 360° period of sine and cosine, give all the solutions.
  • A longer interval for {m:kx}. When x goes from a to b, {m:kx} goes from {m:ka} to {m:kb}. The graph of {m:sin kx} makes k times as many cycles as {m:sin x}, so a horizontal line cuts it about k times as often. That is why you must solve in the longer interval and then divide.
  • A shifted angle. When x goes from a to b, {m:x+c} goes from {m:a+c} to {m:b+c}. A solution for the bracket that is outside the new interval does not give an x in the old interval. A solution that was outside the old interval can be inside the new one, as in {m:sin(x+40deg)=0.5}.
  • Why not divide by {m:cos x} or {m:sin x}. If {m:cos x=0} at some angle, dividing by it is dividing by 0, and any solution at that angle is lost. For {m:a sin x=b cos x}, with a not 0, the angle cannot be a solution with {m:cos x=0}: it would make {m:sin x=0} too, and {m:sin^2 x+cos^2 x=1} says they are never both 0. So there the division loses nothing.
  • Rejecting a value. The sine and cosine of any angle are between {m:-1} and 1, because they are coordinates of a point on the unit circle. A value outside that range cannot happen, so the equation has no solution for it.

Common traps

  • Giving only the calculator’s answer. An equation like {m:sin x=0.5} usually has more than one solution in an interval.
  • Using the wrong pair. Sine uses {m:x} and {m:180deg-x}. Cosine uses {m:x} and {m:-x}, which is {m:360deg-x} in a 0° to 360° interval. Tangent uses {m:x} and {m:x+180deg}.
  • Solving for {m:2x} or {m:x+30deg} and forgetting to finish. Divide or subtract at the end to get x.
  • Using the interval for x as the interval for {m:2x} or for {m:x+30deg}. Work out the new interval first.
  • Dividing both sides by {m:sin x} or {m:cos x}. Move every term to one side and factorise instead.
  • Forgetting a factor. When you factorise, every factor gives its own solutions.
  • Forgetting the second sign when you take a square root: {m:cos^2 x=0.25} gives {m:cos x=0.5} and {m:cos x=-0.5}.
  • Forgetting that an end point counts when the interval uses {m:<=} or {m:>=}.
  • Keeping a value of {m:sin x} or {m:cos x} that is outside {m:-1} to 1.
  • Setting the calculator to radians. Check that it shows DEG.
