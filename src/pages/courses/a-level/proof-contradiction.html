Proof by contradiction

Some statements are hard to prove by starting from the facts. Try the opposite. Assume the statement is false, and follow the steps carefully. If they lead to something impossible, the assumption must be wrong, and the statement is true. This page shows the method in two famous proofs: {m:sqrt(2)} cannot be written as a fraction of two whole numbers, and there are infinitely many prime numbers. Then you use the same method on proofs you have not seen before.

Where you will use this: in questions that say “prove that … is irrational”, “prove that there are no integers …” or “prove that there is no greatest …”. It is also the first move in many proofs in Further Pure and at university.

1. Assume the opposite

A proof by deduction starts from known facts and moves forward. A {term:proof-by-contradiction|proof by contradiction} works the other way. You assume the statement you want to prove is false. Then you follow valid steps. If you reach something that cannot be true, called a {term:contradiction}, then the assumption was wrong. So the statement is true.

This works because a {term:statement} is either true or false, never both. If “the statement is false” leads to nonsense, only “the statement is true” is left.

Key idea: four steps.

  • 1. Assume the opposite. Write the {term:negation} of the statement. It is true exactly when the statement is false.
  • 2. Reason. Use algebra and known facts, one valid step at a time.
  • 3. Reach a contradiction. Something impossible, such as “n is even and n is odd”, or a fraction in lowest terms that is not in lowest terms.
  • 4. Conclude. Say that the assumption is false, so the original statement is true. Write it in the words of the question.

The negation must be exactly right. If it is too weak or too strong, the proof proves the wrong thing. These rules give the negation:

  • The negation of “every …” is “at least one … is not”. It is never “none”.
  • The negation of “there is no …” is “there is at least one …”.
  • The negation of “A or B” is “not A and not B”. The negation of “A and B” is “not A or not B”.
  • The negation of {m:x>3} is {m:x<=3}. The negation of “n is even” is “n is odd”, when n is an integer.

Worked example: there is no greatest integer

Prove that there is no greatest integer.

  1. Assume the opposite. The statement says “there is no greatest integer”. Its negation is “there is a greatest integer”. Call it N.
  2. Reason. The number {m:N+1} is also an integer, because adding 1 to an integer gives an integer.
  3. Contradiction. {m:N+1>N}, so N is not the greatest integer. But N was assumed to be the greatest.
  4. Conclude. The assumption is false. So there is no greatest integer.

Worked example: writing the negation

Write the negation of each statement. For each one, say whether the statement is true or false.

  1. “Every prime number is odd.” Negation: “at least one prime number is not odd”. The number 2 is prime and even, so the negation is true and the statement is false.
  2. “{m:a>5} or {m:b>5}.” Negation: “{m:a<=5} and {m:b<=5}”. Both parts must fail, so the “or” becomes “and”.
  3. “There is no even prime number.” Negation: “there is an even prime number”. The number 2 is one, so the negation is true.

Exactly one of a statement and its negation is true. They are never both true and never both false.

2. Squares, odd and even

The two famous proofs need one fact first. If you know that {m:n^2} is even, can you say that n is even? Test: {m:6^2=36} is even and 6 is even. But a few examples are not a proof. Here is the proof, and it is a small proof by contradiction.

Key idea: for an integer n, if {m:n^2} is even then n is even. If {m:n^2} is a multiple of 3, then n is a multiple of 3.

  • Assume the opposite: n is not even, or n is not a multiple of 3.
  • Write n in each remaining form, and square it. Each square leaves a remainder that {m:n^2} cannot have.
  • That contradicts what you were told about {m:n^2}.
  • The rule is true when the number is a prime, such as 2, 3 or 5. It is false for 4: {m:2^2=4} is a multiple of 4, but 2 is not.

Worked example: {m:n^2} even gives n even

Prove that if {m:n^2} is even then n is even, for an integer n.

  1. Assume the opposite. {m:n^2} is even and n is not even. So n is odd, and {m:n=2k+1} for an integer k.
  2. Square it: {m:(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1}.
  3. {m:2k^2+2k} is an integer, so this is 2 times an integer plus 1. It is odd.
  4. Contradiction. {m:n^2} is even and {m:n^2} is odd.
  5. Conclude. The assumption is false. So n is even.

Worked example: {m:n^2} a multiple of 3 gives n a multiple of 3

Prove that if {m:n^2} is a multiple of 3 then n is a multiple of 3, for an integer n.

  1. Assume the opposite. {m:n^2} is a multiple of 3 and n is not. Every integer is {m:3k}, {m:3k+1} or {m:3k+2}, so n is {m:3k+1} or {m:3k+2}.
  2. Case {m:n=3k+1}: {m:(3k+1)^2=9k^2+6k+1=3(3k^2+2k)+1}. The remainder is 1.
  3. Case {m:n=3k+2}: {m:(3k+2)^2=9k^2+12k+4=3(3k^2+4k+1)+1}. The remainder is 1.
  4. Contradiction. In both cases {m:n^2} leaves remainder 1, so it is not a multiple of 3.
  5. Conclude. The assumption is false. So n is a multiple of 3.

Worked example: the rule fails for 4

Is it true that if {m:n^2} is a multiple of 4, then n is a multiple of 4?

  1. Try {m:n=2}. Then {m:n^2=4}, which is a multiple of 4.
  2. But 2 is not a multiple of 4.
  3. So the statement is false, and {m:n=2} is a counterexample. A proof like the one above would fail. The square of every even number is a multiple of 4, so n does not have to be a multiple of 4.

The rule is true when the number is prime, such as 2, 3 and 5. It is not true for every number. The numbers 4, 8 and 9 fail: the squares of 2, 4 and 3 are multiples of 4, 8 and 9.

3. The square root of 2 is irrational

A {term:rational-number|rational number} can be written as {m:frac(a, b)}, where a and b are {term:integer|integers} and b is not 0. A number that cannot be written this way is an {term:irrational-number|irrational number}. Look at a square with side 1. By Pythagoras, its diagonal has length {m:sqrt(1^2+1^2)=sqrt(2)}.

Your calculator gives {m:sqrt(2)=1.414213562"..."}. It shows only about ten digits, so it cannot show that the digits never end and never repeat. A proof must cover every possible fraction, and there are infinitely many.

Key idea: the proof that {m:sqrt(2)} is irrational has four stages.

  • Assume {m:sqrt(2)=frac(a, b)}, where a and b are positive integers with no common factor. This is {term:lowest-terms|lowest terms}. You can always reach it by cancelling.
  • Square: {m:a^2=2b^2}. So {m:a^2} is even, and so a is even (section 2).
  • Write {m:a=2k}. Then {m:4k^2=2b^2}, so {m:b^2=2k^2}. So {m:b^2} is even, and so b is even.
  • Now a and b are both even. They share the factor 2. This contradicts “no common factor”. So {m:sqrt(2)} is irrational.

Worked example: the proof, line by line

Prove that {m:sqrt(2)} is irrational.

  1. Assume the opposite. Suppose {m:sqrt(2)} is rational. Then {m:sqrt(2)=frac(a, b)} for positive integers a and b. Cancel every common factor, so a and b have no common factor greater than 1.
  2. Square both sides: {m:2=frac(a^2, b^2)}. Multiply by {m:b^2}: {m:a^2=2b^2}.
  3. So {m:a^2} is 2 times an integer. It is even. By section 2, a is even.
  4. Write {m:a=2k}, where k is an integer. Then {m:(2k)^2=4k^2=2b^2}. Divide by 2: {m:b^2=2k^2}.
  5. So {m:b^2} is even. By section 2, b is even.
  6. Contradiction. a and b are both even, so they have the common factor 2. But they have no common factor.
  7. Conclude. The assumption is false. So {m:sqrt(2)} is irrational.

4. Other irrational numbers

The same steps prove other roots are irrational. They also show that adding a rational number, or multiplying by a rational number that is not 0, keeps a number irrational. Each proof uses a result you already have.

Key idea: to prove {m:sqrt(p)} is irrational, where p is 2, 3 or 5, follow the {m:sqrt(2)} proof with p in place of 2.

  • Assume {m:sqrt(p)=frac(a, b)} in lowest terms. Then {m:a^2=p*b^2}.
  • So {m:a^2} is a multiple of p, so a is a multiple of p (section 2). Write {m:a=pk}.

5. There are infinitely many primes

A {term:prime-number|prime number} is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two factors: 1 and itself. The primes start 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. Do they ever stop? Euclid, a Greek mathematician, gave a proof more than two thousand years ago. It is a proof by contradiction.

The proof uses three facts. Fact 1: every integer greater than 1 has at least one {term:prime-factor|prime factor}. Fact 2: if a number divides two integers, it divides their difference. Fact 3: no prime number divides 1.

Key idea: Euclid’s proof, step by step.

  • Assume there are finitely many primes. List them all: {m:p_1}, {m:p_2}, …, {m:p_n}.
  • Let P be their product, and let {m:N=P+1}. Then N is greater than 1, so it has a prime factor q.
  • q is a prime, so it is on the list. So q divides P. Also q divides N. So q divides {m:N-P=1}.
  • No prime divides 1. This is a contradiction, so there are infinitely many primes.
  • N does not have to be prime. The proof only needs N to have a prime factor.

  2. The primes 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 give {m:P=30030} and {m:N=30031}.
  3. {m:30031=59*509}. So N is not prime. But its prime factors 59 and 509 are not on the list either.
  4. Each prime on the list leaves remainder 1 when it divides N. For example {m:30031=2*15015+1} and {m:30031=13*2310+1}. So none of them is a factor of N.

6. Using the method on a proof you have not seen

Exam questions ask for new proofs, not only {m:sqrt(2)} and Euclid. The method is the same. Work through the four steps and look for the kind of clash that suits the question.

Key idea: how to attack an unfamiliar proof.

  • Spot the signs. Statements such as “there are no integers …”, “is irrational”, “there is no greatest or smallest …” are often easier by contradiction.
  • Write the negation exactly. “There are no …” becomes “there are some …”. “A or B” becomes “not A and not B”.
  • Look for the clash. Common clashes: a number that is even and odd, a fraction not in lowest terms, a number that is rational and irrational, a “greatest” that is beaten, an inequality that cannot hold.
  • Finish with a sentence. Say the assumption is false, so the statement is true.

Worked example: no integer solutions

Prove that there are no integers m and n such that {m:14m+21n=1}.

  1. Assume the opposite. There are integers m and n with {m:14m+21n=1}.
  2. Take out the common factor: {m:14m+21n=7(2m+3n)}. So {m:7(2m+3n)=1}.
  3. {m:2m+3n} is an integer, so 1 would be a multiple of 7.
  4. Contradiction. 1 is not a multiple of 7.
  5. Conclude. There are no such integers.

Worked example: an “or” in the conclusion

For real numbers a and b, prove that if {m:a+b>10} then {m:a>5} or {m:b>5}.

  1. Assume the opposite. {m:a+b>10}, and not ({m:a>5} or {m:b>5}). The negation of “or” is “and”: {m:a<=5} and {m:b<=5}.
  2. Add the two inequalities: {m:a+b<=10}.
  3. Contradiction. {m:a+b>10} and {m:a+b<=10} cannot both be true.
  4. Conclude. If {m:a+b>10}, then {m:a>5} or {m:b>5}.

  1. Assume the opposite. There is a smallest positive rational number r. Write {m:r=frac(a, b)}, where a and b are positive integers.
  2. Then {m:frac(r, 2)=frac(a, 2b)}. This is a rational number, and it is positive.

  4. Conclude. There is no smallest positive rational number.

The square root of 2 is irrational.

  1. Assume {m:sqrt(2)=frac(a, b)}, where a and b are positive integers with no common factor.
  2. Then {m:a^2=2b^2}, so {m:a^2} is even, so a is even.
  3. Write {m:a=2k}. Then {m:b^2=2k^2}, so {m:b^2} is even, so b is even.
  4. a and b are both even, which contradicts having no common factor. So {m:sqrt(2)} is irrational.

There are infinitely many primes.

  1. Assume there are finitely many primes, {m:p_1}, {m:p_2}, …, {m:p_n}.
  2. Let {m:N=p_1*p_2*"..."*p_n+1}. N has a prime factor q.
  3. q is one of the {m:p_i}, so q divides the product, and q divides N. So q divides 1.
  4. No prime divides 1. Contradiction, so there are infinitely many primes.

[4 marks] Prove by contradiction that there are no integers x and y such that {m:x^2-y^2=2}. Write your answer on paper, then open the mark scheme.

  2. Look at the sum of the two brackets. Is it even or odd?

Why it works

  • A statement is true or false, never both. If “the statement is false” leads to something impossible, then it cannot be false, so it is true.
  • Valid steps keep the truth. If the assumption were true, every correct step would give a true result. A false result such as “1 is a multiple of 7” can only come from a false assumption.
  • Lowest terms always exist. Any fraction can be cancelled until the top and bottom share no factor. So “{m:sqrt(2)} is a fraction” means “{m:sqrt(2)} is a fraction in lowest terms”.
  • Primes behave. A prime that divides a product of two integers divides one of them. This is why “{m:n^2} is a multiple of 2 or 3” forces “n is a multiple of 2 or 3”. It is not true for 4, because 4 is not prime: 4 divides {m:2*2}, but it does not divide 2.
  • Adding 1 breaks the list. P is a multiple of every prime on the list, so {m:P+1} is one more than a multiple of each. So no prime on the list divides it.

Common traps

  • Writing the negation wrongly. “Every … is odd” becomes “at least one … is not odd”, not “every … is even”.
  • Forgetting to say that {m:frac(a, b)} is in lowest terms, so that “both even” is not a contradiction.
  • Stopping after one of a and b is even. You need to show both.
  • Saying that Euclid’s number N is prime. It only has a prime factor that is not on the list.
  • Using the {m:sqrt(2)} proof for a square number such as 4. Square numbers have rational roots.
  • Saying that the sum of two irrational numbers is irrational, or that the product of a rational and an irrational number is irrational when the rational number is 0.
  • Using the “n squared is a multiple of … so n is a multiple of …” fact for a number such as 4 or 9, where it is false. The page proves it only for primes.
  • Ending without a sentence that says the assumption is false and the statement is true.
  • Treating many examples, or a calculator display, as a proof.
