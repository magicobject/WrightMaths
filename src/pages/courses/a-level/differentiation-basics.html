Differentiation from first principles

A curve is steeper in some places than in others. Differentiation finds exactly how steep it is at any point. You will build the idea from a straight line, then learn the shortcut that every later calculus topic uses.

Where you will use this: finding a speed from a distance graph, how fast a tank fills or drains, the highest or lowest point of a curve (the next topic), and every Year 2 calculus topic.

1. The gradient of a curve

The {term:gradient} of a straight line is the same everywhere: rise divided by run. A curve bends, so its steepness changes from point to point. We need a way to talk about the steepness at one point.

Zoom in very close on a smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. That line is the {term:tangent-to-curve|tangent} to the curve at that point. It touches the curve there and points the same way as the curve.

Key idea: the {term:gradient-of-curve|gradient of a curve} at a point is the gradient of the tangent at that point.

To read a gradient, draw a triangle under the tangent and divide the distance up by the distance across. A positive gradient means the curve is rising at that point. A negative gradient means it is falling.

At A, the gradient is 2: the curve rises 2 units for each 1 unit across.

Worked example

The tangent to a curve at P passes through (1, 4) and (3, 10). Find the gradient of the curve at P.

  1. The gradient of the curve at P is the gradient of the tangent. Use the two points on the tangent.
  2. Rise = 10 − 4 = 6. Run = 3 − 1 = 2.
  3. Gradient = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. At P, the curve rises 3 units for each 1 unit across.

2. Chords closing in on a tangent

A tangent needs only one point, but we can estimate its gradient from two points. A {term:chord-of-curve|chord} is a straight line segment that joins two points on a curve. Its gradient is rise over run, like any line.

Take the point A (1, 1) on {m:y=x^2}. Pick a second point B on the curve, further to the right. Now slide B along the curve towards A. The chord AB turns, and it gets closer and closer to the tangent at A.

Here are more chords from A, with the x-coordinate of B and the gradient of the chord AB:

  • B at {m:x=3}: {m:frac(9-1, 3-1)=4}.
  • B at {m:x=2}: {m:frac(4-1, 2-1)=3}.
  • B at {m:x=1.5}: {m:frac(2.25-1, 1.5-1)=2.5}.
  • B at {m:x=1.1}: {m:frac(1.21-1, 0.1)=2.1}.
  • B at {m:x=1.01}: {m:frac(1.0201-1, 0.01)=2.01}.

The gradients 4, 3, 2.5, 2.1 and 2.01 are getting close to 2. That is the gradient of the tangent at A.

Key idea: as the second point of a chord moves towards the first, the gradient of the chord gets closer to the gradient of the tangent. Each chord gives an estimate. The gradient of the tangent is the value the estimates get close to. B can never be exactly A: a chord needs two different points.

Worked example

  2. Rise = 9.61 − 9 = 0.61. Run = 3.1 − 3 = 0.1.
  3. Gradient of the chord = 0.61 ÷ 0.1 = 6.1. This is an estimate. A closer point, {m:x=3.01}, gives {m:frac(9.0601-9, 0.01)=6.01}. The estimates are getting close to 6.

3. Limits, and differentiation from first principles

The limit idea

Look again at the chord from A (1, 1) to a point B on {m:y=x^2}. Give B the x-coordinate {m:1+h}. The number h is the run: how far B is to the right of A. (If h is negative, B is to the left. The algebra works for either sign.) So B is the point {m:(1+h, (1+h)^2)}.

The rise is {m:(1+h)^2-1=2h+h^2}. The gradient of the chord is {m:frac(2h+h^2, h)}. We may divide the top and the bottom by h, because h is not 0. If h were 0, B would be A and there would be no chord. So for every h that is not 0, the gradient of the chord is {m:2+h}.

As h gets closer to 0, {m:2+h} gets closer to 2. We say that 2 is the {term:limit} of {m:2+h} as h tends to 0 (h gets close to 0), and write

{md:lim(h -> 0) (2+h)=2}

Read it as "the limit, as h tends to zero, of 2 plus h, is 2". "Tends to" means "gets as close as you like to".

Key idea: a limit is the value an expression gets close to as h gets close to 0. The expression does not have to reach that value, and h is never actually 0. So simplify first, while h is not 0, and then see what value the result gets close to. Do not put {m:h=0} into the fraction: it gives 0 over 0, which has no value.

The derivative from the definition

Now do the same at any point. Write the curve as {m:y=f(x)}, read as "f of x". It means the height of the curve at x. Let A be the point {m:(x, f(x))} and let B be the point {m:(x+h, f(x+h))}.

The gradient of the chord is {m:frac(f(x+h)-f(x), h)}. The gradient of the curve at x is the limit of this as h tends to 0. This limit is called the {term:derivative} of f. It is written f′(x), read as "f dash of x".

Key idea (the definition of the derivative):

{md:f'(x)=lim(h -> 0) frac(f(x+h)-f(x), h)}

Using this to find a derivative is called {term:first-principles|differentiation from first principles}.

The method is the same every time:

  1. Write {m:f(x+h)} and expand it.
  2. Find {m:f(x+h)-f(x)}, divide by h and simplify. Now h should have cancelled.
  3. Let h tend to 0.

Worked example

Differentiate {m:f(x)=x^2} from first principles.

  1. {m:f(x+h)=(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2}.
  2. {m:f(x+h)-f(x)=2xh+h^2}. Divide by h, which is allowed because h is not 0: {m:frac(2xh+h^2, h)=2x+h}.
  3. As h tends to 0, {m:2x+h} tends to {m:2x}. So {m:f'(x)=lim(h -> 0)(2x+h)=2x}.

The gradient of {m:y=x^2} at any point is twice the x-coordinate. At {m:x=1} it is 2, as the tangent triangle showed.

4. Notation and the power rule

Two ways to write a derivative

If {m:y=f(x)}, the derivative can be written {m:f'(x)} or {m:frac(dy, dx)}. Say "dy by dx". The symbol means "the derivative of y with respect to x".

The letter d is not a number. It stands for "a small change in". So {m:frac(dy, dx)} is a single symbol: you cannot cancel the d's. We also write {m:frac(d, dx)(x^2)=2x}, read as "d by dx of x squared is 2x". The symbol d by dx means: {term:differentiate} what follows with respect to x.

A pattern

We found two results from first principles: {m:x^2} has derivative {m:2x}, and {m:x^3} has derivative {m:3x^2}. The same method shows that {m:x^4} has derivative {m:4x^3}. Two more facts. The derivative of {m:x} is 1, because the graph of {m:y=x} has gradient 1 everywhere. The derivative of a constant is 0, because the graph of {m:y=7} is horizontal.

Key idea (the {term:power-rule|power rule}):

{md:frac(d, dx)(x^n)=n x^(n-1)}

Bring the power down to the front as a multiplier. Then subtract 1 from the power (this is "taking 1 off"). This works for every rational number n, wherever {m:x^n} is defined: whole numbers, negative numbers and fractions.

We have proved the rule for {m:n=2} and {m:n=3}. The proof for every positive whole number n is in "Why it works" at the end of this page. For negative and fractional n we use the rule without proof here.

Constants, multiples and sums

Three more facts let you differentiate any sum of powers of x.

  • A constant on its own has derivative 0.
  • A constant multiple stays in front: {m:frac(d, dx)(k f(x))=k f'(x)}. For example, {m:5x^4} has derivative {m:5*4x^3=20x^3}.
  • A sum is differentiated term by term: the derivative of {m:f(x)+g(x)} is {m:f'(x)+g'(x)}.

Why this works: multiplying a function by 5 multiplies every chord gradient by 5. Adding two functions adds their chord gradients. Both facts still hold after you take the limit.

Worked example

  2. {m:-5x^2} gives {m:-5*2x=-10x}. {m:7x} is {m:7x^1}, which gives 7. The constant {m:-9} gives 0.
  3. {m:frac(dy, dx)=12x^2-10x+7}.

6. Expand or split before you differentiate

7. The second derivative

Differentiating goes from a function to its derivative. Now run it backwards: given a derivative, find a function that has it.

Forwards

Why it works

  • The tangent is the limit of chords. A chord needs two different points, so h is never 0. We simplify while h is not 0, and then ask what value the chord gradient gets close to. That is how a gradient at one point comes from a gradient between two points.
  • The power rule, for positive whole n. Use the {term:binomial-expansion|binomial expansion} of {m:(x+h)^n}. The first two terms are {m:x^n} and {m:n x^(n-1) h}. Every other term has at least {m:h^2} in it. Subtract {m:x^n} and divide by h: you get {m:n x^(n-1)} plus terms that each still contain h. As h tends to 0, those terms tend to 0. What is left is {m:n x^(n-1)}.
  • Negative and fractional powers. The same rule holds, and the pictures agree: {m:y=sqrt(x)} flattens as x grows, so its gradient {m:frac(1, 2)x^(-1/2)} falls. {m:y=1/x} falls, so its gradient {m:-x^(-2)} is negative. A full proof for these needs ideas from Year 2.
  • Constant multiples and sums. If you multiply a function by 5, every chord gradient is multiplied by 5, and so is the limit. If you add two functions, the chord gradients add, and so do the limits.
  • A constant has derivative 0. Its graph is horizontal, so every chord gradient is 0.
  • The second derivative. It is the rate of change of the gradient. If the gradient is increasing, the tangent turns anticlockwise as you move right, and the curve bends upwards.
  • Working backwards loses the constant. Constants differentiate to 0, so many functions share one derivative. A point on the curve fixes the constant.

Common traps

  • Putting {m:h=0} into the chord gradient before cancelling h.
  • Stopping at {m:2x+h}, or writing it as the derivative. The limit has not been taken yet.
  • Bringing the power down but not taking 1 off it, or taking 1 off but not bringing it down.
  • Leaving a constant term in the derivative. It differentiates to 0.
  • Differentiating {m:frac(1, x^2)} or {m:sqrt(x)} without rewriting them as powers first.
  • Differentiating the top and the bottom of a fraction separately, or multiplying the derivatives of two factors.
  • Losing a minus sign when the power is negative: {m:-2*(-3)=+6}.
  • Putting the x-value into the original function instead of into the derivative.
  • Reading {m:frac(d^2y, dx^2)} as {m:(frac(dy, dx))^2}.
  • Forgetting the constant when working backwards from a gradient.
