Polynomials and the factor theorem

A polynomial is an expression such as {m:x^3-4x^2+x+6}. This page shows you how to expand products of brackets and how to divide a polynomial by a bracket. It then shows the factor theorem, a quick test for whether a bracket is a factor. You use the test to factorise cubics and to solve cubic equations. You also work backwards, from the roots to the polynomial.

Where you will use this: in Graphs of polynomials, to sketch a cubic from its roots. Also in Differentiation, to find where a cubic has its turning points. It comes back in Algebraic fractions, for splitting a fraction into partial fractions. It helps whenever a cubic equation appears and you need its solutions.

1. Polynomials and expanding

A {term:polynomial} is a sum of terms. Each term is a number times a power of x, and each power is a whole number: 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on. So {m:3x^4-2x^2+x-7} is a polynomial. {m:frac(1, x)+x} is not, because {m:frac(1, x)} is {m:x^(-1)}, which is not a whole-number power.

The number in front of a power of x is its {term:coefficient}. In {m:5x^3-x^2+4}, the coefficient of {m:x^3} is 5 and the coefficient of {m:x^2} is {m:-1}. The coefficient of x is 0, because there is no x term. The number on its own, 4, is the {term:constant-term|constant term}.

The {term:degree|degree} of a polynomial is its highest power of x. So {m:5x^3-x^2+4} has degree 3. A polynomial of degree 3 is a {term:cubic}. A polynomial of degree 4 is a {term:quartic}. A quadratic has degree 2.

We often name a polynomial {m:f(x)}. Then {m:f(2)} means the value of the polynomial when {m:x=2}. For {m:f(x)=x^3-4x}, {m:f(2)=8-8=0}.

Key idea: to expand brackets, multiply every term in one bracket by every term in the other. Then collect the terms with the same power of x. When you multiply three brackets, multiply two of them first. Then multiply the result by the third.

Worked example: three brackets

Expand and simplify {m:(x+2)(x-3)(x+1)}.

  1. Multiply the first two brackets: {m:(x+2)(x-3)=x^2-3x+2x-6=x^2-x-6}.
  2. Multiply the result by {m:(x+1)}. Take x first: {m:x(x^2-x-6)=x^3-x^2-6x}.
  3. Now take 1: {m:1*(x^2-x-6)=x^2-x-6}.
  4. Add the two lines: {m:x^3-x^2-6x+x^2-x-6}.
  5. The {m:x^2} terms cancel, and {m:-6x-x=-7x}. So the answer is {m:x^3-7x-6}.

The answer has no {m:x^2} term. Its coefficient is 0. The degree is 3: three brackets, each with an x, give {m:x^3}.

Worked example: a bracket times a quadratic

Expand and simplify {m:(2x-1)(x^2+3x-4)}.

  1. Take {m:2x} first: {m:2x(x^2+3x-4)=2x^3+6x^2-8x}.
  2. Take {m:-1} next: {m:-1*(x^2+3x-4)=-x^2-3x+4}.
  3. Add: {m:2x^3+6x^2-8x-x^2-3x+4}.
  4. Collect like terms: {m:6x^2-x^2=5x^2} and {m:-8x-3x=-11x}.

The answer is {m:2x^3+5x^2-11x+4}. The degree is 1 + 2 = 3, the sum of the degrees of the two factors.

To add or subtract polynomials, collect the terms with the same power of x. When you subtract a bracket, change the sign of every term inside it.

Worked example: subtracting polynomials

  3. Collect the x terms: {m:-x-x=-2x}. Collect the numbers: {m:2+5=7}.

The answer is {m:2x^3+2x^2-2x+7}.

2. Dividing by (x ± p)

You can divide one number by another and get a quotient and a remainder. {m:17=5*3+2} has quotient 3 and remainder 2. You can do the same with polynomials. This is {term:polynomial-division}.

When you divide a polynomial {m:f(x)} by a bracket such as {m:(x-2)}, you get a {term:quotient} {m:q(x)} and a {term:remainder} r. They fit together like this.

{md:f(x)=(x-2)*q(x)+r}

The remainder r is a number, because it has a lower degree than the bracket. The quotient has degree one less than {m:f(x)}. When the remainder is 0, the division is exact, and the bracket is a factor of {m:f(x)}.

There are two methods. Both work for brackets such as {m:(x-2)} and {m:(x+2)}.

Method 1: compare coefficients

Write the quotient with letters for its unknown coefficients. Expand the product. Then match the coefficient of each power of x.

Worked example: compare coefficients

Divide {m:x^3+2x^2-5x-6} by {m:(x-2)}. You are told the division is exact.

  1. The quotient has degree 2. Write {m:x^3+2x^2-5x-6=(x-2)(x^2+ax+b)}.
  2. Expand the right side: {m:x^3+ax^2+bx-2x^2-2ax-2b}. Collect: {m:x^3+(a-2)x^2+(b-2a)x-2b}.
  3. Match {m:x^2}: {m:a-2=2}, so {m:a=4}.
  4. Match x: {m:b-2a=-5}, so {m:b-8=-5} and {m:b=3}.
  5. Check the number term: {m:-2b=-6}. This agrees with the left side.

The quotient is {m:x^2+4x+3}. The check on the number term works because the remainder is 0.

Method 2: long division

Long division works like long division of numbers. At each step, divide the highest power left by x. Write the result in the quotient. Multiply it by the whole bracket. Subtract. Then repeat with what is left.

Key idea: in long division, each step has three moves. Divide the highest power left by x. Multiply the answer by the whole bracket. Subtract the product, changing every sign. Stop when what is left has a lower degree than the bracket. That is the remainder.

Worked example: long division

Divide {m:x^3-6x^2+11x-6} by {m:(x-1)}.

  1. Divide {m:x^3} by x. This gives {m:x^2}. Multiply: {m:x^2(x-1)=x^3-x^2}. Subtract: {m:-6x^2-(-x^2)=-5x^2}. What is left is {m:-5x^2+11x-6}.
  2. Divide {m:-5x^2} by x. This gives {m:-5x}. Multiply: {m:-5x(x-1)=-5x^2+5x}. Subtract: {m:11x-5x=6x}. What is left is {m:6x-6}.
  3. Divide {m:6x} by x. This gives 6. Multiply: {m:6(x-1)=6x-6}. Subtract: 0.

The quotient is {m:x^2-5x+6} and the remainder is 0. Check: {m:(x-1)(x^2-5x+6)=x^3-6x^2+11x-6}.

3. The factor theorem

Section 2 gave this fact. When you divide {m:f(x)} by {m:(x-p)}, then {m:f(x)=(x-p)q(x)+r}. Put {m:x=p} in both sides. The bracket {m:(x-p)} becomes 0, so the product disappears. That leaves {m:f(p)=r}.

So the remainder is {m:f(p)}. The bracket is a factor exactly when the remainder is 0. This is the {term:factor-theorem}.

Key idea: the bracket {m:(x-p)} is a factor of {m:f(x)} exactly when {m:f(p)=0}. To test a bracket, find the value of x that makes it zero. Put that value into {m:f(x)}. If you get 0, the bracket is a factor. If you get anything else, it is not.

Take care with the sign. For {m:(x-2)}, the value that makes the bracket zero is {m:x=2}. For {m:(x+3)}, it is {m:x=-3}. This value is also a {term:root|root} of the equation {m:f(x)=0}.

Worked example: testing brackets

The polynomial is {m:f(x)=x^3+2x^2-5x-6}. Test the brackets {m:(x-2)}, {m:(x+1)} and {m:(x-1)}.

  1. For {m:(x-2)}, use {m:x=2}: {m:f(2)=8+8-10-6=0}. So {m:(x-2)} is a factor.
  2. For {m:(x+1)}, use {m:x=-1}: {m:f(-1)=-1+2+5-6=0}. So {m:(x+1)} is a factor.
  3. For {m:(x-1)}, use {m:x=1}: {m:f(1)=1+2-5-6=-8}. This is not 0, so {m:(x-1)} is not a factor.

The value {m:-8} is the remainder when you divide by {m:(x-1)}. Also {m:f(-3)=-27+18+15-6=0}, so {m:(x+3)} is a third factor.

A bracket such as {m:(2x-1)} works in the same way. The value that makes it zero is {m:x=frac(1, 2)}.

Worked example: a bracket with a number in front of x

Show that {m:(2x-1)} is a factor of {m:h(x)=2x^3+3x^2-8x+3}.

  1. The bracket is zero when {m:2x-1=0}. So {m:x=frac(1, 2)}.
  2. Work out {m:h(frac(1, 2))=2*frac(1, 8)+3*frac(1, 4)-8*frac(1, 2)+3}.
  3. This is {m:frac(1, 4)+frac(3, 4)-4+3=1-4+3=0}.

{m:h(frac(1, 2))=0}, so {m:(2x-1)} is a factor. Notice that {m:h(1)=2+3-8+3=0} as well. So {m:(x-1)} is also a factor.

Extra: the remainder theorem

Why it works

  • Expanding. Multiplying every term by every term is the distributive law used twice. It keeps the value the same for every x, so the expanded form is another way to write the same polynomial.
  • Division. {m:f(x)=(x-p)q(x)+r} is the same fact as {m:17=5*3+2}. You can always check a division by multiplying back and adding the remainder.
  • The remainder is a number. You stop dividing when what is left has a lower degree than the bracket. The bracket has degree 1, so what is left has degree 0. That is a number.
  • The factor theorem. Put {m:x=p} into {m:f(x)=(x-p)q(x)+r}. The product {m:(p-p)q(p)} is 0 times a number, which is 0. So {m:f(p)=r}. A remainder of 0 means {m:(x-p)} divides {m:f(x)} exactly, so it is a factor. If {m:(x-p)} is a factor, the remainder is 0.
  • Trying the factors of the constant term. Take {m:x^3+ax^2+bx+c} with whole-number coefficients, and suppose a whole number p is a root. Then {m:p^3+ap^2+bp+c=0}, so {m:c=-p(p^2+ap+b)}. So p divides c. When the coefficient of {m:x^3} is not 1, a fraction can be a root. It then has the form (a divisor of c) over (a divisor of the leading coefficient).
  • At most three roots. Each root gives one linear factor. A cubic has degree 3, so it has at most three linear factors. A repeated root uses up two of the three factors. The graph touches the axis there, so the cubic has fewer than three different roots.
  • Building from roots. The product of {m:(x-p)} for each root p is zero at each root and nowhere else. Any number times it has the same roots. A point on the curve fixes that number.

Common traps

  • Testing {m:(x+3)} with {m:f(3)}. The bracket {m:(x+3)} is zero when {m:x=-3}, so test {m:f(-3)}.
  • Forgetting the missing power. Write {m:x^3+0x^2-7x-6} before you divide.
  • Losing a sign when you subtract in long division. Change every sign of the line you subtract.
  • Stopping after the first factor. A cubic needs three factors, and the quadratic left over needs factorising too.
  • Dropping a repeated root. For {m:(x-1)^2(x+2)}, the solutions are {m:x=1} and {m:x=-2}, and the first is repeated.
  • Taking the signs of the solutions from the brackets. {m:(x+2)=0} gives {m:x=-2}.
  • Thinking a cubic always has three real roots. After you divide, the quadratic may have a negative discriminant.
  • Testing {m:(2x-1)} with {m:x=2}. Solve {m:2x-1=0} and use {m:x=frac(1, 2)}.
  • Forgetting the number in front when you build a polynomial from its roots. The roots fix the factors, not the size. A point on the curve fixes k.
