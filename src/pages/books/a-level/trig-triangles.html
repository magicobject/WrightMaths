Trigonometry in triangles

At GCSE you used sine, cosine and tangent in right-angled triangles. This page works in any triangle. You will use the sine rule and the cosine rule to find a missing side or angle. You will choose the right rule for the information you have. You will find the area of a triangle from two sides and the angle between them. You will meet the case where two different triangles fit the same facts. You will finish with bearings and problems made of more than one triangle.

Where you will use this: in Trigonometric graphs, where the same ratios are defined for any angle. Also in Vectors, where a triangle of vectors gives lengths and angles that you find with these rules.

1. Labelling a triangle, and the sine rule

The rules in this topic work in any triangle, not only a right-angled one. They need an agreed way of naming the parts.

Name the corners with capital letters: A, B and C. Name each side with the small letter of the corner opposite it. So side a is opposite angle A, side b is opposite angle B, and side c is opposite angle C.

Take the side a and the angle A opposite it. Call them a matching pair. The {term:sine-rule} says that the ratio of a side to the sine of its matching angle is the same for all three pairs.

Key idea: in any triangle ABC,

{md:frac(a, sin A)=frac(b, sin B)=frac(c, sin C)}

This is the {term:sine-rule|sine rule}. To find an angle, use the upside-down form {m:frac(sin A, a)=frac(sin B, b)=frac(sin C, c)}. It needs one matching pair, and one other side or angle.

Here is a first example. You know two angles and a side that is not opposite either of them.

Worked example: finding a side

In triangle ABC, {m:A=40deg}, {m:B=65deg} and {m:b=12} cm. Find the side a, to 3 significant figures.

  1. The matching pairs are a with A, and b with B. You know A, B and b, so use both pairs.
  2. {m:frac(a, sin(40deg))=frac(12, sin(65deg))}.
  3. Multiply both sides by {m:sin(40deg)}: {m:a=frac(12 sin(40deg), sin(65deg))}.
  4. {m:a=8.51} cm to 3 significant figures.

Side a is shorter than side b, which fits: {m:40deg} is smaller than {m:65deg}. The larger angle is opposite the longer side.

To find an angle, put the sines on top. Then take the inverse sine, {m:sin^(-1)}, at the end.

Worked example: finding an angle

In triangle ABC, {m:a=12} cm, {m:A=70deg} and {m:b=9} cm. Find angle B, to 1 decimal place.

  1. The matching pairs are a with A, and b with B. You know a, A and b.
  2. Put the sines on top: {m:frac(sin B, 9)=frac(sin(70deg), 12)}.
  3. {m:sin B=frac(9 sin(70deg), 12)=0.7048}.
  4. {m:B=sin^(-1)(0.7048)=44.8deg} to 1 decimal place.

Side b is shorter than side a, so B must be smaller than A. The answer {m:44.8deg} is smaller than {m:70deg}. A sine can also come from an obtuse angle, but here {m:180deg-44.8deg=135.2deg} is too big: with A it would make more than {m:180deg}. So there is one answer.

When you are given two angles, you can find the third. The angles add to {m:180deg}. This lets you make a matching pair.

Worked example: find the third angle first

In triangle ABC, {m:A=38deg}, {m:B=60deg} and {m:a=7} cm. Find side c, to 3 significant figures.

  1. The side c is opposite C. Find C: {m:C=180-38-60=82deg}.
  2. Use the pairs c with C, and a with A: {m:frac(c, sin(82deg))=frac(7, sin(38deg))}.
  3. {m:c=frac(7 sin(82deg), sin(38deg))=11.3} cm to 3 significant figures.

Keep the full calculator value between steps and round only at the end. A rounded value in the middle can change the last digit.

2. The cosine rule

The sine rule needs a matching pair. Sometimes you do not have one. You may know two sides and the angle between them, or all three sides. Then use the {term:cosine-rule}.

The angle between two given sides is the {term:included-angle}.

Key idea: in any triangle ABC,

{md:a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc cos A}

The side on the left, a, is opposite the angle A. The angle A is between the sides b and c. This is the {term:cosine-rule|cosine rule}. To find an angle, rearrange it: {m:cos A=frac(b^2+c^2-a^2, 2bc)}.

If A is a right angle, {m:cos A=0} and the rule becomes Pythagoras’ theorem. The extra term {m:-2bc cos A} corrects it for other angles.

Worked example: finding a side

In triangle ABC, {m:b=7} cm, {m:c=9} cm and {m:A=52deg}. Find side a, to 3 significant figures.

  1. The angle A is between b and c, so use {m:a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc cos A}.
  2. {m:a^2=7^2+9^2-2*7*9*cos(52deg)}.
  3. {m:a^2=49+81-126 cos(52deg)=130-77.58=52.42}.
  4. Take the square root: {m:a=sqrt(52.42)=7.24} cm to 3 significant figures.

Do not forget the square root. The calculation gives {m:a^2}, not a.

To find an angle from three sides, use the rearranged form. A negative answer for the cosine means the angle is obtuse.

Worked example: finding an angle

In triangle ABC, {m:a=7}, {m:b=9} and {m:c=12}. Find angle C, to 1 decimal place.

  1. Angle C is opposite side c, so put {m:c^2} on the left: {m:cos C=frac(a^2+b^2-c^2, 2ab)}.
  2. {m:cos C=frac(49+81-144, 2*7*9)=frac(-14, 126)=-0.1111}.
  3. {m:C=cos^(-1)(-0.1111)=96.4deg} to 1 decimal place.

The cosine is negative, so C is obtuse: more than {m:90deg}. That fits: C is opposite the longest side.

The cosine rule gives exact answers when the numbers are kind. For example, take {m:b=6}, {m:c=10} and {m:A=120deg}. Then {m:cos(120deg)=-frac(1, 2)}, so {m:a^2=36+100-120*(-frac(1, 2))=196} and {m:a=14}.

3. Choosing the right rule

Most of the skill is deciding where to start. Ask what you know about the triangle. The answer tells you the rule.

Key idea: choose by what you are given.

  • A side and its opposite angle, plus one more side or angle. Use the sine rule.
  • Two angles and any side. Find the third angle ({m:180deg} minus the two), then use the sine rule.
  • Two sides and the angle between them. Use the cosine rule to find the third side.
  • All three sides. Use the cosine rule, rearranged, to find an angle.

With two sides and an angle that is not between them, use the sine rule, but read the section on the ambiguous case first.

After you find a missing side with the cosine rule, you have a matching pair. The sine rule is then a quick way to find another angle. Find the smaller of the two remaining angles with the sine rule: a smaller angle is never obtuse, so there is one answer.

Worked example: which rule?

Say which rule you would use first.

  1. {m:A=50deg}, {m:B=60deg}, {m:a=8}: find b. The pairs are a with A and b with B, so use the sine rule.
  2. {m:a=5}, {m:b=6}, {m:C=70deg}: find c. The angle C is between a and b, so use the cosine rule.
  3. {m:a=4}, {m:b=5}, {m:c=7}: find the largest angle. All three sides, so use the cosine rule, rearranged.
  4. {m:A=44deg}, {m:C=71deg}, {m:b=10}: find c. Side b has no matching angle yet. Find B, {m:180-44-71=65deg}, then use the sine rule.

4. The area of a triangle

The area of a triangle is half the base times the perpendicular height. Often you are not given the height. Trigonometry finds it from a side and an angle.

Take the side a as the base. The height from A to this base is {m:b sin C}, because it is the side opposite {m:C} in a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse b. So the area is half the base times the height.

Key idea: the area of triangle ABC is

{md:"Area"=frac(1, 2)ab sin C}

This is the {term:area-of-triangle-sine|area formula}. The angle C must be the {term:included-angle} between the sides a and b. In the same way, {m:frac(1, 2)bc sin A} and {m:frac(1, 2)ac sin B} also give the area.

Worked example: the area from two sides and the angle between them

Triangle ABC has {m:a=11} cm, {m:b=8} cm and {m:C=38deg}. Find its area, to 3 significant figures.

  1. The angle C is between a and b.
  2. {m:"Area"=frac(1, 2)*11*8*sin(38deg)=44 sin(38deg)}.
  3. The area is 27.1 cm² to 3 significant figures.

The formula works for an obtuse angle too. The sine of an obtuse angle is positive, so the area is positive.

Worked example: an obtuse angle

Triangle ABC has {m:a=9} cm, {m:b=6} cm and {m:C=125deg}. Find its area, to 3 significant figures.

  1. {m:"Area"=frac(1, 2)*9*6*sin(125deg)=27 sin(125deg)}.
  2. {m:sin(125deg)=0.8192}, so the area is 22.1 cm².

The sine of an obtuse angle equals the sine of {m:180deg} minus it: {m:sin(125deg)=sin(55deg)}.

You can run the formula backwards. If you know the area and two sides, you can find the angle between them.

Worked example: the angle from the area

Triangle ABC has area 24 cm², {m:a=10} cm and {m:b=7} cm. Angle C is acute. Find C to 1 decimal place.

  1. {m:frac(1, 2)*10*7*sin C=24}, so {m:35 sin C=24}.
  2. {m:sin C=frac(24, 35)=0.6857}.
  3. {m:C=sin^(-1)(0.6857)=43.3deg}.

If the question did not say acute, there would be a second angle, {m:180deg-43.3deg=136.7deg}. Both give the same area.

When you know all three sides but no angle, find an angle with the cosine rule. Then use the area formula.

Worked example: the area from three sides

Triangle ABC has {m:a=8}, {m:b=9} and {m:c=11}. Find its area to 3 significant figures.

  1. Find C with the cosine rule: {m:cos C=frac(64+81-121, 2*8*9)=frac(24, 144)=frac(1, 6)}.
  2. {m:C=cos^(-1)(frac(1, 6))=80.4deg}.
  3. {m:"Area"=frac(1, 2)*8*9*sin(80.4deg)=36 sin(80.4deg)=35.5}.

The area is 35.5 cm².

5. The ambiguous case [OCR]

This section is needed for OCR. It is not needed for the other boards, but it explains an error many students make.

Two sides and the angle between them fix a triangle. Two sides and an angle that is not between them may not. This is the {term:ambiguous-case}.

The reason is that two angles have the same sine: {m:sin theta=sin(180deg-theta)}. For example, {m:sin(30deg)} and {m:sin(150deg)} are both 0.5. Your calculator gives only the smaller one, and you must decide whether the other also fits.

Key idea: you are given the angle A and the sides a and b. The sine rule gives {m:sin B=frac(b sin A, a)}. Then:

  • If {m:sin B} is more than 1, there is no triangle.
  • Otherwise, B is {m:sin^(-1)} of the value, or {m:180deg} minus that angle. Keep the second angle only if {m:A+B} is less than {m:180deg}. Then there are two triangles.
  • If a is at least as long as b, the second angle never fits. There is one triangle.

Worked example: two triangles

Triangle ABC has {m:A=30deg}, {m:a=7} and {m:b=10}. Find the possible values of angle B, and of side c.

  1. {m:sin B=frac(10 sin(30deg), 7)=frac(5, 7)=0.7143}.
  2. {m:B=sin^(-1)(0.7143)=45.6deg}, or {m:B=180-45.6=134.4deg}.
  3. Check both: {m:30+45.6=75.6} and {m:30+134.4=164.4} are both less than 180. Both triangles exist.
  4. For {m:B=45.6deg}: {m:C=104.4deg} and {m:c=frac(7 sin(104.4deg), sin(30deg))=13.6}.
  5. For {m:B=134.4deg}: {m:C=15.6deg} and {m:c=frac(7 sin(15.6deg), sin(30deg))=3.76}.

The two triangles have the same {m:A}, a and b. They are different triangles, with different third sides.

Worked example: no triangle

Triangle ABC has {m:A=40deg}, {m:a=5} and {m:b=9}. Find angle B.

  1. {m:sin B=frac(9 sin(40deg), 5)=1.157}.
  2. A sine is never more than 1. So there is no angle B, and no triangle.

The short side a is too short to reach the base line from C.

Worked example: only one triangle

Triangle ABC has {m:A=55deg}, {m:a=10} and {m:b=7}. Find angle B.

  1. {m:sin B=frac(7 sin(55deg), 10)=0.5734}.
  2. {m:B=sin^(-1)(0.5734)=35.0deg}, or {m:B=180-35.0=145.0deg}.
  3. The second angle fails: {m:55+145=200} is more than 180.

So {m:B=35.0deg}. Here a is longer than b, so the second angle never fits.

One special case sits between. If {m:sin B=1}, then B is exactly {m:90deg}, and the two triangles become one right-angled triangle.

6. Bearings and problems in two dimensions

A {term:bearing} gives a direction as an angle measured clockwise from north. Write it with three digits: east is 090°, south is 180° and 40° is written 040°.

A bearing is measured at the place you start from. The bearing of B from A is measured at A. The bearing of A from B is measured at B. The two are {m:180deg} apart.

Key idea: to find the bearing of A from B, add {m:180deg} to the bearing of B from A if it is less than {m:180deg}. Subtract {m:180deg} if it is {m:180deg} or more. Always draw a north line at each point you start from.

For a journey in two legs, draw the path and a north line at each turning point. Then work out the angle inside the triangle between the two legs. Use the cosine rule and the sine rule as before.

Worked example: a ship in two legs

A ship sails 12 km from P to Q on a bearing of 060°. It then sails 9 km to R on a bearing of 130°. Find PR, to 3 significant figures, and the bearing of R from P to the nearest degree.

  1. The bearing of P from Q is {m:60+180=240deg}. The bearing of R from Q is {m:130deg}. So angle PQR is {m:240-130=110deg}.
  2. Two sides, 12 and 9, and the angle between them: the cosine rule. {m:PR^2=12^2+9^2-2*12*9*cos(110deg)=225+73.88=298.88}.
  3. {m:PR=sqrt(298.88)=17.3} km.
  4. Now use the sine rule to find angle QPR: {m:frac(sin(QPR), 9)=frac(sin(110deg), 17.29)}, so {m:sin(QPR)=0.4892}, and {m:QPR=29.3deg}.
  5. The bearing of R from P is {m:60+29.3=89.3deg}, which is 089° to the nearest degree.

The angle at P is the smaller of the two unknown angles, so the inverse sine gives the right one.

Many problems have two triangles that share a side. Find the shared side in one triangle. Then use it in the other.

Worked example: two triangles

  1. In triangle ABC, the cosine rule: {m:AC^2=6^2+9^2-2*6*9*cos(110deg)=117+36.94=153.94}.
  2. {m:AC=sqrt(153.94)=12.4} m, to 3 significant figures.
  3. In triangle ACD you know all three sides: 8, 7 and AC. The angle D is opposite AC: {m:cos D=frac(8^2+7^2-153.94, 2*8*7)=frac(-40.94, 112)=-0.3655}.
  4. {m:D=cos^(-1)(-0.3655)=111.4deg}, to 1 decimal place.

Flip it: run it backwards

You have found a missing side, and a missing angle. Each is the reverse of the other. You also found an area from sides and an angle. You can find an angle from an area.

Forwards

From the angles and a side to a missing side. Put the sides on top: {m:frac(a, sin A)=frac(b, sin B)}. Multiply to get the side.

Backwards

With the sides 5, 8 and 10: {m:cos C=frac(25+64-100, 80)=-0.1375}, so {m:C=97.9deg}.

Backwards

  2. Angle PQR is {m:255-160=95deg}.
  3. Use the cosine rule with 20, 14 and {m:95deg}.
  • You used 85 degrees for the angle at Q. The angle PQR is {m:255-160=95deg}.

{m:PQR=255-160=95deg}. {m:PR^2=400+196-560 cos(95deg)=644.8}, so {m:PR=25.4} km.

  • B1 for angle {m:PQR=95deg}
  • M1 for the cosine rule with 20, 14 and {m:95deg}
  • A1 for 25.4

(b) [3 marks] Using the same journey (20 km on 075°, then 14 km on 160°), find the bearing of R from P, to the nearest degree. Type only the number.

  1. Use the sine rule in triangle PQR to find angle QPR. {m:PR=25.39}.
  2. Add the angle QPR to 075°.
  • That is the angle QPR. A bearing is measured from north, so add the 75°.

{m:sin(QPR)=frac(14 sin(95deg), 25.39)=0.5493}, so {m:QPR=33.3deg}. The bearing is {m:75+33.3=108.3}, which is 108° to the nearest degree.

  • M1 for {m:frac(sin(QPR), 14)=frac(sin(95deg), PR)}
  • A1 for {m:QPR=33.3deg}
  • A1 for 108

[3 marks] In triangle ABC, {m:a=8.5} cm, {m:b=11.2} cm and {m:C=41deg}. Find the area in cm², to 3 significant figures. Type only the number.

  1. The angle C is between a and b.
  2. {m:frac(1, 2)*8.5*11.2*sin(41deg)}.
  • That is {m:frac(1, 2)*8.5*11.2}. Multiply by {m:sin(41deg)} as well.

{m:"Area"=frac(1, 2)*8.5*11.2*sin(41deg)=47.6 sin(41deg)=31.2} cm².

  • M1 for {m:frac(1, 2)*8.5*11.2*sin(41deg)}
  • A1 for 31.2 or better
  • A1 for 31.2 to 3 significant figures

Why it works

  • The area formula. Take side a as the base. Drop a perpendicular from A. The height is {m:b sin C}, because it is the side opposite {m:C} in a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse b. The area is {m:frac(1, 2)*a*b sin C}. If C is obtuse, the height is {m:b sin(180deg-C)}, which equals {m:b sin C}.
  • The sine rule. The area is {m:frac(1, 2)bc sin A}, {m:frac(1, 2)ac sin B} and {m:frac(1, 2)ab sin C}. They are all the same number. Set the first two equal and divide by {m:frac(1, 2)c}: {m:b sin A=a sin B}, so {m:frac(a, sin A)=frac(b, sin B)}.
  • The cosine rule. Drop the perpendicular of height h from B to side b. It splits b into {m:c cos A} and {m:b-c cos A}, and {m:h=c sin A}. Pythagoras on the right-hand triangle gives {m:a^2=h^2+(b-c cos A)^2=c^2 sin^2 A+b^2-2bc cos A+c^2 cos^2 A}. Since {m:sin^2 A+cos^2 A=1}, this is {m:a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc cos A}.
  • The ambiguous case. A circle of radius a round C can cut the line from A in two points, one point, or none. That is two triangles, one triangle, or none. It is the sine rule that cannot tell the two points apart, because {m:sin B=sin(180deg-B)}.
  • Bearings. North lines at different points are parallel. The bearing from A to B and the bearing from B to A are the two ends of one line, so they differ by {m:180deg}.

Common traps

  • Using a calculator set to radians. Check that it shows DEG.
  • Pairing a side with the wrong angle. A side goes with the angle opposite it.
  • Forgetting the square root at the end of the cosine rule.
  • Using {m:cos A} in the area formula, or using an angle that is not between the two sides.
  • Forgetting the half in {m:frac(1, 2)ab sin C}.
  • Rounding in the middle of a calculation. Keep the full value and round at the end.
  • Giving only one angle from the sine rule when the question could have two, or giving a second angle that does not fit.
  • Reading a bearing from the wrong point. The bearing of A from B is measured at B.
  • Using the cosine rule for an angle with the wrong side on the left. The side opposite the angle goes first.
