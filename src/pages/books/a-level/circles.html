Circles

A circle is every point at one fixed distance from a centre. This page gives you its equation in two forms. You will move between the forms, and find the centre and radius. You will use three facts about circles, and find tangents and normals. You will also find where a line meets a circle, and when two circles meet.

1. The equation of a circle

A {term:circle} is every point that is the same distance from one fixed point. The fixed point is the {term:centre}. The distance is the {term:radius}, written r.

Take a circle with centre {m:(2, 1)}. Let P be any point {m:(x, y)} on the circle. The distance from the centre to P is r. The distance between two points comes from Pythagoras’ theorem, as in Straight lines.

Key idea: the circle with centre {m:(a, b)} and radius r has the equation {m:(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2}. This is the {term:circle-equation|equation of a circle}. The signs in the brackets are the opposite of the signs of the centre. The number on the right is {m:r^2}, not r.

If the centre is the origin {m:(0, 0)}, then {m:a=0} and {m:b=0}. The equation becomes {m:x^2+y^2=r^2}.

Worked example: from a centre and a radius to an equation

Write the equation of the circle with centre {m:(3, -2)} and radius 4.

  1. The centre is {m:(a, b)=(3, -2)}, so {m:a=3} and {m:b=-2}.
  2. Put these in: {m:(x-3)^2+(y-(-2))^2=4^2}.
  3. Subtracting a negative number adds, so {m:y-(-2)=y+2}.
  4. The equation is {m:(x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=16}.

Now go the other way. To read off the centre and the radius, undo each step.

Worked example: from an equation to a centre and a radius

Find the centre and the radius of the circle {m:(x+3)^2+(y-2)^2=16}.

  1. Write {m:x+3} as {m:x-(-3)}. So {m:a=-3}.
  2. The bracket {m:(y-2)} gives {m:b=2}. The centre is {m:(-3, 2)}.
  3. The right side is {m:r^2=16}. So {m:r=sqrt(16)=4}.

The radius is a length, so it is positive. The answer {m:r=-4} is never right.

Often you are given the centre and one point on the circle. The radius is the distance between them. You do not need to take the square root, because the equation needs {m:r^2}.

Worked example: a centre and a point on the circle

A circle has centre {m:(1, 2)} and passes through the point {m:(4, 6)}. Find its equation.

  1. {m:r^2} is the distance squared. The change in x is {m:4-1=3} and the change in y is {m:6-2=4}.
  2. {m:r^2=3^2+4^2=9+16=25}.
  3. The equation is {m:(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2=25}.

A point is on the circle when its distance from the centre is r. So you can test any point. Put its coordinates into the left side and compare the answer with {m:r^2}.

Key idea: for the circle {m:(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2}, put a point into the left side. If you get {m:r^2}, the point is on the circle. If you get less than {m:r^2}, it is inside. If you get more than {m:r^2}, it is outside.

Worked example: on, inside or outside?

The circle is {m:(x-1)^2+(y+2)^2=25}. Where are the points {m:(4, 2)}, {m:(2, -1)} and {m:(5, 3)}?

  1. {m:(4, 2)}: {m:(4-1)^2+(2+2)^2=9+16=25}. This equals {m:r^2}, so the point is on the circle.
  2. {m:(2, -1)}: {m:(2-1)^2+(-1+2)^2=1+1=2}. This is less than 25, so the point is inside.
  3. {m:(5, 3)}: {m:(5-1)^2+(3+2)^2=16+25=41}. This is more than 25, so the point is outside.

A {term:diameter} is a line across a circle through its centre. The centre of the circle is the midpoint of any diameter. So the ends of a diameter give you the centre and the radius.

Worked example: the ends of a diameter

  1. The centre is the midpoint of AB: {m:(frac(-3+5, 2), frac(2+6, 2))=(1, 4)}.
  2. {m:r^2} is the distance squared from the centre to B. The changes are {m:5-1=4} and {m:6-4=2}.
  3. {m:r^2=4^2+2^2=16+4=20}.
  4. The equation is {m:(x-1)^2+(y-4)^2=20}.

The radius is {m:sqrt(20)}, which is not a whole number. That is fine, because the equation uses {m:r^2}.

2. The general form of a circle equation

Multiply out the brackets of a circle equation and move everything to one side. This gives the {term:circle-general-form|general form}. Here is an example.

Worked example: from the brackets to the general form

Write {m:(x-2)^2+(y+1)^2=9} with no brackets, in the form {m:x^2+y^2+ax+by+c=0}.

  1. Expand {m:(x-2)^2=x^2-4x+4} and {m:(y+1)^2=y^2+2y+1}.
  2. The equation is {m:x^2-4x+4+y^2+2y+1=9}.
  3. Subtract 9 from both sides and collect the numbers: {m:x^2+y^2-4x+2y-4=0}.

Key idea: in the general form {m:x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+c=0}, {m:x^2} and {m:y^2} both have coefficient 1. There is no xy term. The letters g, f and c stand for numbers. The centre is {m:(-g, -f)} and the radius squared is {m:r^2=g^2+f^2-c}. Some papers write {m:x^2+y^2+ax+by+c=0} instead. The method below works for both.

Do not rely on remembering the formulas. Complete the square on the x terms and on the y terms, as in Quadratics. This turns the general form back into the brackets form, and it always works.

Worked example: from the general form to the centre and radius

Find the centre and the radius of the circle {m:x^2+y^2-6x+4y-12=0}.

  1. Group the x terms and the y terms. Move the number to the right: {m:(x^2-6x)+(y^2+4y)=12}.
  2. Complete the square on x. Half of {m:-6} is {m:-3}, so {m:x^2-6x=(x-3)^2-9}.
  3. Complete the square on y. Half of 4 is 2, so {m:y^2+4y=(y+2)^2-4}.
  4. Put these in: {m:(x-3)^2-9+(y+2)^2-4=12}. Add 9 and 4 to both sides.
  5. {m:(x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=25}.

The centre is {m:(3, -2)} and the radius is {m:sqrt(25)=5}. Check with the formulas: {m:g=-3}, {m:f=2} and {m:c=-12}. So {m:r^2=9+4+12=25}.

Sometimes {m:x^2} and {m:y^2} have the same coefficient, but it is not 1. Divide every term by that coefficient first.

Worked example: divide first

Find the centre and the radius of the circle {m:2x^2+2y^2+4x-8y-8=0}.

  2. Group and move the number: {m:(x^2+2x)+(y^2-4y)=4}.

  4. {m:(x+1)^2+(y-2)^2=9}.

  • Positive: the equation is a circle, and the radius is its square root.
  • Zero: the equation is a single point, the centre. The radius is 0.
  • Negative: there are no points. No real x and y give a negative number when squared and added.

3. Three facts about circles

Three facts about circles help you find centres, radii and {term:tangent|tangents}. Each one turns into a gradient test. Two lines are perpendicular when their gradients multiply to {m:-1}. You met this in Straight lines.

Fact 1: the angle in a semicircle

A semicircle is half a circle, cut off by a diameter. Take a diameter AB and any other point P on the circle. Draw the lines PA and PB.

Key idea: the {term:angle-in-semicircle|angle in a semicircle} is a right angle. Let AB be a diameter and P be on the circle. Then angle APB is {m:90deg}. It also works backwards. If angle APB is {m:90deg}, then P is on the circle with diameter AB. With coordinates, test it with gradients. The gradient of AP times the gradient of PB is {m:-1}. If AP is vertical, it has no gradient. Then PB is horizontal, and the two lines are still at a right angle.

Worked example: the angle in a semicircle

The circle {m:(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2=25} has a diameter AB, with {m:A(-4, 2)} and {m:B(6, 2)}. The point {m:P(4, 6)} is on the circle. Show that angle APB is {m:90deg}.

  1. The gradient of AP is {m:frac(6-2, 4-(-4))=frac(4, 8)=frac(1, 2)}.
  2. The gradient of PB is {m:frac(2-6, 6-4)=frac(-4, 2)=-2}.
  3. {m:frac(1, 2)*(-2)=-1}, so AP and PB are perpendicular.

So angle APB is {m:90deg}. This agrees with the fact, because AB is a diameter.

Why it works

  • The equation of a circle. A point {m:(x, y)} is on the circle when it is r from the centre {m:(a, b)}. The distance squared is {m:(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2}, by Pythagoras. So the point is on the circle when this equals {m:r^2}.
  • The general form. Multiply out {m:(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2}. The x and y terms are {m:-2ax} and {m:-2by}, and the number is {m:a^2+b^2-r^2}. Completing the square reverses the multiplying out.
  • The angle in a semicircle. Join P to the centre C. The triangles APC and BPC each have two equal sides, which are radii. In an isosceles triangle the two base angles are equal. Call them a at A and b at B. The angles of triangle APB add to {m:180deg}, so {m:a+b+(a+b)=180deg}. So {m:a+b=90deg}, and that is the angle at P.
  • A line from the centre to a chord. Triangles CAM and CBM share the side CM. Their sides CA and CB are radii, so they are equal. Each has a right angle at M. So they are the same shape and size, and AM equals MB.
  • The tangent and the radius. Suppose the tangent at P is not at a right angle to CP. Then some point on the line is closer to C than P is. That point is inside the circle. So the line enters the circle and meets it again. A tangent meets the circle once. So it must be at a right angle to CP.
  • A line and a circle. The solutions of the quadratic are the x-coordinates of the common points. The discriminant counts the solutions: two, one repeated, or none.
  • The circle through three points. The centre is as far from A as from B. So it is on the perpendicular bisector of AB. The same holds for BC. The two bisectors cross at only one point, so there is one circle.
  • Two circles. A common point P and the two centres make a triangle. Its sides are r, s and d. In a triangle, each side is less than the sum of the other two. It is more than their difference. If d equals the sum or the difference, the triangle is flat. Then P is on the line of centres.

Common traps

  • Copying the signs from the brackets. The centre of {m:(x+3)^2+(y-2)^2=16} is {m:(-3, 2)}.
  • Giving {m:r^2} as the radius. Take the square root of the number on the right.
  • Forgetting to divide through when {m:x^2} and {m:y^2} have a coefficient other than 1.
  • Not checking that the right side is positive after completing the square.
  • Using the gradient of the radius as the gradient of the tangent. The tangent needs the negative reciprocal.
  • Forgetting that the normal goes through the centre. It has the gradient of the radius.
  • Dividing by x to solve {m:2x^2+6x=0}. This loses the solution {m:x=0}. Factorise instead.
  • Finding y from the circle equation instead of the line. This gives extra points that are not on the line.
  • Using the gradient of the side itself for the perpendicular bisector. Use its negative reciprocal.
  • Mixing up the sum and the difference of the radii when two circles touch.
