Straight lines

A straight line has the same steepness everywhere. This page gives you three ways to write its equation. You will test for parallel and perpendicular lines. You will also find the length and the middle of a line segment. A line segment is a piece of a line between two points.

Where you will use this: in Circles, where the radius is a distance. A tangent there is a line at right angles to it. Also in Differentiation, where a tangent and a normal are straight lines. It helps whenever one thing changes at a steady rate. A taxi fare and a leaking tank are examples.

1. The gradient and the equation y = mx + c

The {term:gradient} of a line measures how steep it is. It is the change in y divided by the change in x, between any two points on the line. Many people call these two changes “rise” (how far up) and “run” (how far across).

Key idea: the gradient of the line through {m:(x_1, y_1)} and {m:(x_2, y_2)} is {m:m=frac(y_2-y_1, x_2-x_1)}. Read {m:x_1} as “x one”. The small numbers only label the two points. Use the same order on the top and the bottom.

The sign of the gradient tells you the direction. A line that goes up to the right has a positive gradient. A line that goes down to the right has a negative gradient. A horizontal line has gradient 0.

Worked example

Find the gradient of the line through {m:(-2, 5)} and {m:(4, -1)}.

  1. Call the first point {m:(x_1, y_1)=(-2, 5)} and the second {m:(x_2, y_2)=(4, -1)}.
  2. The change in y is {m:-1-5=-6}.
  3. The change in x is {m:4-(-2)=6}. Subtracting a negative number adds.
  4. {m:m=frac(-6, 6)=-1}.

The gradient is negative, so the line goes down to the right. It goes down 1 for every 1 across.

A straight line has an equation. The line with gradient m that crosses the y-axis at the point {m:(0, c)} has the equation {m:y=mx+c}. The point {m:(0, c)} is the {term:y-intercept}. This is the form {term:equation-of-a-line|y = mx + c}.

Key idea: in {m:y=mx+c}, the number in front of x is the gradient. The number on its own is where the line crosses the y-axis. Put y on its own on one side before you read off m and c.

Worked example: read off m and c

Find the gradient and the y-intercept of the line {m:2y+6x=10}.

  1. Get y on its own. Subtract {m:6x} from both sides: {m:2y=-6x+10}.
  2. Divide every term by 2: {m:y=-3x+5}.
  3. The gradient is {m:m=-3}. The y-intercept is {m:(0, 5)}.

The gradient is not 6. The 6 was only the number before you divided by 2.

Two kinds of line do not fit {m:y=mx+c}. A horizontal line has the equation {m:y=k}, where k is a number, and its gradient is 0. A vertical line has the equation {m:x=k}. A vertical line has no gradient. Its change in x is 0, and you cannot divide by 0.

A point is on a line when its coordinates fit the equation. To test a point, put its x-value into the equation and see whether you get its y-value.

Worked example: is the point on the line?

Is the point {m:(3, 7)} on the line {m:y=2x+1}? Is the point {m:(2, 6)}?

  1. For {m:(3, 7)}: put in {m:x=3}. {m:2*3+1=7}, which equals the y-value. So the point is on the line.
  2. For {m:(2, 6)}: put in {m:x=2}. {m:2*2+1=5}, which is not 6. So the point is not on the line.

2. The equation of a line through a point

Often you do not know where the line crosses the y-axis. You know its gradient and one point on it instead. Then it is easier to use the {term:point-gradient-form|point-gradient form}.

Let {m:(x_1, y_1)} be the point you know. Let {m:(x, y)} be any other point on the line. The gradient between them is m, so {m:frac(y-y_1, x-x_1)=m}. Multiply both sides by {m:x-x_1} to get the key idea below.

Key idea: the line with gradient m through the point {m:(x_1, y_1)} has the equation {m:y-y_1=m(x-x_1)}. Subtract the coordinates of the point from x and y. Then expand the bracket and rearrange if you want {m:y=mx+c}.

Worked example

Find the equation of the line with gradient 3 through {m:(2, 5)}. Give it in the form {m:y=mx+c}.

  1. Put {m:m=3}, {m:x_1=2} and {m:y_1=5} into the formula: {m:y-5=3(x-2)}.
  2. Expand the bracket: {m:y-5=3x-6}.
  3. Add 5 to both sides: {m:y=3x-1}.
  4. Check the point: when {m:x=2}, {m:y=3*2-1=5}. This matches.

Worked example: a negative coordinate

Find the equation of the line with gradient {m:-2} through {m:(-1, 4)}.

  1. The point has {m:x_1=-1}, so {m:x-x_1=x-(-1)=x+1}. The formula gives {m:y-4=-2(x+1)}.
  2. Expand the bracket: {m:y-4=-2x-2}.
  3. Add 4 to both sides: {m:y=-2x+2}.
  4. Check the point: when {m:x=-1}, {m:y=-2*(-1)+2=4}. This matches.

When you are given two points, first find the gradient. Then use either point in the formula. Both points give the same line.

Worked example: through two points

Find the equation of the line through {m:A(1, 5)} and {m:B(3, 9)}.

  1. Find the gradient: {m:m=frac(9-5, 3-1)=frac(4, 2)=2}.
  2. Use point A in the formula: {m:y-5=2(x-1)}.
  3. Expand: {m:y-5=2x-2}, so {m:y=2x+3}.
  4. Check with point B, which you did not use: {m:2*3+3=9}. This matches.

Using B instead gives {m:y-9=2(x-3)}, which also simplifies to {m:y=2x+3}.

3. The general form ax + by + c = 0

Exam questions often say “give your answer in the form {m:ax+by+c=0}, where a, b and c are integers”. An integer is a whole number. It can be positive, negative or zero. This is the {term:general-form-of-a-line|general form} of a line. Everything is on one side and the other side is 0.

Follow these steps to reach the general form.

  1. If there are fractions, multiply every term by a number that clears them.
  2. Move every term to one side, so that the other side is 0.
  3. If you can, make the number in front of x positive.

If a, b and c all divide by the same number, divide through by it. Then they have no common factor.

Worked example

Write {m:y=frac(3, 4)x-2} in the form {m:ax+by+c=0}, where a, b and c are integers.

  1. Multiply every term by 4 to clear the fraction: {m:4y=3x-8}.
  2. Move every term to the right side, so that the left side is 0: {m:0=3x-4y-8}.
  3. The answer is {m:3x-4y-8=0}.

Check with a point on the original line. When {m:x=4}, {m:y=3-2=1}. Then {m:12-4-8=0}.

Worked example: through two points

You can also find where the line meets each axis. Put {m:y=0} to find the {term:x-intercept}. Put {m:x=0} to find the y-intercept.

4. Parallel and perpendicular lines

{term:parallel-lines|Parallel lines} never meet. They are equally steep, so they have the same gradient.

Two lines are {term:perpendicular} when they meet at a right angle (90 degrees). Their gradients are linked. Take the gradient of one line, turn it upside down and change its sign. The result is the {term:negative-reciprocal|negative reciprocal}. It is the gradient of a perpendicular line.

Key idea: two lines are parallel when their gradients are equal: {m:m_1=m_2}. Read {m:m_1} as “m one” and {m:m_2} as “m two”. Two lines that are not horizontal or vertical are perpendicular when {m:m_1*m_2=-1}. That means {m:m_2=-frac(1, m_1)}. Always put each equation in the form {m:y=mx+c} before you compare gradients.

5. Distance, midpoint and perpendicular bisector

A {term:line-segment|line segment} is the part of a line between two end points. It has a length and a middle.

The {term:midpoint} of a line segment is the point exactly halfway along it. To find it, take the average of the two x-coordinates and the average of the two y-coordinates.

Key idea: the midpoint of {m:(x_1, y_1)} and {m:(x_2, y_2)} is {m:(frac(x_1+x_2, 2), frac(y_1+y_2, 2))}. Add the coordinates, then halve. Do not subtract.

The {term:distance|distance} between two points is the length of the line segment that joins them. Draw a right-angled triangle on the segment. The two short sides are the change in x and the change in y. Pythagoras’ theorem gives the longest side.

6. Modelling with straight lines

Many real situations change at a steady rate. A taxi fare rises by the same amount for each kilometre. A tank leaks the same volume each minute. A straight-line equation that describes such a situation is a {term:linear-model}.

In a linear model, the gradient is the rate of change. Its units are the units of y per unit of x, such as pounds per hour. The y-intercept is the starting value, when x is 0.

Key idea: to build a linear model, find the gradient (the rate) and a starting value. Write the equation with letters that fit the situation. Then say what each number means, with its units, and say what you have assumed.

Worked example

  3. The cost of 2.5 hours is 115 pounds.

The 40 is the call-out fee, in pounds. The 30 is the cost of each hour, in pounds per hour. The model assumes that the hourly rate stays the same. It only makes sense when t is greater than or equal to 0. In symbols, that is {m:t>=0}.

Why it works

  • The gradient. A straight line has the same steepness everywhere. So any two points on it give the same {m:frac(y_2-y_1, x_2-x_1)}.
  • The point-gradient form. Take any point {m:(x, y)} on the line. The gradient from {m:(x_1, y_1)} to it is m, so {m:frac(y-y_1, x-x_1)=m}. Multiply both sides by {m:x-x_1}.
  • Perpendicular gradients. A line with gradient m goes across 1 and up m. Turn that step through a quarter turn. It becomes across {m:-m} and up 1. Its gradient is {m:frac(1, -m)=-frac(1, m)}.
  • The distance. The change in x and the change in y are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle. The distance is the longest side, so Pythagoras gives it.
  • The midpoint. Halfway between two numbers is their average. The x and y directions are independent, so you average each one on its own.
  • The perpendicular bisector. Every point on it is the same distance from A as from B. That is why it must pass through the midpoint and meet the segment at a right angle.
  • Linear models. A straight line has a constant gradient. So a linear model has a constant rate of change. That is the assumption you are making.

Common traps

  • Putting the change in x on top of the gradient. The change in y goes on top.
  • Reading the gradient from an equation that is not in the form {m:y=mx+c}. Rearrange first.
  • Mixing the order of the points. Use the same order on the top and the bottom.
  • Forgetting the double minus. {m:3-(-1)=4}, not 2.
  • Writing {m:(x+1)} where the point has {m:x_1=1}. Subtract the point: {m:(x-1)}.
  • In {m:ax+by+c=0}, reading c as the y-intercept. It is {m:-frac(c, b)}.
  • Turning the gradient upside down but keeping the sign, or changing the sign but not turning it upside down.
  • Using the midpoint formula with a minus instead of a plus.
  • Stopping at {m:d^2} and forgetting the square root in the distance formula.
  • Giving a model's answer with no units, or outside the range where the model makes sense.
