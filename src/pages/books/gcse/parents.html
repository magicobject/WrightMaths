What this is

A free online GCSE maths book, written by Greg Wright, a maths tutor with a current Enhanced DBS check (see the About page). It starts at the base of the subject and builds upwards, so a gap from earlier years can be found and mended. Anyone can use it. There is nothing to sign up for.

While it is a preview it is free, and nothing is sold through the book. The tuition on the rest of the site is separate. Your child never needs it to use the book.

Is it safe?

  • No accounts, no chat, no adverts. Nobody can message your child through the book.
  • What is kept, and where. Which questions were finished, the dates of practice and of each check, the results of “Find my gaps” (which topics to work on), the marks they give themselves on mark schemes, and reading settings such as text size, voice and speed are kept in this browser, on this device. A half-finished “Find my gaps” is kept only until the browser tab is closed. None of this is sent to us or anyone else. Anyone who uses the same browser on the same device can see them. All of it is cleared by the “Clear my ticks on this device” button on the book’s front page, or by clearing the browser’s site data.
  • The “duck” box. What your child types to the duck stays on the screen. It is not saved and not sent.
  • Other websites. The book’s pages do not load fonts, scripts, trackers or videos from other websites. Like any website, the host keeps ordinary server records (such as the address a page was requested from) when a page is fetched. The book has no server collecting your child’s answers.
  • Listen. The Listen buttons read the page aloud using a voice built into your device. Some browsers also offer “online” voices that send the text to the browser maker. The book only uses one of those if your child picks it, and it is labelled. If a device has no voice of its own, Listen stays silent.
  • The search box. The box at the top of every page of the book finds topics, maths words, sections and these guides (for example, type “circle theorems”). It works on the device: the book’s list of titles is fetched from this site the first time the box is used, and what your child types is never saved, sent or kept in the address. It does not search the answers.
  • Links out. Apart from the site designer’s credit in the page footer, links that leave the book go to other pages of this site, or open your phone or email app.

Where to start

  1. Do Find my gaps together, once. It is eleven questions and takes about ten minutes. There is no pass mark. A gap is simply a place to start.
  2. Pick the topic it suggests from the lowest layer of the pyramid. About 20 minutes, three times a week, is plenty. Stop when your child is tired.
  3. Add Give me five for two or three minutes on other days. Mixed questions are the best practice for the real exam, because in the exam nobody says which topic a question is from.
  4. After about a month, do Find my gaps again to see what has moved.

When your child is stuck, say this

“What is it asking? What do you know? Tell me out loud.”

Explaining a problem to someone else, even a rubber duck, often shows the next step. That is why the book has a “Say it out loud” step. Avoid “it’s easy”. Let your child use one clue at a time, and try not to give the answer. Getting help is never a failure.

How to tell it is working

  • Your child needs fewer clues on the same kind of question.
  • Older topics near the bottom of the pyramid get their ticks.
  • The second Find my gaps shows more topics as secure.

Your own refresher

Methods have changed a little since you were at school. If you want to check your child’s working, these pages show the method used now:

  • The four operations: column methods, and what some people call “borrowing” is called exchanging.
  • Negative numbers
  • Fractions, with pictures of the pieces.

Maths words have a plain explanation, with examples, in the glossary. Hover over or tap any highlighted word in a question to see it.

Exam boards, tiers and grades

AQA, Edexcel and OCR all follow the same national GCSE maths content. This book does not cover all of it yet (see the front page for what is still to come), so use it alongside what the school gives you. There are two tiers: Foundation (grades 1 to 5) and Higher (grades 4 to 9). Ask the school which tier your child is entered for. Grade 4 is the standard pass and grade 5 a strong pass. Many colleges and apprenticeships ask for at least a grade 4 in maths, so it is worth finding out early what your child’s plan needs. The page on raising your grade says what changes at each step.
