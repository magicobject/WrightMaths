Trigonometry

In a right-angled triangle, the angles and the sides are tied together. Trigonometry gives you the link, so you can find a missing side from an angle, or a missing angle from two sides.

Where you will use this: roof pitch and ramp angles, setting the angle of a ladder, and working out heights you cannot measure directly, such as a tree, a mast or a cliff.

1. Naming the sides

Trigonometry (“trig”) works only in right-angled triangles. Pick one of the other two angles. Then name each side by where it sits compared with that angle.

  • The {term:hypotenuse} is the longest side. It is always opposite the right angle.
  • The {term:opposite} side is across from the angle you picked. It does not touch the angle.
  • The {term:adjacent} side is next to the angle you picked. It is not the hypotenuse.

Key idea: the names depend on the angle you pick. The hypotenuse never changes. But opposite and adjacent swap if you pick the other angle.

The right angle is at B, so the side opposite it is AC. The hypotenuse is AC.

  1. Opposite means across from the angle, not touching it.
  2. The angle is at C. Which side does not have C at either end?
  • BC touches C, so it is next to x. That makes it the adjacent side.
  • AC is the hypotenuse. Opposite means across from the angle, not the longest side.

x is at C. The side that does not touch C is AB, so AB is opposite x.

  1. Adjacent means next to the angle.
  2. Two sides touch C. One of them is the hypotenuse. Which is left?
  • AC is the hypotenuse. Adjacent is the other side that touches the angle.
  • AB is opposite x. Adjacent is the side that touches C.

Two sides touch C: AC and BC. AC is the hypotenuse, so the adjacent side is BC.

  1. The angle is now at A. Which side is across from A?
  2. The opposite side does not touch A.
  • AB touches A, so it is next to y. It is the adjacent side.
  • AC is the hypotenuse. It touches A. Opposite is across from the angle.

y is at A. The side that does not touch A is BC, so BC is opposite y. (AB is adjacent to y. Last time AB was opposite: the names change with the angle.)

A ladder leans against a wall. The angle x is between the ladder and the ground. Which side is opposite x?

2. Sine, cosine and tangent

Draw a right-angled triangle with an angle of 35°. Now draw a bigger one with the same angle. The sides are longer, but the triangles are the same shape. The ratios of their sides are the same.

So the ratio of one side to another depends only on the angle. Each useful ratio has a name:

  • {term:sine|sine (sin)}: opposite ÷ hypotenuse
  • {term:cosine|cosine (cos)}: adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
  • {term:tan-ratio|tan}: opposite ÷ adjacent

{term:soh-cah-toa|SOH CAH TOA}: Sin = Opp / Hyp   Cos = Adj / Hyp   Tan = Opp / Adj

Key idea: the ratio is fixed by the angle. Your calculator knows these ratios, so sin 30° is always 0.5, however big the triangle is.

Your calculator must be in degrees

Calculators can measure angles in different units. For GCSE you need degrees. Look for a small D or DEG on the screen. To check, work out sin 30. You should get 0.5. If you do not, change the angle setting.

Worked example: the ratios in a triangle

In this triangle, find sin x, cos x and tan x.

  1. The sides are: opposite = 3, adjacent = 4, hypotenuse = 5.
  2. sin x = opp ÷ hyp = {frac:3/5}.
  3. cos x = adj ÷ hyp = {frac:4/5}.
  4. tan x = opp ÷ adj = {frac:3/4}.

3. Finding a side

Follow the same four steps every time.

  1. Label the sides: hypotenuse, opposite and adjacent, from the angle you are given.
  2. Choose sin, cos or tan. Pick the one that uses the side you know and the side you want. (SOH CAH TOA.)
  3. Write the equation, for example sin 35° = {frac:x/12}.
  4. Rearrange and use your calculator.

Worked example: the unknown is on top

Find x. Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

  1. The angle is 35°. The side x is opposite it. The 12 cm side is the hypotenuse.
  2. Opposite and hypotenuse: use sin. sin 35° = {frac:x/12}.
  3. Multiply both sides by 12: x = 12 × sin 35°.
  4. x = 12 × 0.5736… = 6.88…, which is 6.9 cm to 1 decimal place.

Sense check: the opposite side must be shorter than the hypotenuse (12). 6.9 is. ✓

Worked example: the unknown is on the bottom

Find h. Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

  1. The angle is 40°. The 8 m side is adjacent to it. The side h is the hypotenuse.
  2. Adjacent and hypotenuse: use cos. cos 40° = {frac:8/h}.
  3. h is on the bottom. Swap h and cos 40°: h = 8 ÷ cos 40°.
  4. h = 8 ÷ 0.7660… = 10.44…, which is 10.4 m to 1 decimal place.

Sense check: the hypotenuse must be the longest side. 10.4 is longer than 8. ✓

4. Finding an angle

Sometimes you know two sides and need the angle. Use the same steps, but at the end you need to undo sin, cos or tan. The undo buttons are written {term:inverse-trig|inverse sin, inverse cos and inverse tan}. On most calculators you press SHIFT or 2nd first, then sin, cos or tan.

Key idea: if sin x = 0.5, then x = sin−1(0.5) = 30°. The inverse button takes a ratio and gives back the angle.

Worked example

Find the angle x to 1 decimal place.

  1. The 5 cm side is opposite x. The 8 cm side is the hypotenuse.
  2. Opposite and hypotenuse: use sin. sin x = {frac:5/8}.
  3. Use the inverse: x = sin−1({frac:5/8}).
  4. x = 38.68…°, which is 38.7° to 1 decimal place.

Sense check: an angle in a right-angled triangle is between 0° and 90°. ✓

Now you finish it

Find the angle x to 1 decimal place. The opposite side is 7 m and the adjacent side is 10 m.

  1. Opposite and adjacent: use tan. tan x = {frac:7/10} = 0.7.
  2. x = tan−1(0.7).
  3. x = ___°.
Check my answer

tan−1(0.7) = 34.99…°, which is 35.0° to 1 decimal place.

Sin and cos of an angle in a right-angled triangle are always between 0 and 1, because the hypotenuse is the longest side. If you work out a sin or cos bigger than 1, check the sides again.

5. Angles of elevation and depression

Real problems often give an angle measured from the horizontal.

  • An {term:angle-of-elevation|angle of elevation} is measured up from the horizontal, when you look up at something.
  • An {term:angle-of-depression|angle of depression} is measured down from the horizontal, when you look down at something.

Key idea: draw the horizontal line first. Then make a right-angled triangle that uses it. The angle of depression from A down to B is the same size as the angle of elevation from B up to A (they are alternate angles).

Worked example: elevation

Priya stands 30 m from the base of a tower. The angle of elevation of the top is 38°. How tall is the tower, to 1 decimal place?

  1. Draw the triangle: the tower is the vertical side (opposite the angle). The ground is 30 m (adjacent).
  2. Opposite and adjacent: use tan. tan 38° = {frac:h/30}.
  3. h = 30 × tan 38° = 30 × 0.7812… = 23.4 m.

Worked example: depression

From the top of a 40 m cliff, the angle of depression of a boat is 25°. How far is the boat from the foot of the cliff, to 1 decimal place?

  1. The angle of depression is measured down from the horizontal at the top. By alternate angles, the angle at the boat is also 25°.
  2. The cliff (40 m) is opposite the 25° angle. The distance d is adjacent.
  3. tan 25° = {frac:40/d}, so d = 40 ÷ tan 25° = 85.8 m.

Flip it: run it backwards

Everything so far goes one way each time. Now reverse it: from angle to side, and from sides back to the angle.

Finding a side

angle + one side → another side

Use sin, cos or tan on the angle, then multiply or divide.

Finding an angle

two sides → the angle

Make the ratio of the two sides, then use sin−1, cos−1 or tan−1.

Key idea: sin and sin−1 undo each other, in the same way that × and ÷ do. sin−1(sin 40°) = 40°. The same is true for cos and tan.

Exact values of sin, cos and tan Higher

For 30°, 45° and 60° you can give {term:exact-trig-values|exact values} with no calculator. They come from two simple triangles.

Cut an equilateral triangle of side 2 in half. You get a right-angled triangle with sides 1, √3 and 2, and angles 30° and 60°. (The height is √(2² − 1²) = √3 by Pythagoras.) Cut a square of side 1 along its diagonal. You get a right-angled triangle with sides 1, 1 and √2, and two angles of 45°.

Read off the ratios with SOH CAH TOA. For example sin 30° = {frac:1/2}, cos 30° = {frac:√3/2} and tan 30° = {frac:1/√3}.

  • 0°: sin = 0, cos = 1, tan = 0
  • 30°: sin = {frac:1/2}, cos = {frac:√3/2}, tan = {frac:1/√3}
  • 45°: sin = {frac:√2/2}, cos = {frac:√2/2}, tan = 1
  • 60°: sin = {frac:√3/2}, cos = {frac:1/2}, tan = √3
  • 90°: sin = 1, cos = 0, tan is not defined

Look for the pattern: sin 30° = cos 60°, and sin 60° = cos 30°. In a right-angled triangle the sine of one angle is the cosine of the other.

Why it works

  • Same angle, same ratios. Two right-angled triangles with the same angles are enlargements of each other. All the lengths are multiplied by the same scale factor, so sides divided by sides stay the same.
  • Sin and cos are never more than 1. The hypotenuse is the longest side, so opposite ÷ hypotenuse and adjacent ÷ hypotenuse are less than 1.
  • The inverse buttons. The calculator can go from an angle to a ratio (sin). The inverse button goes the other way, from a ratio back to an angle.
  • Pythagoras and trig work together. Pythagoras links the three sides. Trig links the sides to the angles. With a right angle and one more piece of information you can find everything.

Common traps

  • Calculator in the wrong mode (radians instead of degrees).
  • Naming opposite and adjacent from the wrong angle, or from the right angle.
  • Multiplying when the unknown is on the bottom of the fraction (you must divide).
  • Forgetting the inverse button when finding an angle.
  • Rounding too early. Keep the full calculator value until the end.
  • Using trig on a triangle with no right angle in it.
