1. What a fraction is

A {term:fraction} describes equal parts of a whole. The bottom number is the {term:denominator}. It says how many equal parts the whole is cut into. The top number is the {term:numerator}. It says how many parts you have.

{frac:3/8} = 3 parts out of 8 equal parts

Key idea: the denominator names the size of the parts (eighths, quarters, tenths). The numerator counts them. Bigger denominator means smaller pieces.

Try it: write {frac:1/2} as quarters.

A fraction line also means divide. {frac:3/4} is the same as 3 ÷ 4. So 1 ÷ 8 = {frac:1/8}.

Equivalent fractions

{term:equivalent-fractions|Equivalent fractions} look different but are worth the same: {frac:1/2} = {frac:2/4} = {frac:3/6}. To make one, multiply (or divide) the top and bottom by the same number.

Worked example

Fill the gap: {frac:2/3} = {frac:?/12}

  1. Look at the bottoms: 3 became 12, so it was multiplied by 4.
  2. Do the same to the top: 2 × 4 = 8.
  3. So {frac:2/3} = {frac:8/12}.

To {term:simplify-fraction|simplify} a fraction, go the other way. Divide top and bottom by the same number. Stop when nothing else divides both. Dividing by the {term:hcf|HCF} does it in one step.

Worked example

Simplify {frac:12/18}.

  1. HCF of 12 and 18 is 6.
  2. 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  3. {frac:12/18} = {frac:2/3}. Nothing else divides both 2 and 3, so it is fully simplified.

2. Adding and subtracting

You can only add or subtract parts of the same size. That means the same denominator. If they are different, make a {term:common-denominator} first. The neatest one is the {term:lcm|lowest common multiple} of the denominators.

Key idea: change the pieces to the same size, then add the counts. The size (denominator) never gets added.

Try it: work out {frac:1/5} + {frac:2/5}.

Worked example

Work out {frac:2/3} + {frac:1/4}.

  1. LCM of 3 and 4 is 12, so use twelfths.
  2. {frac:2/3} = {frac:8/12} and {frac:1/4} = {frac:3/12}.
  3. Add the numerators: 8 + 3 = 11. The denominator stays 12.
  4. {frac:2/3} + {frac:1/4} = {frac:11/12}.

Now you finish it

Work out {frac:3/5} + {frac:1/2}.

  1. LCM of 5 and 2 is 10, so use tenths.
  2. {frac:3/5} = {frac:6/10} and {frac:1/2} = {frac:5/10}.
  3. Add the tops: 6 + 5 = ___.
  4. So the answer is ___ over 10.

Answer: 6 + 5 = 11, so {frac:11/10} = 1 {frac:1/10}.

An answer bigger than 1 is fine. {frac:11/10} is an {term:improper-fraction}. 1 {frac:1/10} is a {term:mixed-number}. They are the same number.

Why not just add tops and bottoms?

Two halves make a whole, so {frac:1/2} + {frac:1/2} = 1.

Adding tops and bottoms gives {frac:2/4}, which is only a half.

The denominator is the size of the pieces. Putting two halves together does not make the pieces smaller.

3. Multiplying and dividing

To multiply, multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms.

To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply. The flipped fraction is the {term:reciprocal}.

Key idea: dividing by a fraction asks: how many of these fit in? That is the same as multiplying by the reciprocal.

Try it: work out {frac:1/2} × {frac:1/2}.

Worked example: multiply

Work out {frac:2/3} × {frac:3/5}.

  1. Tops: 2 × 3 = 6. Bottoms: 3 × 5 = 15.
  2. {frac:6/15}, which simplifies (divide by 3) to {frac:2/5}.

Worked example: divide

Work out {frac:3/4} ÷ {frac:1/2}.

  1. Flip the second fraction: {frac:1/2} becomes {frac:2/1}.
  2. Multiply: {frac:3/4} × {frac:2/1} = {frac:6/4}.
  3. Simplify: {frac:6/4} = {frac:3/2} = 1 {frac:1/2}.

Sense check: how many halves fit into three quarters? One and a half. ✓

With a {term:mixed-number}, convert to an {term:improper-fraction} first: 1 {frac:1/2} = {frac:3/2}.

Work out {frac:3/5} of 40.

  1. Find one fifth first: 40 ÷ 5.
  2. Then take three of them.
  • That is one fifth. You need three of them.

40 ÷ 5 = 8, so {frac:3/5} of 40 = 3 × 8 = 24.

Flip it: run it backwards

Everything so far goes forwards. Now reverse it. Same idea, other direction.

Forwards

{frac:3/5} of 40 = ?

Divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.

Backwards

{frac:3/5} of ? = 24

Divide by the top, multiply by the bottom.

Key idea: “{frac:3/5} of a number is 24” means three fifths equal 24. So one fifth is 24 ÷ 3 = 8. The whole is five fifths: 8 × 5 = 40. Use the two operations in the opposite order.

Another reverse: “What must I add to {frac:2/5} to make 1?” is the same as 1 − {frac:2/5}.

Why it works

  • Common denominators are about piece size. You can only count pieces of the same size. 3 apples and 2 oranges do not make 5 apples.
  • Flipping to divide. Dividing by {frac:1/2} asks how many halves fit. Each whole holds two halves, so you multiply by 2.
  • Equivalent fractions are the same number. Multiplying top and bottom by 4 is multiplying by {frac:4/4} = 1, which changes nothing.

Common traps

  • Adding the denominators.
  • Adding the same number to the top and bottom to make an equivalent fraction.
  • Multiplying by the second fraction when you should flip it for a division.
  • Forgetting to convert a mixed number before multiplying or dividing.
  • Leaving an answer unsimplified.

Going further

You know {frac:1/2} + {frac:1/3} = {frac:5/6}. Without starting a fresh calculation, what is {frac:5/6} − {frac:1/2}?

{frac:5/6} − {frac:1/2} = {frac:1/3}. Subtracting undoes adding. You can check it the other way: {frac:1/3} + {frac:1/2} = {frac:5/6}.
