A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices. You eat 3 and a friend eats 2. What fraction of the pizza has gone?

Work out 7 × 8.

  1. 5, 6, 7, 8: "56 = 7 × 8". Say it out loud.

What is the {term:hcf}, the highest common factor, of 12 and 18?

  1. List the {term:factor|factors} of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Now list those of 18.
  2. Which is the biggest number in both lists?
  • 36 is a multiple of both 12 and 18, not a factor of both. You need the biggest factor they share.

Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. The biggest in both lists is 6.

What is the {term:lcm}, the lowest common multiple, of 4 and 6?

  1. List {term:multiple|multiples} of 4: 4, 8, 12 … and of 6: 6, 12 …
  2. What is the first number in both lists?
  • 24 is a common multiple, but not the lowest one. Look for the first in both lists.
  • 2 is the HCF (a factor). The LCM is a multiple, so it is at least as big as both numbers.

Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. Multiples of 6: 6, 12. First common: 12.

1. What a fraction is

A {term:fraction} describes equal parts of a whole. The bottom number, the {term:denominator}, says how many equal parts the whole is cut into. The top number, the {term:numerator}, says how many of those parts you have.

{frac:3/8} = 3 parts out of 8 equal parts

Key idea: the denominator names the size of the parts (eighths, quarters, tenths). The numerator counts them. Bigger denominator means smaller pieces.

Equivalent fractions

{term:equivalent-fractions|Equivalent fractions} look different but are worth the same: {frac:1/2} = {frac:2/4} = {frac:3/6}. You make one by multiplying (or dividing) the top and bottom by the same number.

Worked example

Fill the gap: {frac:2/3} = {frac:?/12}

  1. Look at the bottoms: 3 became 12, so it was multiplied by 4.
  2. Do the same to the top: 2 × 4 = 8.
  3. So {frac:2/3} = {frac:8/12}.

To {term:simplify-fraction|simplify} a fraction, go the other way: divide top and bottom by the same number until nothing else divides both. Dividing by the {term:hcf|HCF} does it in one step.

Worked example

Simplify {frac:12/18}.

  1. HCF of 12 and 18 is 6.
  2. 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  3. {frac:12/18} = {frac:2/3}. Nothing else divides both 2 and 3, so it is fully simplified.

Check your knowledge: parts and equivalents

What is the {term:denominator} of {frac:5/8}?

  1. It is the number on the bottom.
  • 5 is the numerator (the top number). The denominator is on the bottom.

The denominator is 8: the whole is cut into eighths.

Fill the gap: {frac:2/3} = {frac:?/12}. What number goes where the ? is?

  1. What did you multiply 3 by to get 12?
  2. Do the same to the 2.
  • That comes from adding 9 to the top and 9 to the bottom. Adding the same number changes the value. Multiply top and bottom by the same number instead.

3 × 4 = 12 so 2 × 4 = 8. {frac:2/3} = {frac:8/12}.

Simplify {frac:12/18} fully. Give your answer as a fraction.

  1. Which number divides into both 12 and 18?
  2. Try the highest one: you found it in the warm-up.
  • Correct value but not fully simplified: 6 and 9 both divide by 3. Keep going, or divide by 6 in one step.
  • Correct value but not fully simplified: 4 and 6 both divide by 2. Keep going.

Divide top and bottom by 6: {frac:2/3}.

Which is bigger: {frac:3/5} or {frac:4/7}?

  • {frac:3/5}
  • {frac:4/7}
  • They are equal
  1. Write both over the same denominator. 5 × 7 = 35 works.
  2. {frac:3/5} = {frac:21/35}. What is {frac:4/7} over 35?

{frac:3/5} = {frac:21/35} and {frac:4/7} = {frac:20/35}. So {frac:3/5} is bigger.

2. Adding and subtracting

You can only add or subtract when the parts are the same size, which means the same denominator. If they are not, make a {term:common-denominator} first. The neatest one is the {term:lcm|lowest common multiple} of the denominators.

Key idea: change the pieces to the same size, then add the counts. The size (denominator) never gets added.

Worked example

Work out {frac:2/3} + {frac:1/4}.

  1. LCM of 3 and 4 is 12, so use twelfths.
  2. {frac:2/3} = {frac:8/12} and {frac:1/4} = {frac:3/12}.
  3. Add the numerators: 8 + 3 = 11. The denominator stays 12.
  4. {frac:2/3} + {frac:1/4} = {frac:11/12}.

Now you finish it

Work out {frac:3/5} + {frac:1/2}.

  1. LCM of 5 and 2 is 10, so use tenths.
  2. {frac:3/5} = {frac:6/10} and {frac:1/2} = {frac:5/10}.
  3. Add: 6 + 5 = ___. So the answer is {frac:11/10} = 1 {frac:1/10}.

An answer bigger than 1 is fine. {frac:11/10} is an {term:improper-fraction} and 1 {frac:1/10} is a {term:mixed-number}. They are the same number.

Why not just add tops and bottoms?

Because {frac:1/2} + {frac:1/2} must be 1 (two halves make a whole), and adding tops and bottoms gives {frac:2/4} = {frac:1/2}. Adding half to half cannot make half. The denominator tells you the size of the pieces, and putting two halves together does not make the pieces smaller.

Check your knowledge: add and subtract

Work out {frac:1/5} + {frac:3/5}.

  1. Same denominator already, so add the tops only.
  • You added the denominators as well. The pieces are still fifths.

1 + 3 = 4 fifths: {frac:4/5}.

Work out {frac:1/2} + {frac:1/3}.

  1. What is the LCM of 2 and 3?
  2. {frac:1/2} = {frac:3/6}. What is {frac:1/3} as sixths?
  • That adds the tops and the bottoms. Convert to sixths first.

{frac:3/6} + {frac:2/6} = {frac:5/6}.

Work out {frac:3/4} − {frac:1/6}.

  1. Twelfths. {frac:3/4} = {frac:9/12}.
  2. Subtract the tops only.
  • You subtracted the tops and the bottoms. As with adding, change both to twelfths first and keep 12 on the bottom.

{frac:9/12} − {frac:2/12} = {frac:7/12}.

Work out {frac:2/3} + {frac:3/4}. Either an improper fraction or a mixed number is fine.

  1. Twelfths again.
  2. {frac:8/12} + {frac:9/12}

{frac:8/12} + {frac:9/12} = {frac:17/12} = 1 {frac:5/12}.

3. Multiplying and dividing

These are easier than adding. To multiply, multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms. To divide, flip the second fraction (find its {term:reciprocal}) and multiply.

Key idea: "dividing by a fraction" asks "how many of these fit in?", which is the same as multiplying by the {term:reciprocal|reciprocal}. This is multiplication and division being each other's reverse, again.

Worked example: multiply

Work out {frac:2/3} × {frac:3/5}.

  1. Tops: 2 × 3 = 6. Bottoms: 3 × 5 = 15.
  2. {frac:6/15}, which simplifies (divide by 3) to {frac:2/5}.

Worked example: divide

Work out {frac:3/4} ÷ {frac:1/2}.

  1. Flip the second fraction: {frac:1/2} becomes {frac:2/1}.
  2. Multiply: {frac:3/4} × {frac:2/1} = {frac:6/4}.
  3. Simplify: {frac:6/4} = {frac:3/2} = 1 {frac:1/2}.

Sense check: how many halves fit into three quarters? One and a half. ✓

With a {term:mixed-number}, convert to an {term:improper-fraction} first: 1 {frac:1/2} = {frac:3/2}.

Check your knowledge: multiply and divide

Work out {frac:2/3} × {frac:3/5}. Give your answer in its simplest form.

  1. Multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms.
  2. Then simplify: you need the HCF.
  • Right value but not simplified. Divide top and bottom by 3.

{frac:6/15} = {frac:2/5}.

Work out {frac:3/4} ÷ {frac:1/2}. Give your answer in its simplest form, as a fraction or a mixed number (not a decimal).

  1. Flip the second fraction and multiply.
  • You multiplied. Dividing by a half means multiplying by 2, not by a half.
  • Right value but it can be simplified.

{frac:3/4} × {frac:2/1} = {frac:6/4} = {frac:3/2} = 1 {frac:1/2}.

Work out 1 {frac:1/2} × {frac:2/3}.

  1. 1 {frac:1/2} as an improper fraction is {frac:3/2}.

{frac:3/2} × {frac:2/3} = {frac:6/6} = 1.

Work out {frac:3/5} of 40.

  1. Find one fifth first: 40 ÷ 5.
  2. Then take three of them.
  • That is one fifth. You need three fifths.

40 ÷ 5 = 8, so {frac:3/5} of 40 = 3 × 8 = 24.

Flip it: run it backwards

Everything so far goes forwards. Now reverse it. This is where most of the real understanding comes from.

Forwards

{frac:3/5} of 40 = ?

Divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.

Backwards

{frac:3/5} of ? = 24

Divide by the top, multiply by the bottom.

Key idea: "{frac:3/5} of a number is 24" means three fifths equal 24. So one fifth is 24 ÷ 3 = 8, and the whole (five fifths) is 8 × 5 = 40. You use the two operations in the opposite order.

Another reversal: adding and subtracting. "What must I add to {frac:2/5} to make 1?" is the same as 1 − {frac:2/5}.

Check your knowledge: flip it

{frac:3/4} of a number is 18. What is the number?

  1. Three quarters is 18. What is one quarter?
  2. The whole number is four quarters.
  • You found three quarters of 18. The question runs it the other way: 18 is three quarters of the number.
  • That is one quarter. You need all four quarters.

18 ÷ 3 = 6 is one quarter; 6 × 4 = 24.

What fraction must you add to {frac:2/5} to make 1?

  1. This is the reverse of adding: subtract.
  2. 1 is the same as {frac:5/5}.

{frac:5/5} − {frac:2/5} = {frac:3/5}.

Write 0.375 as a fraction in its simplest form.

  1. 0.375 is 375 thousandths: {frac:375/1000}.
  2. Divide top and bottom by 125.
  • Right value, but it needs simplifying.

{frac:375/1000} = {frac:3/8}. Check the other way: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375.

Write 1 ÷ 8 as a fraction.

  1. Dividing a whole into 8 equal parts gives one eighth.

1 ÷ 8 = {frac:1/8}. Division and fractions are the same thing written two ways: {frac:3/4} = 3 ÷ 4.

Spot the mistake

Finding the error in someone else's working is one of the fastest ways to learn. For each one, decide what went wrong.

Mia says: {frac:1/3} + {frac:1/4} = {frac:2/7}. What went wrong?

  • She added the tops and the bottoms. The pieces need to be the same size first.
  • She made an arithmetic slip adding 3 and 4.
  • The question cannot be answered.
  1. Is {frac:2/7} sensible? {frac:1/3} alone is already about 0.33 and {frac:2/7} is about 0.29. A sum cannot be smaller than a part.

The correct answer is {frac:4/12} + {frac:3/12} = {frac:7/12}.

Sam says: {frac:1/2} ÷ {frac:1/4} = {frac:1/8}. What went wrong?

  • He should have added them
  • He multiplied instead of flipping the second fraction. {frac:1/2} ÷ {frac:1/4} = {frac:1/2} × {frac:4/1} = 2.
  • The fractions were the wrong way round
  1. How many quarters fit in a half? Think of pizza.

There are 2 quarters in a half, so the answer is 2.

In a bag of sweets, {frac:2/5} are red and {frac:1/4} are green. What fraction are neither red nor green?

  1. Add the two fractions first: {frac:2/5} + {frac:1/4} = ?
  2. Then take that away from 1 (the whole bag).
  • That is the fraction that are red or green. The question asks for the rest: take it from 1.

{frac:8/20} + {frac:5/20} = {frac:13/20}. So {frac:20/20} − {frac:13/20} = {frac:7/20}.

Exam style

Use these like a real paper: no calculator, show your working. The marks tell you how much to write.

(a) [2 marks] Jo has £60. She spends {frac:1/3} of it on a game and {frac:1/4} of it on lunch. How much does she spend altogether? (Answer in pounds, without the £ sign if you like.)

  1. Find {frac:1/3} of 60 and {frac:1/4} of 60 separately.
  2. Add them.
  • That is only the game. Add the lunch too.
  • That is only lunch. Add the game too.

60 ÷ 3 = 20 and 60 ÷ 4 = 15. 20 + 15 = £35.

(b) [2 marks] What fraction of Jo's money is left?

  1. She spent {frac:1/3} + {frac:1/4} = {frac:7/12} of it.
  2. What is left is 1 − that.
  • £25 is the amount left, which is right as money, but the question asks for a fraction of her money.

{frac:12/12} − {frac:7/12} = {frac:5/12}. Check: £25 left, and {frac:25/60} = {frac:5/12} ✓

Why it works

  • Common denominators are about piece size. You can only count pieces that are the same size, the way you cannot say "3 apples + 2 oranges = 5 apples".
  • Flipping to divide. Dividing by {frac:1/2} asks how many halves fit. Each whole holds two halves, so you multiply by 2.
  • Equivalent fractions are the same number. Multiplying top and bottom by 4 is multiplying by {frac:4/4} = 1, which changes nothing.

Common traps

  • Adding the denominators.
  • Adding the same number to the top and bottom to "make equivalent".
  • Multiplying by the second fraction when you should flip it for a division.
  • Forgetting to convert a mixed number before multiplying or dividing.
  • Leaving an answer unsimplified.

Going further

You know {frac:1/2} + {frac:1/3} = {frac:5/6}. Without starting a fresh calculation, what is {frac:5/6} − {frac:1/2}?

  1. Taking away what you added should give you back what you started with.

{frac:5/6} − {frac:1/2} = {frac:1/3}. Subtracting undoes adding: that is the "flip" in action. You can check it the other way: {frac:1/3} + {frac:1/2} = {frac:5/6}.
